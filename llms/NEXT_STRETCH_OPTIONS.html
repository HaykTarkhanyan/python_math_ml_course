Black box, or open it? The next stretch of the LLM part

Notes for a discussion, 2026-11-12. Nothing in the repo changes until you decide. Session counts below are "sessions from now", after the four already delivered (LLM-1, practical 02, LLM-2 Decoding, B0). Job numbers are companies in the course's own snapshot (_knowledge/ml_job_market/, 2026-10-28).

In short
  • None of the nine student projects needs attention or the transformer internals to be built. They need tool calls, embeddings, retrieval, evals and agents. Two need LoRA, one benefits from quantization.
  • The stakes are smaller than they look. Finishing the transformer run first (LLM-3 to LLM-6, four decks) delays tool calling by 4 sessions and RAG by 3: one to two weeks at 2 to 3 sessions a week, not months.
  • My pick: build now (option B). Tool calling next, projects kick off right after it, LLM-3 Embeddings just before search, and the transformer run once projects are under way, so students build in the background while the lectures go deep.
  • B and a 2-to-1 mix (C') agree on the next five sessions, so today you only decide "build now or open the box now". The rhythm after that can wait.
  • The honest cost of B: the attention thread from [54] "The road to attention" stays cold until session 8, and every applied session needs a notebook built before it. The theory decks are ready today.

2. What students need from inside the box

"Black box" does not have to mean "knows nothing". To build well, a student needs a working picture of the box, not its wiring. Most of that picture is taught already.

3. Four orderings, for and against

A. Open the box first: LLM-3 to LLM-6, then build

Embeddings, attention, the block, transformers in the world (90 + 68 + 46 + 32 pages), then the applied block. Pretraining, post-training and the rest come later, between applied sessions.

For

  • Closes the arc from [54]: RNNs, then the road to attention, then the transformer. The natural climax of the neural-network part.
  • Zero build work: the decks are built, and LLM-3 to LLM-6 were reviewed by students three times.
  • Plays to the course's strength: these students have the math, which is what sets them apart from a bootcamp graduate.
  • Attention is the classic interview question for ML roles.
  • The delay is bounded: four sessions, then building.

Against

  • Tool calling at session 5, RAG at 8, agents at 10. Projects start one and a half to two weeks later.
  • The API momentum from B0 is lost, and B0 ended on "you can call any model now".
  • The theory is unmotivated by use: students meet attention before they have hit a problem it explains.
  • Four decks in a row is a long stretch without anything students can run themselves.

Right pick if the class is more excited by how it works than by building, or the projects can wait two weeks.

B. Build now, theory when it is needed (my pick)

Tool calling, prompting, LLM-3 (the one theory deck the applied line needs), search, RAG, evals, agents. Then the transformer run and the rest of the theory, while the projects run.

For

  • Tool calling next, which needs only B0. Projects kick off after it: the MCP-server and SQL-agent projects can start at once.
  • RAG at session 5 instead of 8, agents at 7 instead of 10 or 12.
  • Projects then run in the background while the lectures go deep. Theory first runs things in series: projects squeezed at the end.
  • Theory lands better after use: "why did caching cut my bill" is the way into the KV cache, "why did my RAG invent a citation" into hallucination (the plan page's "used now, explained later" table).
  • Matches your #109 framing ("magic for now") and the job table's top applied skills.
  • Chip Huyen's AI Engineering gives the model one chapter of ten; fast.ai teaches top-down (section 7).

Against

  • The attention thread from [54] stays cold until session 8 (session 6 with C').
  • Every applied session needs a notebook built before it: about one working session each, plus your review. The first (tool calling) does not exist yet. If the next class is very soon, swap LLM-3 to the front (it is ready and needed anyway).
  • The course's math identity takes a back seat for two to three weeks.
  • Risk that the theory gets squeezed later. Pin LLM-4 to LLM-6 right after agents now, so it cannot slide.
  • Applied material goes stale fast (SDKs, MCP), so it needs a re-check before every delivery.
  • The prompting deck (LLM-10) refers to LLM-7 and LLM-9 as already taught; a few callbacks become forward pointers.

Right pick if you want projects started soon and the class is itching to build.

C. Alternate one to one (the two-streams plan as drawn)

One theory deck, one applied session, repeat. LLM-3 first.

For

  • Neither thread goes cold; attention at session 3.
  • The next session (LLM-3) is ready, which gives time to build the first notebook.
  • Already planned and reviewed (DECISIONS #105, the plan page).

Against

  • Slowest to the applied toolkit: RAG at 8, agents at 12.
  • Every session switches mode, and the LLM-3 to LLM-6 run is broken up by workshops.
  • Needs a free practical slot every week (the plan page's open Q2).

C'. Two applied, one theory

Tool calling, prompting, LLM-3, search, RAG, LLM-4, evals, agents, LLM-5, and so on.

For

  • Almost as fast as B to the toolkit (RAG at 5, agents at 8), and attention arrives at session 6, not 8.
  • The theory never stops.
  • Identical to B for the next five sessions, so it can be chosen later.

Against

  • The transformer run is spread thin (one deck every third session), which costs its "one story" feel.
  • The same build load as B.

6. Ideas that fit any ordering
  1. Kick off projects at the first applied session. Teams and topics picked then; every applied session adds one capability that becomes a project milestone (tools, then retrieval, then an eval set, then an agent). The notebooks double as templates students fork.
  2. Build, break, fix instead of measuring. If the job-postings practical felt like a measurement study, shape each applied session as: about 25 minutes of slides, about 50 minutes building something that works, about 15 minutes breaking it on purpose (a prompt injection, a wrong tool argument, the right chunk lost in the middle) and fixing it. One small number per session, not tables.
  3. A "peek inside" frame at the end of each applied session. One frame: what just happened inside the box, and which lecture opens it ("the cached part of your prompt is a reused KV cache: LLM-14"). It keeps the theory wanted. The plan page already lists these links.
  4. Optional: "How an LLM is made", one lecture, no math. Pretraining, post-training and inference as one picture, built from the existing roadmap figures and a few LLM-7 / LLM-9 frames. It gives the black box a shape, like Huyen's single model chapter (training data, modeling, post-training, sampling). Costs a new compact deck.
  5. Reuse the shelved job-postings work. The 148 staff.am postings make a ready tool for session 1 ("search the job ads"), and the strict-schema-vs-asking result (0 broken replies with a schema, up to 56 of 148 without) is a five-minute aside.
  6. Pin the theory's slot now. Whatever goes first, write down when LLM-4 to LLM-6 happen, so the applied block cannot quietly eat them.

7. What other curricula do
CurriculumOrder
fast.ai, Practical Deep LearningTop-down on purpose: start with complete working solutions to real problems, then work down to the foundations. Lesson 1 recommends David Perkins' Making Learning Whole ("play the whole game"). A third-party review notes it suits some learners and frustrates others.
Chip Huyen, AI Engineering (O'Reilly)Ten chapters. One, "Understanding Foundation Models" (training data, modeling, post-training, sampling), covers the model; then evaluation (two chapters), prompt engineering, RAG and agents, finetuning, dataset engineering, inference optimization, architecture and user feedback. Evaluation comes before prompting.
Maxime Labonne, llm-courseTwo separate tracks, "The LLM Scientist" and "The LLM Engineer" (read for the plan page, 2026-10-28). The engineer track treats the model as given.
Bottom-up coursesKarpathy's "Zero to Hero" builds from a tiny autograd engine up to a GPT: understanding first, applications barely at all. It fits researchers more than people shipping LLM apps. (From memory, not re-checked today.)

This course has done both already: the ML part was bottom-up (regression to RNNs), and the LLM part switched to "magic for now" at B0. Option B continues that switch; option A returns to bottom-up for four decks.
