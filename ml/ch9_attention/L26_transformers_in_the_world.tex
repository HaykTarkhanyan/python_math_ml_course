\begin{frame}{The same block, three different jobs}
  \vskip 8pt
  You can now build a Transformer block. But the block alone does not tell you whether you are
  building \textbf{BERT}, \textbf{GPT} or a translation system.

  \vskip 10pt
  The difference is not the block. It is one decision:

  \vskip 10pt
  \begin{center}
    {\large\color{popblue}\textbf{who is allowed to look at whom?}}
  \end{center}

  \vskip 12pt
  \keybox{Change the attention mask and the same architecture becomes a text classifier, a
  chatbot, or a translator. That is this lecture's first section - and then we ask why this
  design beat everything else, and where it is still weak.}
\end{frame}

% ============================================================ SECTION 1
\section{The family}
\sectiontransition{The family}{One block, three ways of wiring it.}

\begin{frame}{The original: an encoder and a decoder}
  \vskip 4pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.40\textwidth}
      \vskip -2pt
      % Figure 1 sits at roughly x 206-432pt, y 381-728pt on the 612x792pt page.
      % trim is left/bottom/right/top, so cut everything outside that box.
      \includegraphics[height=0.60\textheight, keepaspectratio,
                       trim=195 402 170 55, clip]{vaswani_fig1_page.pdf}\\
      \paper{Vaswani et al. (2017), Figure 1}
    \column{0.57\textwidth}
      \vskip 8pt
      {\small The 2017 paper was about \textbf{translation}, so it has two stacks:

      \vskip 6pt
      \textbf{Encoder} -- reads the source sentence. Every token may see every other token.

      \vskip 6pt
      \textbf{Decoder} -- writes the target sentence, one token at a time, so it is
      \textbf{causally masked} (L25).

      \vskip 8pt
      They are joined by \textbf{cross-attention}: the decoder supplies the queries, and the
      \emph{encoder's} output supplies the keys and values.}
  \end{columns}

  \vskip 4pt
  \keybox{Self-attention asks ``what in \emph{this} sentence matters to me?'' Cross-attention
  asks ``what in \emph{that other} sequence matters to me?'' Identical mechanism; the keys and
  values just come from somewhere else.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Cross-attention, with actual numbers}
  \vskip 2pt
  The decoder is writing an English sentence and has produced ``the''. The encoder has already
  read the French source. Where should ``the'' look?

  \vskip 4pt
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.84\linewidth]{l26_cross_attention.pdf}
  \end{center}

  \vskip -2pt
  {\footnotesize One head, $d_k = 4$. Scores $+0.339$, $-0.273$, $+0.304$ become weights
  \textbf{0.399}, \textbf{0.216}, \textbf{0.385} after softmax -- and they sum to 1, exactly as
  in L24.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What actually changed}
  \vskip 10pt
  Compare with the self-attention you computed in L24. The formula is character-for-character
  the same:
  \vskip 4pt
  \begin{center}
    $\text{Attention}(Q, K, V) = \text{softmax}\!\left(\dfrac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right) V$
  \end{center}

  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{center}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{lll}
    \toprule
    & \textbf{Self-attention} & \textbf{Cross-attention} \\
    \midrule
    $Q$ comes from & this sequence & \textbf{the decoder} \\
    $K, V$ come from & this sequence & \textbf{the encoder} \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \vskip 10pt
  \keybox{That is the entire difference: \textbf{two of the three inputs come from somewhere
  else}. No new mechanism, no new maths -- which is why the original paper could reuse the same
  block for both.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Three architectures}
  \vskip 4pt
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.90\linewidth]{l26_three_arch.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vskip -2pt
  \takebox{Nobody changed the block. \textbf{Encoder-only} sees in both directions and is good
  at understanding. \textbf{Decoder-only} sees only backwards, which makes it able to generate.
  \textbf{Encoder-decoder} keeps both and joins them with cross-attention.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why decoder-only won the last five years}
  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{One objective, unlimited data.} Predicting the next token needs no labels, so
      any text on the internet is training data.
    \item \textbf{It subsumes the others.} ``Translate this: \ldots'' is just a prompt. Tasks
      that needed a separate architecture became a formatting question.
    \item \textbf{It is the simplest thing to scale.} One stack, one loss, no encoder to keep
      in sync.
  \end{itemize}

  \vskip 10pt
  \watchbox{This is a statement about the last few years, not a theorem. Encoder-only models
  are still the right tool when you want an \emph{embedding} rather than a continuation -- which
  is exactly what the retrieval chapter used them for.}
\end{frame}

% ============================================================ SECTION 2
\section{How it learns}
\sectiontransition{How it learns}{One objective, and the trick that turns it into a chatbot.}

\begin{frame}{The objective you have already seen}
  \vskip 8pt
  In \textbf{L21} we factorised a language model:
  \vskip 4pt
  \begin{center}
    $P(y_1, \ldots, y_T) = \prod_{t=1}^{T} P(y_t \mid y_{<t})$
  \end{center}

  \vskip 8pt
  That is all GPT is trained to do. Show it a prefix, ask for the next token, compare with the
  truth, backpropagate. The loss is plain cross-entropy.

  \vskip 10pt
  \keybox{Nothing about the objective is new. What changed is that the \textbf{causal mask}
  (L25) lets every position in a sequence be a training example in the same forward pass, and
  that the architecture is parallel enough to make internet-scale data affordable.}

  \vskip 6pt
  {\small So the modern language model is an old idea with a new engine.}
\end{frame}

\bbbcap{snape_prediction.png}{Next-token prediction, in practice: the model outputs a
distribution over the whole vocabulary, and one continuation is far more likely than the rest.}

\begin{frame}{From a vector to a word}
  \vskip 4pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.46\textwidth}
      \vskip -4pt
      \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{unembedding_matrix.png}\\ \bbb
    \column{0.50\textwidth}
      \vskip 10pt
      {\small After the last block, the final token's vector is multiplied by an
      \textbf{unembedding matrix} -- one row per vocabulary entry, so roughly 50,000 numbers
      come out.

      \vskip 8pt
      Those are \textbf{logits}. A softmax turns them into a probability distribution, and you
      sample from it.

      \vskip 8pt
      That is the entire output stage.}
  \end{columns}

  \vskip 4pt
  \keybox{The model does not choose a word. It produces a \emph{distribution} over every word,
  and the choosing is a separate decision -- which is where temperature and the other decoding
  strategies live, later in this chapter.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Autocomplete is not a chatbot}
  \vskip 8pt
  A model trained this way continues text. Given ``the capital of Armenia is'', it says
  ``Yerevan''. Given ``how do I reset my password?'', it might continue with
  \emph{another question}, because that is what web pages look like.

  \vskip 10pt
  The fix that started the current era is embarrassingly simple: put the conversation in a
  format where the helpful continuation \emph{is} the likely one.

  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=lightbg, colframe=gray, boxrule=0.5pt, arc=2pt, width=0.86\textwidth]
    \ttfamily\scriptsize
    You are a helpful assistant.\\
    User: how do I reset my password?\\
    Assistant:
  \end{tcolorbox}
  \end{center}

  \vskip 4pt
  \takebox{Now the most likely continuation of the \emph{text} is the answer you wanted. The
  system prompt is not a new capability - it is a \textbf{framing} that makes autocomplete
  behave like a conversation. Fine-tuning, and then training on human preferences, make it
  reliable - that is the \texttt{llm\_training} track.}
\end{frame}

% ============================================================ SECTION 3
\section{Why it won, and what it costs}
\sectiontransition{Why it won, and what it costs}{Every architecture buys something with
something.}

\begin{frame}{Why transformers won}
  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item[\textbf{1.}] \textbf{It parallelises.} An RNN had to walk the sequence in order.
      Attention is three big matmuls over the whole sequence at once (L24), which is exactly the
      workload a GPU wants.
    \item[\textbf{2.}] \textbf{Because it parallelises, it scales.} You can afford more data and
      more parameters.
    \item[\textbf{3.}] \textbf{Because it scales, it kept getting better} -- and past certain
      sizes, qualitatively so. The clearest example is \textbf{in-context learning}: give a
      model a few examples in the prompt and it follows the pattern, \emph{with no weight
      updates at all}. Small models essentially cannot do this; past a certain scale they can.
  \end{enumerate}

  \vskip 12pt
  \keybox{Notice the shape of that argument: the win is not that attention is a cleverer model
  of language. It is that attention is a \textbf{better fit for the hardware}, and everything
  else followed from being able to train bigger. Architecture that suits the machine beats
  architecture that suits your intuition.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The bill: attention is quadratic}
  \vskip 4pt
  Every token attends to every token, so the score matrix is $n \times n$. Double the context
  and you quadruple that cost.

  \vskip 4pt
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.60\linewidth]{l26_quadratic.pdf}
  \end{center}

  \vskip -2pt
  {\small Computed for $d_{\text{model}} = 4096$ with a $4\times$ feed-forward.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Read that graph carefully}
  \vskip 8pt
  The usual story is ``attention is the bottleneck''. The arithmetic says: \textbf{not until
  the sequence gets long}.

  \vskip 8pt
  You can work out where it flips in two lines. Attention costs about $n^2 d$; the
  feed-forward costs about $n d^2 f$, where $f$ is the widening factor (usually 4). Set them
  equal:
  \vskip 2pt
  \begin{center}
    $n^2 d = n d^2 f \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{n = d \cdot f}$
  \end{center}

  \vskip 6pt
  So for $d_{\text{model}} = 4096$ and $f = 4$, the crossover is at
  $4096 \times 4 = \mathbf{16{,}384}$ tokens. Halve the model and you halve the crossover.

  \vskip 12pt
  \watchbox{So for a 2,048-token context, optimising attention is optimising the smaller half.
  ``Quadratic'' describes how the cost \emph{grows}, not where the cost \emph{is} at every
  size -- worth knowing before you optimise the wrong thing. FlashAttention and the sparse
  variants attack the $n^2$ term, later in this chapter.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The honest limit: almost no inductive bias}
  \vskip 6pt
  Every architecture in this course encoded an assumption about its data:

  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{center}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{lll}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Model} & \textbf{Built-in assumption} & \textbf{Consequence} \\
    \midrule
    Decision tree (ch4) & axis-aligned splits matter & works on tiny tabular data \\
    CNN (ch6) & nearby pixels are related & learns vision from thousands of images \\
    RNN (ch7) & order matters, recent matters more & handles sequences by construction \\
    \textbf{Transformer} & \textbf{almost nothing} & \textbf{needs enormous data} \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \vskip 8pt
  A Transformer does not know that neighbouring words are related, or even that words have an
  order -- we had to \emph{bolt position on} in L25.

  \vskip 6pt
  \trapbox{That weakness is the same fact as its strength. Assuming nothing means it can learn
  any structure, and \textbf{must} learn all of it from data. This is why a ViT loses to a CNN
  on a small image dataset and wins on a huge one, and why transformers arrived only once the
  data and the compute did.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Same block, everywhere}
  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{center}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{lll}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Domain} & \textbf{What became a token} & \textbf{Chapter} \\
    \midrule
    Text & subword pieces & this one, and \texttt{llm\_training} \\
    Images (ViT) & 16$\times$16 patches & ch12, vision-language models \\
    Audio & spectrogram frames or codec codes & ch13 \\
    Proteins & amino acids & AlphaFold \\
    Actions & robot commands & ch15, vision-language-action \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \vskip 10pt
  \takebox{Nobody redesigned the block for any of these. They redesigned what counts as a
  \textbf{token}. That is the practical legacy of the architecture: if you can serialise your
  problem into a sequence of vectors, you can throw a Transformer at it.}
\end{frame}

% ============================================================ RECAP
\begin{frame}{Recap}
  \vskip 4pt
  \small
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{One block, three wirings.} Encoder-only (BERT) understands, decoder-only (GPT)
      generates, encoder-decoder (T5) translates. The difference is the mask, not the block.
    \item \textbf{Cross-attention} is ordinary attention with the keys and values taken from
      another sequence.
    \item \textbf{Training is L21's objective} -- predict the next token -- made affordable by
      the causal mask and by hardware-friendly parallelism.
    \item \textbf{The output is a distribution}, not a word. A system prompt turns autocomplete
      into a chatbot by making the helpful continuation the likely one.
    \item \textbf{It won because it fits the GPU}, not because it is a better theory of language.
    \item \textbf{It is quadratic in sequence length}, but at $d_{\text{model}}=4096$ that only
      dominates past 16,384 tokens.
    \item \textbf{It assumes almost nothing}, so it can learn anything and needs vast data to
      do it.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}
