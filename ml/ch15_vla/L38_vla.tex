% COLD OPEN
\begin{frame}{You taught it to see. Now make it do something.}
  \small
  In L33 a model looked at a kitchen photo and described it. In L34 it drew one. Both
  outputs were the same kind of thing: \textbf{tokens a human reads}.
  \vskip 6pt
  Point the same camera at the same kitchen and say \emph{"put the bowl in the sink"}. Now
  the output has to be a number that a motor can execute, right now, and if it is wrong the
  bowl breaks.
  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\small
      A \textbf{Vision-Language-Action model} (\textbf{VLA}) is a vision-language model
      fine-tuned so that its output is not text but \textbf{robot actions}.}}
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\small
      That one change breaks almost everything you have relied on: no ground truth to
      condition on, no internet-scale corpus, and no way to run the test set twice.}}
  \end{columns}
  \vskip 8pt
  \footnotesize
  \centering
  This lecture is deliberately two-sided. The first half is why this is exciting. The
  second half is what the measurements actually say.
\end{frame}

\section{What a VLA actually is}

\begin{frame}[plain]
  \vfill
  \begin{center}
    {\Large\color{popblue}\textbf{What a VLA actually is}}\\[0.9em]
    \begin{minipage}{0.82\textwidth}\centering\small
      One network replaces an entire hand-built robotics stack. That is the whole claim.
    \end{minipage}
  \end{center}
  \vfill
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Five robotics words, then we can talk normally}
  \small
  You have no robotics background and you do not need one. You need these five.
  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{center}
    \footnotesize
    \begin{tabular}{p{3.0cm}p{8.6cm}}
      \toprule
      \textbf{End effector} & The business end of the arm: a gripper, a suction cup, a hand.
        Policies usually output where the \emph{end effector} should go, not each joint
        angle, because that transfers between arms of different shape. \\
      \textbf{Degrees of freedom} & How many numbers you independently control. Arm plus
        gripper is \textbf{7}: three positions, three rotations, one open/close. A humanoid
        with two five-fingered hands is 40+. \\
      \textbf{Proprioception} & The robot's sense of its own body: current joint angles,
        gripper width. Cheap, exact, fed in alongside the images. \\
      \textbf{Teleoperation} & A human driving the robot live, through a controller or a
        small leader arm the real arm mirrors. This is how essentially all training data is
        made. \\
      \textbf{Embodiment} & A specific robot body. Different cameras, different action
        dimensions, different reach. "Cross-embodiment" means one model driving several. \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The definition, and what it deletes}
  \small
  A VLA is built on \textbf{backbones pretrained on large-scale vision-language data}, then
  fine-tuned to emit control commands. The pretraining is load-bearing: a transformer
  trained from scratch on robot demonstrations is a visuomotor policy, not a VLA.
  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \footnotesize
    \textbf{The classical stack}, four modules, hand-designed interfaces:
    \vskip 4pt
    perception $\rightarrow$ object poses \\
    symbolic planner $\rightarrow$ subgoals \\
    motion planner $\rightarrow$ trajectory \\
    controller $\rightarrow$ torques
    \vskip 6pt
    Every stage has its own representation and its own failure mode.
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      \textbf{A VLA deletes all four.} One network trained end to end on (observation,
      instruction, action) triples.
      \vskip 4pt
      Everything else in this lecture, good and bad, is a consequence of that one
      decision.}}
  \end{columns}
  \vskip 8pt
  \footnotesize
  \centering
  The bet: internet-scale visual and linguistic knowledge is what robots were always
  missing, and once a model has it, \emph{one} set of weights can drive many robots at many
  tasks.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The loop, once around}
  \small
  \vskip -2pt
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.96\textwidth]{vla_loop.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vskip -4pt
  \footnotesize
  Two things a language modeller would not expect. The actions are usually \textbf{deltas}
  ("move 2 cm left"), not absolute positions, so the same action means the same thing
  anywhere in the workspace. And the VLA never closes the torque loop: a conventional
  controller underneath still does that at hundreds of Hz.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{There is no teacher forcing on a robot}
  \small
  In language modelling, a mistake at step $t$ does not corrupt step $t+1$ during training:
  you feed the true token. On a robot, an error at step $t$ moves the arm somewhere the
  demonstration never visited, and step $t+1$ is now out of distribution.
  \vskip 6pt
  \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.97\linewidth}{\footnotesize
    \textbf{Compounding error.} Behaviour cloning drifts, and the drift feeds itself. This
    has no analogue in ordinary supervised learning, where a wrong prediction on row 7 does
    not change row 8.}}
  \vskip 8pt
  \textbf{Action chunking} is the fix, from ACT (Zhao et al., 2023). Predict the next $H$
  actions in one shot and execute them open-loop before asking again. Typical $H$ is 8 to
  50; pi-0 uses $H = 50$.
  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      Error now accumulates over $T/H$ decisions instead of $T$. It also lets the model
      commit to one intent instead of averaging over a hesitating human's several.}}
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!12}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      \textbf{The cost is reactivity.} During a chunk the robot is blind. And there is a
      visible jerk every time a new chunk starts.}}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Three ways to make a language model emit a number}
  \small
  Discretize, or model the continuous distribution directly? The same question as ch10.
  \vskip 4pt
  \begin{center}
    \footnotesize
    \begin{tabular}{p{2.9cm}p{4.4cm}p{4.4cm}}
      \toprule
      & \textbf{How} & \textbf{Why it wins or loses} \\
      \midrule
      \textbf{Discrete bins} \newline RT-2, OpenVLA
        & Cut each action dimension into 256 bins, treat each bin as a token, autoregress.
          OpenVLA overwrites the 256 least-used Llama-2 tokens.
        & Zero architectural change: the action head \emph{is} the language head. But 7
          dimensions $\times$ 50 steps is 350 tokens, far too slow. \\
      \addlinespace
      \textbf{Compressed tokens} \newline FAST
        & Discrete cosine transform along the chunk, drop the small high-frequency terms,
          then byte-pair encode.
        & Robot trajectories are smooth signals, so treat them like audio. Roughly 10x
          compression, 5x faster training. \\
      \addlinespace
      \textbf{Continuous head} \newline Octo, pi-0, GR00T N1
        & A separate head trained as conditional diffusion or flow matching, conditioned on
          the backbone's features.
        & Demonstrations are genuinely multimodal: two humans reach around a mug two ways,
          and averaging goes \emph{through} the mug. \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
  \end{center}
  \vskip 2pt
  \footnotesize
  \centering
  FAST is Frequency-space Action Sequence Tokenization (Pertsch et al., 2025). The trick is
  borrowed straight from JPEG.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Too slow to touch anything}
  \small
  \vskip -4pt
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.80\textwidth]{control_rates.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vskip -6pt
  \footnotesize
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\scriptsize
      \textbf{Dual-system designs.} A slow semantic model and a fast reactive one. Figure's
      Helix runs a 7B model at 7-9 Hz that compresses the scene into a \emph{single latent
      vector}, driving an 80M policy at 200 Hz. GR00T N1 has the same shape.}}
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\scriptsize
      \textbf{Real-time chunking} (Black et al., 2025). Compute the next chunk while
      executing the current one, freeze the actions already committed, and \emph{inpaint}
      the rest. Yes, that is ch10's inpainting, applied to a trajectory.}}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The chart that does not look like the language one}
  \small

    \includegraphics[width=0.79\textwidth]{model_timeline.pdf}
  \end{center}

  \begin{columns}[T]

    \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\scriptsize
      \textbf{Two hard caps.} The model has to run \emph{on the robot} at control rate,

    \column{0.48\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!12}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\scriptsize
      RT-2 (2023) was 55B and ran at 3 Hz, so it was never deployable. OpenVLA (Kim et al.,

  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Demo, company-reported, or measured benchmark?}
  \small
  \vskip -6pt
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.90\textwidth]{gemini2_measured.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vskip -2pt
  \footnotesize
  \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!12}{\parbox{0.97\linewidth}{\scriptsize
    Robotics publishes an unusual amount of marketing, so separate three things every time.
    A \textbf{demo} is a video with no number (a robot folding laundry for eighteen hours
    tells you the system does not fall over, not its success rate). A
    \textbf{company-reported} number is real but unreproducible: self-chosen tasks,
    unpublished protocol. A \textbf{measured benchmark} is something a second lab can run.
    The chart above is the middle kind, and it is more honest than most: screwing a bulb
    \emph{in} is 36\%.}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{"Generalist policy" is three claims, not one}
  \small
  They travel under one label and they are not equally supported. Keep them apart.
  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.32\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      \textbf{1. Multi-task.} \\ One set of weights, many tasks in one environment, selected
      by the instruction.
      \vskip 4pt
      \textbf{Solved} since RT-1 in 2022: 700+ instructions, one model.}}
    \column{0.32\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!12}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      \textbf{2. Cross-embodiment.} \\ One set of weights drives \emph{different robots}.
      \vskip 4pt
      \textbf{Mostly works.} Open X-Embodiment pooled 60 datasets and got positive
      transfer. Conditions apply - see later.}}
    \column{0.32\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      \textbf{3. Open-world.} \\ New objects, new scenes, new instructions, zero-shot.
      \vskip 4pt
      \textbf{The research frontier}, and where the marketing lives. This is the claim that
      justifies the whole enterprise.}}
  \end{columns}
  \vskip 10pt
  \footnotesize
  \centering
  The 2026 review of 164 VLA submissions to one conference is blunt: open models match the
  frontier \emph{in simulation} and "dramatically lag" it in zero-shot open-world behaviour.
\end{frame}

\section{There is no internet of robot data}

\begin{frame}[plain]
  \vfill
  \begin{center}
    {\Large\color{popblue}\textbf{There is no internet of robot data}}\\[0.9em]
    \begin{minipage}{0.82\textwidth}\centering\small
      In vision and language, architecture was the bottleneck and data was free. Here it is
      exactly reversed.
    \end{minipage}
  \end{center}
  \vfill
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The gap}
  \small
  \vskip -4pt
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.80\textwidth]{data_gap.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vskip -4pt
  \footnotesize
  \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.97\linewidth}{\scriptsize
    \textbf{And it cannot be closed by scraping, because the data does not exist to be
    scraped.} It has to be generated by a human moving a robot in real time: roughly one
    human-hour per robot-hour. That is a structural fact, not an engineering backlog. Every
    strange decision in this field is a response to it.}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Four ways to manufacture what you cannot scrape}
  \small
  \begin{center}
    \footnotesize
    \begin{tabular}{p{2.6cm}p{9.0cm}}
      \toprule
      \textbf{Teleoperation} & The gold standard and the expensive one. An ALOHA bimanual
        rig is around \$20k; GELLO does the same job with a 3D-printed exoskeleton for about
        \$300. DROID took 50 collectors at 13 institutions 12 months to reach 350 hours. \\
      \addlinespace
      \textbf{Human video} & The largest available signal, with no action labels and a hand
        that is not a gripper. The 2026 answer is retargeting wrist and hand poses into
        pseudo-actions. EgoScale pretrained on 20,854 hours of egocentric video and reports
        a \emph{log-linear scaling law} between human-action loss and data scale. One paper,
        one lab, but it is the first credible route to a corpus that scales. \\
      \addlinespace
      \textbf{Simulation} & Nearly free per sample, with resets and ground truth. Rendering
        is close to solved; friction, deformation and contact are not, and contact is the
        hard part. \\
      \addlinespace
      \textbf{Data engines} & Systems that \emph{manufacture} demonstrations. MimicGen turns
        200 human demonstrations into 50,000 by replaying them onto new object poses. Huge
        multiplier, but the diversity is combinatorial, not semantic: same skill, new
        places, no new skills. \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Sim-to-real: what actually transfers}
  \small
  Folklore until recently. Jin et al.\ (2026) ran \textbf{over 10,000 real-world trials} to
  ablate it, which is roughly 400 times more than a typical paper affords.
  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.53\textwidth}
    \footnotesize
    \begin{itemize}\itemsep 3pt
      \item \textbf{Spatial randomization beats appearance randomization.} Randomizing
        camera pose and table height helped more than randomizing backgrounds and lighting.
      \item Randomizing \textbf{per frame} beats per episode.
      \item Rendering fidelity helps, then plateaus.
      \item \textbf{Reinforcement learning (ch11) is the big lever}: 5.6\% $\rightarrow$
        33.4\% real-world success, and 42.8\% combined with randomization.
    \end{itemize}
    \column{0.47\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!12}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      Counter-intuitive, and useful: everyone reaches for \emph{visual} domain
      randomization first because it is easy, and it is the less valuable axis.}}
    \vskip 6pt
    \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      \textbf{Read 42.8\% again.} That is the \emph{good} number, after the best recipe in
      the largest study of its kind. Sim-to-real for manipulation remains bad in absolute
      terms.}}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Cross-embodiment transfer is conditional, not general}
  \small
  Pooling robots usually helps. Open X-Embodiment (2023) merged 60 datasets from 34 labs
  into 1M+ trajectories across 22 embodiments, and the models trained on it beat their own
  single-robot baselines. Gemini Robotics 1.5's Motion Transfer is a whole mechanism built
  to move a skill from one body to another.
  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      \textbf{But negative transfer is reported.} Embodiments with few close relatives in
      the pool can do \emph{worse} than training alone, when a lot of low-quality data meets
      high body diversity.
      \vskip 3pt
      Reported in the 2026 cross-embodiment literature; I could not trace the specific
      robots to a paper I read, so treat the direction as the claim, not a number.}}
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      \textbf{The honest framing:} transfer is a similarity-weighted average, not a free
      lunch. Pooling helps bodies with close relatives in the pool and can hurt the ones
      without.
      \vskip 3pt
      You have seen this before: multi-task learning helps until task conflict dominates.}}
  \end{columns}
  \vskip 8pt
  \footnotesize
  \centering
  So "one brain, any robot" is really "one brain, any robot sufficiently similar to
  something in the training set". That is a much weaker claim, and it changes the economics.
\end{frame}

\section{The language channel is largely dead}

\begin{frame}[plain]
  \vfill
  \begin{center}
    {\Large\color{popblue}\textbf{The language channel is largely dead}}\\[0.9em]
    \begin{minipage}{0.82\textwidth}\centering\small
      This is the sequel to L33's hallucination frame, and it is worse than it was there.
    \end{minipage}
  \end{center}
  \vfill
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Predict first}
  \small
  A team reports that their VLA's success rate is \textbf{almost unchanged} when you
  rephrase the instruction. "Open the drawer", "pull the drawer open", "could you open that
  drawer" all score the same.
  \vskip 12pt
  \begin{center}
    \textbf{Good news or bad news?}
  \end{center}
  \vskip 12pt
  \pause
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\small
      \textbf{It reads like robustness.} The model understood the request rather than
      memorising a string. This is how such a result is usually reported.}}
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\small
      \textbf{It is evidence the channel is dead.} A model that is insensitive to
      \emph{every} change in wording may not be reading the wording at all.}}
  \end{columns}
  \vskip 8pt
  \footnotesize
  \centering
  The test that separates the two: change the wording so the \emph{meaning} changes, and
  check that the behaviour changes too.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The experiment that settles it}
  \small
  \textbf{LIBERO-PRO} (Zhou et al., 2026) takes the standard benchmark and perturbs four
  things: the object, its starting position, the instruction wording, and the environment.
  \vskip 6pt
  \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.97\linewidth}{\small
    Models that score \textbf{over 90\%} on standard LIBERO score \textbf{0.0\%} under the
    generalized setting. Not degraded. Zero.}}
  \vskip 8pt
  The diagnostic is better than the headline. Replace the instruction with a
  \textbf{meaningless string} - the paper's examples are \texttt{fdsgfdsgsd} and
  \texttt{xxx} - and the model still executes nearly the same trajectory.
  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.56\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      The policy is not reading the instruction. It is matching the \emph{scene} to a
      memorised trajectory. The authors' own word for the reported accuracies is "rote
      recall".}}
    \column{0.44\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!12}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      They also find a hard positional cliff: success collapses once the object is displaced
      past a small threshold, and stays at zero.}}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{It is not one benchmark having a bad day}
  \small
  \vskip -4pt
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.79\textwidth]{language_collapse.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vskip -4pt
  \footnotesize
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.53\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\scriptsize
      \textbf{DAERT} is Diversity-Aware Embodied Red Teaming (Tong et al., 2026): train an
      attacker with reinforcement learning to \emph{search} for rephrasings that break the
      policy. No adversarial patch, no jailbreak - just different English words for the same
      request.}}
    \column{0.47\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!12}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\scriptsize
      Human raters judge the generated instructions \textbf{natural}. A related 2026 method
      also shows a partial defence: fine-tuning on generated adversarial instructions
      recovers some ground on \emph{unseen} ones.}}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{L33 said the prior outvotes the image. Here it is worse.}
  \small
  In L33, a vision-language model hallucinated a refrigerator into a kitchen photo: the
  \textbf{language prior} was trained on trillions of tokens and easily outvoted 576 blurry
  visual vectors.
  \vskip 6pt
  \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.97\linewidth}{\small
    Flip it around. In a VLA the \textbf{vision} pathway carries the policy and the language
    input can be ignored entirely. Swap the object named in the instruction and the model
    executes the original task anyway. It is not that language loses a vote - it never cast
    one.}}
  \vskip 8pt
  \textbf{Two structural causes, both fixable in principle:}
  \vskip 4pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \footnotesize
    \textbf{1. The training instructions are templated.} If every demonstration says "pick
    up the X and place it in the Y", the model learns the slot filler, not the sentence.
    Everyone optimised task diversity; nobody optimised linguistic diversity.
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \footnotesize
    \textbf{2. Vision wins every conflict.} Build a scene where the instruction contradicts
    the visual prior (Fang et al., 2026) and models systematically follow the picture, even
    when following the text was correct.
  \end{columns}
  \vskip 6pt
  \footnotesize
  \centering
  A 2026 ablation study points the same way from the architecture side: the \emph{visual}
  module is the bottleneck, and a better general vision-language model does not reliably
  make a better controller.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{In Armenian it would be much worse}
  \small
  Dong et al.\ (2026) translated the standard benchmark into ten languages and measured
  \textbf{success-rate drops of 30 to 50\% on non-English instructions}.
  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.52\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      Their more interesting finding is \textbf{step-wise} sensitivity: language dependence
      is wildly non-uniform along a trajectory. A few steps dominate all the failure; most
      steps are effectively language-agnostic.
      \vskip 3pt
      Their fix intervenes only at those steps.}}
    \column{0.48\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!12}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      \textbf{A concrete prediction you can check.} Hand an open VLA an instruction in
      Armenian and it will very probably fail - and now you can say roughly why, and roughly
      \emph{where} in the trajectory.}}
  \end{columns}
  \vskip 10pt
  \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.97\linewidth}{\footnotesize
    \textbf{What would count as fixed.} A model that is flat under adversarial paraphrase
    search \emph{and} whose success collapses appropriately when the instruction is replaced
    with nonsense. Both directions together. Nobody has shown this.}}
\end{frame}

\section{Embodied red teaming}

\begin{frame}[plain]
  \vfill
  \begin{center}
    {\Large\color{popblue}\textbf{Embodied red teaming}}\\[0.9em]
    \begin{minipage}{0.82\textwidth}\centering\small
      A new category of attack: nobody jailbreaks anything. The attacker moves an object.
    \end{minipage}
  \end{center}
  \vfill
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Token space is not configuration space}
  \small
  Everything you know about model safety was learned on text, and it does not carry over.
  \vskip 10pt
  \begin{center}
    \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.93\linewidth}{\small
      A model that refuses to \emph{describe} how to knock a glass off a table will happily
      emit the action trajectory that knocks it off.
      \vskip 4pt
      The safety constraint was learned in \textbf{token space}. The harm happens in
      \textbf{configuration space}.}}
  \end{center}
  \vskip 10pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \footnotesize
    That framing is from SafeVLA (Zhang et al., 2025), which formulates safety as a
    constrained Markov decision process and optimises against elicited unsafe behaviours.
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!12}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      The 2026 framing is sharper still: risk "has fundamentally shifted from the intent
      space to the physical space". The intent stays benign throughout.}}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{RedVLA: the attacker moves a knife}
  \small
  \textbf{RedVLA} (Zhang et al., April 2026) is a red-teaming framework for \emph{physical}
  safety. Two stages, and neither touches the prompt.
  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \footnotesize
    \textbf{1. Risk scenario synthesis.} Find the critical interaction regions along a
    perfectly benign trajectory. Inject a risk object - a knife, a bottle - into one of
    them, keeping the task feasible and the instruction unchanged.
    \vskip 6pt
    \textbf{2. Trajectory-driven amplification.} Gradient-free search over \emph{where to
    put it}, refined until it reliably triggers unsafe behaviour. Gradient-free, so it
    works across model families.
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      \textbf{Attack success rate, six models, LIBERO:} \\
      64.9\% (OpenVLA) to \textbf{95.5\%} (pi-0.5), within 10 optimisation iterations.
      \vskip 4pt
      Validated sim-to-real on a physical Franka arm as well.}}
    \vskip 5pt
    \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!12}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      The scene looks benign. The instruction \emph{is} benign. Nothing in the input would
      trip a text filter.}}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The better policy is the more attackable one}
  \small
  \vskip -6pt
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.60\textwidth]{redvla_coupling.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vskip -4pt
  \footnotesize
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\scriptsize
      \textbf{The harm happens while the robot is working.} Across all six models most unsafe
      rollouts are "Success $+$ Unsafe" or "Attempt $+$ Unsafe" - the policy completed or was
      actively pursuing the task \emph{and} caused harm on the way. Outright collapse is only a
      small fraction.}}
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!12}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\scriptsize
      \textbf{So task success rate is not a safety metric, and here it is
      anti-correlated with one.} Every leaderboard in this lecture would score those
      rollouts as wins.}}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Defences cost capability, and the robots are already shipping}
  \small
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \footnotesize
    \textbf{What exists.} Runtime monitors that halt execution before an unsafe action;
    constrained-optimisation safety training; external safety controllers.
    \vskip 5pt
    RedVLA's own monitor cuts attack success by roughly two thirds and costs about 4 points
    of benign performance. SafeVLA reports the friendlier result - an 83.6\% cut in
    cumulative violation cost with task success \emph{up} 3.85\% - but on its own
    procedurally generated benchmark.
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      \textbf{What does not exist.} Formal verification of a neural policy. Certification.
      Any accepted standard for what a VLA must be tested against before it works near a
      person.
      \vskip 4pt
      The 2026 consensus is \emph{layered} mitigation, which is an admission that the policy
      itself cannot be trusted.}}
  \end{columns}
  \vskip 8pt
  \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!12}{\parbox{0.97\linewidth}{\footnotesize
    \textbf{Put these two facts on the same slide, because they are true at the same time.}
    Humanoids are in early commercial deployment and the research literature reports attack
    success rates above 90\% on the same class of model. An industrial cell with cages and
    interlocks is a different risk category from a kitchen, and the field is moving toward
    the kitchen.}}
\end{frame}

\section{Can we measure any of this?}

\begin{frame}[plain]
  \vfill
  \begin{center}
    {\Large\color{popblue}\textbf{Can we measure any of this?}}\\[0.9em]
    \begin{minipage}{0.82\textwidth}\centering\small
      Including the evidence in the last two sections. Especially that.
    \end{minipage}
  \end{center}
  \vfill
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The test set is a room}
  \small
  Simulation is a proxy, and the saturated kind: LIBERO now sits at 95-98\% and the
  difference between 98\% and 99\% carries no information. So the claims that matter are
  made on physical robots, where evaluation has problems with no analogue anywhere else in
  machine learning.
  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \footnotesize
    \begin{itemize}\itemsep 3pt
      \item \textbf{The test set is a physical room.} Lighting drifts through the day,
        objects get placed slightly differently, the gripper wears. Another lab cannot
        reproduce your evaluation because they do not have your room.
      \item \textbf{Every trial costs a minute and a human reset.} So $n$ is tiny.
      \item \textbf{There is no held-out set} in any meaningful sense.
    \end{itemize}
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      PhAIL (Arkhangelskiy, 2026) states the practice plainly: real-world VLA evaluation
      "still rests on binary success rate at a fixed timeout with $N \le 25$ rollouts per
      condition, almost always \textbf{without confidence intervals or paired statistical
      comparison}".}}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Do the arithmetic}
  \small
  \vskip -6pt
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.72\textwidth]{wilson_ci.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vskip -4pt
  \footnotesize
  \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.97\linewidth}{\scriptsize
    Two policies reported at \textbf{70\%} and \textbf{80\%} on 20 trials are statistically
    indistinguishable. A very large share of published VLA comparisons sits inside that
    noise band - which means the ranking, and sometimes the paper's conclusion, could flip
    on a rerun.}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Now turn it on this lecture}
  \small
  Everything in the last two sections is subject to its own critique, and a course that
  taught you to check numbers should say so out loud.
  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!12}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      \textbf{The bad news is measured on the same weak instruments.} The 0.0\%, the
      93.3\% $\rightarrow$ 5.9\%, the attack success rates - almost all of it is
      \emph{simulation}, on the benchmark the same authors just told you is saturated.}}
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      \textbf{Why it still stands.} The direction is unanimous across at least five
      independent groups with different methods, and the gibberish-instruction result needs
      no statistics at all: either the trajectory changes or it does not.}}
  \end{columns}
  \vskip 8pt
  \textbf{How to read any VLA result, in six questions:}
  \vskip 3pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \footnotesize
    \begin{enumerate}\itemsep 1pt
      \item Simulation or real? If real, what is $n$?
      \item Which LIBERO? 97\% on the plain one means almost nothing in 2026.
      \item Was the instruction varied at all?
    \end{enumerate}
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \footnotesize
    \begin{enumerate}\itemsep 1pt
      \setcounter{enumi}{3}
      \item Was the camera pose or object placement varied?
      \item Company blog or paper?
      \item Is a demo being used as evidence?
    \end{enumerate}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Recap}
  \small
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.54\textwidth}
    \begin{enumerate}\scriptsize\itemsep 3pt
      \item A VLA is a vision-language model whose output is a motor command. It deletes the
        four-stage classical stack; everything else follows from that.
      \item Action chunking exists because there is no teacher forcing on a robot.
        Dual-system designs exist because a 3B forward pass is a decade too slow for contact.
      \item Data is the whole problem. The corpus is three orders of magnitude short of
        video and cannot be scraped.
      \item \textbf{The language channel is largely dead.} 90\%+ collapses to 0.0\%, and
        gibberish instructions produce the same trajectory.
      \item \textbf{Embodied red teaming is a new risk category.} Capability and
        vulnerability rise together, and the robot succeeds \emph{dangerously}.
      \item \textbf{And we cannot measure any of it well.} $N \le 25$ trials, no intervals -
        which cuts against finding 4's own benchmarks too.
    \end{enumerate}
    \column{0.46\textwidth}
    \vskip 4pt
    \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\scriptsize
      \textbf{Next:} the chapter page carries the reading list and the sources behind every
      number in this deck.
      \vskip 3pt
      Three observations would make this deck out of date, and all three are live: a data
      source that scales without human robot-time; a model flat under paraphrase search
      \emph{and} broken by nonsense; one shared real-robot evaluation a second lab can
      reproduce inside stated intervals.}}
    \vskip 4pt
    \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!12}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\scriptsize
      And yet the trajectory is steep. RT-1 was under four years ago, ran at 3 Hz, on one
      robot, in one kitchen.}}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}
