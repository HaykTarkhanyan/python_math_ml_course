% ============================================================ COLD OPEN
\begin{frame}{The question we ended on}
  \vspace{0.7em}
  Two lectures of methods, all of them operating on \textbf{heads}, \textbf{layers} and
  \textbf{neurons} - the units the architecture happens to give us.

  \vspace{0.7em}
  And the results kept being strange: a head that suppresses the right answer, four heads waiting
  to take over, a head that stares at the answer and writes nothing.

  \vspace{1.0em}
  \begin{center}
    {\large What if heads and neurons are simply the \textbf{wrong units}?}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{1.0em}
  \keybox{Compare: you can describe a chemical reaction atom by atom, and you will be correct and
  learn nothing. \textbf{Molecules} are the level at which chemistry becomes sayable.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Two neurons}
  \vspace{0.1em}
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.99\textwidth]{fig/polysemantic_neuron.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vspace{-0.5em}
  {\footnotesize Top activations over a corpus of 36 sentences spanning ten unrelated domains.
  Left: numbers, every time, whatever the topic. Right: the Silk Road, simmering a stew, Roman
  emperors, and freezing weather.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{One of those is the exception}
  \vspace{0.8em}
  Neuron 1912 has \textbf{one readable job}: it fires on number words, in a finance sentence, a
  programming sentence and a history sentence alike. You could name it.

  \vspace{0.7em}
  Neuron 152 has \textbf{several}, and they have nothing to do with each other. You cannot name
  it, because there is no single thing to name.

  \vspace{0.9em}
  \keybox{\textbf{Polysemanticity.} One neuron, many unrelated meanings. In a real network this
  is the \textbf{normal case} - which means ``find the neuron for X'' is usually the wrong
  question, and every method from the last two lectures inherits that problem.}

  \vspace{0.7em}
  {\footnotesize And remember lecture 1: top-activating examples are exactly the method that gave
  a clean, convincing, \emph{wrong} story about a BERT neuron. We are looking at weak evidence,
  on purpose, because it is the evidence the units give us.}
\end{frame}

% ============================================================ SECTION 1
\section{Superposition}
\sectiontransition{Superposition}{Why the network does this to us on purpose}

\begin{frame}{Predict first}
  \vspace{1.0em}
  GPT-2 small's residual stream is \textbf{768 numbers} wide.

  \vspace{1.3em}
  \begin{center}
    {\large How many distinct concepts can it represent?}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{1.3em}
  \begin{center}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{@{}ccc@{}}
    fewer than 768 & exactly 768 & many more than 768\\
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{1.2em}
  \begin{center}
    {\footnotesize The intuitive answer is 768 - one concept per dimension, like columns in a
    dataframe.}
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The intuition is wrong, and the geometry says why}
  \vspace{0.1em}
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.94\textwidth]{fig/superposition_geometry.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vspace{-0.4em}
  {\footnotesize Left: pairwise cosine similarity of random unit vectors. Right: the worst
  interference among $n$ random directions, as $n$ grows.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Reading those two panels}
  \vspace{0.6em}
  \textbf{Left.} In two dimensions, two random directions are usually far from perpendicular. By
  768 dimensions, essentially every pair is nearly perpendicular - the distribution collapses onto
  zero.

  \vspace{0.7em}
  \textbf{Right.} Pack in more directions and interference grows, but \emph{slowly}:

  \vspace{0.4em}
  \begin{center}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{@{}lrr@{}}
    \toprule
    & \textbf{4 directions} & \textbf{4096 directions}\\
    \midrule
    $d = 16$ & 0.42 & 0.91\\
    $d = 128$ & 0.23 & 0.46\\
    $d = 768$ & \textbf{0.06} & \textbf{0.20}\\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.7em}
  \keybox{At width 768 you can hold \textbf{4096} directions - five times the number of
  dimensions - and no two of them overlap by more than \textbf{0.20}. Nearly-orthogonal is
  cheap; exactly-orthogonal is what costs a dimension.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{So the network takes the deal}
  \vspace{0.7em}
  Two facts about the concepts a language model needs:

  \vspace{0.5em}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item there are \textbf{far more} of them than it has dimensions;
    \item almost all of them are \textbf{off} at any given moment - a sentence about cheese does
      not need the feature for Armenian grammar or for Python syntax.
  \end{itemize}

  \vspace{0.9em}
  \keybox{\textbf{Superposition.} Given sparse features and limited width, the network stores
  more features than it has dimensions, accepting a little interference in exchange for a lot of
  capacity. Polysemantic neurons are the \textbf{symptom}; this trade is the cause.}

  \vspace{0.8em}
  \takebox{The model is not being difficult. It is being efficient, and our preferred unit of
  analysis is the casualty.}

  \vspace{0.4em}
  {\footnotesize Elhage et al.\ (2022), \emph{Toy Models of Superposition} - where this is
  demonstrated in models small enough to see it happen.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The assumption underneath all of this}
  \vspace{0.9em}
  Everything we are about to do rests on one idea:

  \vspace{0.7em}
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armblue!8, colframe=armblue, boxrule=0.8pt, arc=3pt,
                    width=0.88\textwidth]
    \centering\large
    \textbf{The linear representation hypothesis}\\[0.5em]
    Concepts are stored as \textbf{directions}, and combinations of concepts are
    \textbf{sums} of those directions.
  \end{tcolorbox}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.9em}
  It has good evidence: steering by adding a vector works, probes find things, the whole
  attribution arithmetic from lecture 1 relies on it.

  \vspace{0.8em}
  \watchbox{It is a \textbf{hypothesis}, not a theorem. There is active work arguing that
  important structure is not linear at all. When you read that a feature ``is'' a direction, the
  word doing the work is an assumption.}
\end{frame}

% ============================================================ SECTION 2
\section{Sparse autoencoders}
\sectiontransition{Sparse autoencoders}{You have already built one of these}

\begin{frame}{Back to the autoencoder chapter}
  \vspace{0.7em}
  In chapter 8 you built a sparse autoencoder and ran it on a small recurrent network's hidden
  state. You already know:

  \vspace{0.5em}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item encoder $\rightarrow$ a \textbf{wide, sparse} code $\rightarrow$ decoder;
    \item the decoder columns form a \textbf{dictionary} of directions;
    \item the sparsity penalty is L1, the same one from Lasso in chapter 1;
    \item $L_0$ - how many features fire at once - is the number that matters.
  \end{itemize}

  \vspace{0.9em}
  \keybox{Nothing about the method is new today. What is new is the \textbf{scale}, and the fact
  that you will \textbf{use} one rather than train it.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What changes at language-model scale}
  \vspace{0.6em}
  \begin{center}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
    \toprule
    & \textbf{Your homework} & \textbf{A real one}\\
    \midrule
    Read from & a 32-unit hidden state & a 768- to 5000-wide residual stream\\[0.4em]
    Dictionary size & 512 features & tens of thousands to millions\\[0.4em]
    Ground truth & you generated the corpus & none whatsoever\\[0.4em]
    Who trains it & you, in a notebook & a lab, over weeks\\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.8em}
  The third row is the hard one. In your homework you could check a feature's name because you
  built the data. On real text, \textbf{nobody can}.

  \vspace{0.8em}
  \takebox{Which is why the discipline from lecture 2 carries over unchanged: naming a feature is
  a guess, and \textbf{ablating it is the test}.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{You do not have to train one}
  \vspace{0.7em}
  \textbf{Gemma Scope 2} (Google DeepMind, December 2025) is an open suite of sparse autoencoders
  and transcoders covering \textbf{every layer} of every Gemma 3 model, from 270 million to 27
  billion parameters, pretrained and instruction-tuned.

  \vspace{0.6em}
  Producing it took roughly \textbf{110 petabytes} of stored activations and over a trillion
  trained parameters.

  \vspace{0.9em}
  \keybox{That work is done, and it is free. The interesting question stopped being ``can we
  train a dictionary'' and became \textbf{``what do we ask it?''}}

  \vspace{0.8em}
  \textbf{Neuronpedia} hosts the results: a browsable index of features with their top
  activations, their effects on the output, and their connections. No installation, no GPU, no
  download.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Let us actually look}
  \vspace{1.0em}
  \demobox{\texttt{neuronpedia.org} - pick a model, search for a concept, read the feature.}

  \vspace{1.0em}
  What to look at while we browse:

  \vspace{0.5em}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item the \textbf{top activating} text for a feature - is it one thing?
    \item what the feature \textbf{does to the output} when it fires;
    \item how many features look like near-duplicates of each other.
  \end{itemize}

  \vspace{0.9em}
  \watchbox{Keep lecture 1 in mind while it looks impressive. We are reading top-activating
  examples again - the method that has already fooled this field once.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Where sparse autoencoders are weak}
  \vspace{0.5em}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Feature splitting.} Widen the dictionary and one broad feature becomes several
      narrow ones. So the ``true'' number of features is not well defined - it is a function of
      how big a dictionary you trained.
    \item \textbf{Dead features.} A large fraction never fire at all, wasting the dictionary.
    \item \textbf{No ground truth.} We cannot check the answers, only their plausibility.
    \item \textbf{They describe representation, not computation.} A list of what is present says
      nothing about what is \emph{done} with it - the lecture-1 problem, one level up.
  \end{itemize}

  \vspace{0.7em}
  \trapbox{A 2025 paper states it flatly in the title: \emph{Sparse Autoencoders Do Not Find
  Canonical Units of Analysis}.}

  \vspace{0.6em}
  \takebox{Current advice from the field, roughly: \textbf{use them as a tool inside a project
  about something else}, not as the project.}
\end{frame}

% ============================================================ SECTION 3
\section{Attribution graphs}
\sectiontransition{Attribution graphs}{From what is represented to what is computed}

\begin{frame}{The gap that needs closing}
  \vspace{0.8em}
  \begin{center}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{@{}ll@{}}
    \toprule
    Lecture 2 gave us & circuits, by hand, over months, for one task\\
    Sparse autoencoders give us & good units, and no circuit\\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{1.0em}
  \keybox{What we want is lecture 2's \textbf{circuit}, drawn automatically, in lecture 3's
  \textbf{units}.}

  \vspace{1.0em}
  The obstacle is the multi-layer perceptron blocks: a sparse autoencoder describes the
  activations going in and coming out, but not the map between them.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Transcoders}
  \vspace{0.9em}
  A \textbf{transcoder} replaces an entire block with a sparse, interpretable approximation of
  \emph{what it computes} - input features in, output features out.

  \vspace{0.9em}
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tikzpicture}[font=\small]
    \node[draw, rounded corners, fill=lightbg, minimum width=2.6cm, minimum height=0.9cm,
          align=center] (in) at (0,0) {features\\in};
    \node[draw, rounded corners, fill=armorange!22, minimum width=2.9cm, minimum height=0.9cm,
          align=center] (t) at (4.3,0) {\textbf{transcoder}\\\scriptsize sparse, readable};
    \node[draw, rounded corners, fill=paramgreen!15, minimum width=2.6cm, minimum height=0.9cm,
          align=center] (out) at (8.6,0) {features\\out};
    \draw[-{Latex[length=2.5mm]}, thick] (in) -- (t);
    \draw[-{Latex[length=2.5mm]}, thick] (t) -- (out);
  \end{tikzpicture}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.9em}
  \takebox{A sparse autoencoder makes the \textbf{state} readable. A transcoder makes the
  \textbf{step} readable. Chain the steps and you have a graph.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Attribution graphs}
  \vspace{0.7em}
  Trace which features cause which other features, all the way to the output, and you get an
  \textbf{attribution graph}: a wiring diagram for one specific prompt.

  \vspace{0.8em}
  \keybox{This is lecture 2's project - done automatically, in minutes, in units that mean
  something. It is the single biggest practical change in this field in the last two years.}

  \vspace{0.9em}
  Anthropic published the method in March 2025 and \textbf{open-sourced the tooling} in May.
  \texttt{circuit-tracer} runs on Gemma-2-2B, Llama-3.2-1B and Qwen3-4B, and the graphs are
  explorable in the browser through Neuronpedia.

  \vspace{0.5em}
  {\footnotesize Ameisen, Lindsey, Pearce et al.\ (2025), \emph{Circuit Tracing}.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What they found: the model plans ahead}
  \vspace{0.7em}
  Ask a model to write a rhyming couplet. The natural assumption is that it writes the second
  line word by word and arranges for a rhyme when it arrives at the end.

  \vspace{0.9em}
  \trapbox{The graph shows otherwise. The feature for the \textbf{rhyming word at the end of the
  line} is active \emph{before the model has written the words leading up to it}. It picks the
  destination, then writes a line that gets there.}

  \vspace{0.9em}
  \keybox{Next-token prediction is the \textbf{training objective}. It is not a description of
  what the model does internally. Those are different claims, and this is the clearest evidence
  we have that they come apart.}

  \vspace{0.4em}
  {\footnotesize Lindsey et al.\ (2025), \emph{On the Biology of a Large Language Model}.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{And: concepts are shared across languages}
  \vspace{0.8em}
  The same features fire for a concept whether the prompt is in English, French or Chinese. The
  model appears to translate into an internal representation, operate there, and translate back
  out.

  \vspace{1.0em}
  \keybox{For a bilingual room this is worth sitting with: the evidence suggests the model is not
  running a separate Armenian model and English model. It has \textbf{one} set of concepts, with
  language as something closer to an input and output format.}

  \vspace{0.9em}
  {\footnotesize A caution: ``appears to'' is doing real work in that sentence. The finding is
  about feature overlap in one model family, not a general law about multilingual networks.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Try it yourself}
  \vspace{1.1em}
  \demobox{\texttt{neuronpedia.org/graph} - type a prompt, get its attribution graph.}

  \vspace{1.1em}
  \takebox{The thing to notice: this took a research team \textbf{months} in lecture 2, on a
  smaller model, for a simpler task. It now takes a text box and about a minute.\\[0.5em]
  That is what progress looks like in this field - not a better theory, but a tool that makes the
  old work cheap.}
\end{frame}

% ============================================================ SECTION 4
\section{Steering}
\sectiontransition{Steering}{If a concept is a direction, push in that direction}

\begin{frame}{The simplest possible intervention}
  \vspace{0.9em}
  If the linear representation hypothesis holds, then to make a model more about something, you
  add that something's direction to the residual stream:

  \vspace{0.6em}
  \[
    \text{activation} \;\leftarrow\; \text{activation} \;+\; \alpha \cdot \mathbf{v}_{\text{concept}}
  \]

  \vspace{0.9em}
  \keybox{You have seen this move before. In the diffusion chapter, classifier-free guidance
  pushed a generation toward a prompt by moving along a direction. Same idea, different object.}

  \vspace{0.8em}
  It is the cheapest control mechanism there is: no fine-tuning, no retraining, one vector
  addition at inference time.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Golden Gate Claude}
  \vspace{0.8em}
  You met this in chapter 8. Anthropic found the feature for the Golden Gate Bridge and clamped
  it on.

  \vspace{0.7em}
  The model brought the bridge into everything - recipes, arithmetic, its own description of
  itself. Asked what it was, it said it was the bridge.

  \vspace{1.0em}
  \keybox{Why it matters as \textbf{evidence}, not as a joke: it demonstrates the feature was
  \textbf{causal}. Naming a feature from its top activations is a guess. Clamping it and watching
  identity-level behaviour follow is a test - the lecture-2 standard, applied to a feature.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The refusal direction}
  \vspace{0.8em}
  A model's willingness to refuse a request turns out to be mediated substantially by a
  \textbf{single direction} in activation space.

  \vspace{0.8em}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.47\textwidth}
      \textbf{Add it}\\[0.3em]
      \small The model refuses more, including things it should answer.
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.47\textwidth}
      \textbf{Subtract it}\\[0.3em]
      \small Safety training stops applying. This is a jailbreak, and it needs no prompt
      engineering at all.
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}

  \vspace{1.0em}
  \trapbox{The same result is a safety tool and an attack. Interpretability is not inherently
  defensive - understanding a mechanism is exactly what you need in order to disable it.}

  \vspace{0.6em}
  {\footnotesize This is a good argument for open-weight interpretability research being done in
  public, and a good argument against it. Both are real.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Steering is not editing}
  \vspace{1.0em}
  \watchbox{Steering vectors are \textbf{brittle}. The effect often does not generalise past the
  prompts it was built on, it degrades the model's general capability, and it has off-target
  consequences nobody looked for.}

  \vspace{1.0em}
  A steering vector is a demonstration that a direction is causal. It is not a reliable way to
  change what a model does in production.

  \vspace{1.0em}
  \takebox{Useful rule: treat steering as \textbf{evidence about the model}, not as a
  \textbf{feature of your product}.}
\end{frame}

% ============================================================ SECTION 5
\section{Does the model mean what it says?}
\sectiontransition{Does the model mean what it says?}{The part of this chapter you will use tomorrow}

\begin{frame}{You all use these}
  \vspace{0.8em}
  Modern reasoning models print their chain of thought - a paragraph of working before the
  answer. You read it, and it is genuinely useful.

  \vspace{1.0em}
  \begin{center}
    {\large Is that text a \textbf{transcript} of the computation,\\ or a \textbf{story about}
    the computation?}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{1.0em}
  \begin{center}
    {\footnotesize Answer honestly before the next slide. Almost everyone reads it as a
    transcript - that is what it looks like.}
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Faithfulness, and how to test it}
  \vspace{0.7em}
  \textbf{Faithfulness} is the technical name for that assumption: does the stated reasoning
  correspond to the computation that produced the answer?

  \vspace{0.8em}
  It is testable, and the shape of the test is the one from lecture 2:

  \vspace{0.5em}
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item change something that provably affects the answer;
    \item check whether the stated reasoning mentions it.
  \end{enumerate}

  \vspace{0.9em}
  \trapbox{Where the answer moves and the explanation does not, the text is \textbf{not} a
  record of the computation. And this is measurable - it is not a philosophical worry.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What the graphs show}
  \vspace{0.8em}
  The attribution-graph work found cases where the model's \textbf{stated reasoning does not
  match its traced computation} - the written steps and the causal path diverge.

  \vspace{1.0em}
  \keybox{We are not inferring this from behaviour. We can see one path in the graph and read a
  different one in the text.}

  \vspace{1.0em}
  Two consequences, and the second is the serious one:

  \vspace{0.5em}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item a plausible explanation is not evidence the answer is right;
    \item \textbf{reading the chain of thought is only oversight to the degree it is faithful.}
      If a model can reach an answer by a route its transcript never mentions, watching the
      transcript is not watching the model.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What this means for you, concretely}
  \vspace{0.9em}
  \takebox{The reasoning trace is \textbf{evidence about} the answer. It is not a
  \textbf{proof of} the answer.}

  \vspace{1.0em}
  Do not treat ``the model explained its steps'' as verification. That is the same category error
  we made in lecture 1, at full scale: a probe finding information is not the model using it, and
  a model narrating a method is not the model having used it.

  \vspace{1.0em}
  \watchbox{And the honest limit: we can detect \textbf{some} unfaithfulness. We cannot certify
  faithfulness. There is no procedure today that takes a chain of thought and returns
  ``verified''.}

  \vspace{0.5em}
  {\footnotesize Interpretability for reasoning models is one of the field's most active gaps -
  the models people actually use are the ones we understand least.}
\end{frame}

% ============================================================ SECTION 6
\section{What this is for}
\sectiontransition{What this is for}{Sorting the parts you can use from the parts you watch}

\begin{frame}{Probes, in production}
  \vspace{0.8em}
  Of everything in these three lectures, this is the technique most likely to end up in your job.

  \vspace{0.7em}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item A probe is logistic regression on activations - lecture 1, chapter 3.
    \item Cheap to train, cheap to run, runs alongside the model.
    \item Already deployed for real content monitoring.
  \end{itemize}

  \vspace{0.9em}
  \keybox{If you are running an open-weight model and you need to know whether it is about to do
  something - a probe on its activations is a better detector than a classifier on its output,
  because it fires \textbf{before} the text exists.}

  \vspace{0.6em}
  \watchbox{With lecture 1's caveat attached forever: a probe tells you the information is
  \textbf{present}. Validate against behaviour before you trust it as a control.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Debugging a model you actually ship}
  \vspace{0.8em}
  The canonical example is embarrassing and real: models insisting that \textbf{9.8 is smaller
  than 9.11}.

  \vspace{0.7em}
  Not a knowledge gap - they know which is bigger if you ask differently. Interpretability work
  traced the internal features responsible, including ones that treat the strings as version
  numbers and dates.

  \vspace{0.9em}
  \takebox{The general move: \textbf{when a model fails strangely and the input looks fine, the
  explanation is in the activations.} Behavioural testing tells you \emph{that} it failed.
  Nothing else tells you \emph{why}.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Reading a model before you trust it}
  \vspace{0.8em}
  Most of you will deploy \textbf{open-weight} models - Gemma, Llama, Qwen - which are exactly
  the models the tooling in this lecture supports.

  \vspace{0.7em}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Neuronpedia and \texttt{circuit-tracer} need no training and no GPU.
    \item Gemma Scope 2 covers every layer of every Gemma 3 size.
  \end{itemize}

  \vspace{0.9em}
  \keybox{Frame it as \textbf{due diligence}, not research. Before you put a model in front of
  customers, you can now ask what it is doing on your actual inputs - and that is a new option,
  about two years old.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Evaluating claims made at you}
  \vspace{0.9em}
  You will be told that a model ``understands'' your domain, ``reasons about'' your data, or
  ``knows'' when it is uncertain. Sometimes by a vendor, sometimes by a paper, sometimes by a
  colleague.

  \vspace{1.1em}
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=paramgreen!8, colframe=paramgreen, boxrule=0.8pt, arc=3pt,
                    width=0.86\textwidth]
    \centering\large
    \textbf{Did anyone intervene, or did they just look?}
  \end{tcolorbox}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{1.1em}
  \takebox{That one question separates the two halves of this chapter, and you can ask it without
  running anything. It is the most portable thing here.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What the labs are doing with it}
  \vspace{0.6em}
  Compressed deliberately - this is the part you \textbf{watch}, not the part you use.

  \vspace{0.6em}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Auditing games.} Hide a goal inside a model, then see whether a red team can
      find it with interpretability tools. Worth knowing about because it manufactures the
      \textbf{ground truth} this field otherwise lacks.
    \item \textbf{Model organisms.} Deliberately install a known behaviour so methods can be
      tested against a known answer. Same motivation.
    \item \textbf{Monitoring reasoning at scale}, which is section 5's problem with a budget.
  \end{itemize}

  \vspace{0.7em}
  {\footnotesize MIT Technology Review named mechanistic interpretability one of its ten
  breakthrough technologies of 2026. Context for why the field suddenly has money - \textbf{not}
  an argument that it works.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The asymmetry, said out loud}
  \vspace{1.0em}
  \trapbox{Most of the headline results in this chapter come from three or four well-funded labs,
  with model access, compute and staff that nobody in this room has.}

  \vspace{1.0em}
  That is a real limitation on the field, and you should factor it into how you read the
  literature.

  \vspace{1.0em}
  \takebox{It is \textbf{not} a reason the techniques are useless. Probes, patching, attribution
  and ablation all work fine on a 124-million-parameter model on a laptop - as every figure in
  these three lectures demonstrates. All of them were made on this machine.}
\end{frame}

\section{Where this leaves us}
\sectiontransition{Where this leaves us}{An honest summary, and how to go further}

\begin{frame}{What is genuinely unsolved}
  \vspace{0.7em}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{No ground truth.} We usually cannot check whether an explanation is right,
      which is why auditing games and model organisms exist at all.
    \item \textbf{No canonical units.} Neurons are polysemantic; sparse-autoencoder features
      split as you widen the dictionary. The right unit is an open question.
    \item \textbf{Coverage.} We explain individual behaviours on individual prompts. Nobody
      explains a model.
    \item \textbf{Reasoning models} - the ones everyone uses - are the least understood.
  \end{itemize}

  \vspace{0.7em}
  {\footnotesize A good map of the open problems: Sharkey et al.\ (2025), \emph{Open Problems in
  Mechanistic Interpretability}.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The sentence to leave with}
  \vspace{1.6em}
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armblue!8, colframe=armblue, boxrule=0.9pt, arc=3pt,
                    width=0.9\textwidth]
    \centering\large
    We can now say useful, testable things about what is inside a language model.\\[0.7em]
    We cannot yet explain one.
  \end{tcolorbox}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{1.6em}
  \begin{center}
    Both halves are true, and people will try to sell you either half on its own.
  \end{center}
\end{frame}
