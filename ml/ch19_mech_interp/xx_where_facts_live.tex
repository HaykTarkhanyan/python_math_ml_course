% ============================================================ SECTION 1
\section{An MLP is a lookup table}
\sectiontransition{An MLP is a lookup table}{Keys that recognise, values that write}

\begin{frame}{The MLP, written out}
  At one position, the MLP of a block reads the stream $x$ (768 numbers) and writes back:
  \[
    h = \operatorname{GELU}(x\, W_{\text{in}} + b_{\text{in}}) \quad (3072 \text{ numbers}),
    \qquad \text{out} = h\, W_{\text{out}} + b_{\text{out}}
  \]

  Now read $h\, W_{\text{out}}$ one neuron at a time. $W_{\text{out}}$ has one \textbf{row} per
  neuron, so the product is a weighted sum of rows:
  \begin{align*}
    h\, W_{\text{out}}
      &= h_1\, W_{\text{out}}[1] + h_2\, W_{\text{out}}[2] + \cdots + h_{3072}\, W_{\text{out}}[3072]\\
      &= \sum_{n=1}^{3072} \underbrace{h_n}_{\substack{\text{how strongly neuron } n\\ \text{recognised its pattern}}}
         \cdot \underbrace{W_{\text{out}}[n]}_{\text{what neuron } n \text{ writes}}
  \end{align*}

  \vspace{-0.4em}
  \keybox{\small Neuron $n$ is a \textbf{key} (column $n$ of $W_{\text{in}}$: the pattern it fires
  on) and a \textbf{value} (row $n$ of $W_{\text{out}}$: what it adds). An MLP is a soft lookup
  table with 3072 entries. {\footnotesize (Geva et al., 2021)}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{By hand: three memories}
  \vspace{0.2em}
  A toy MLP reading a 2-number stream, with ReLU instead of GELU so the arithmetic is easy:

  \vspace{0.4em}
  \begin{center}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{@{}llll@{}}
    \toprule
    neuron & key (fires on\ldots) & value (writes\ldots) & \\
    \midrule
    1 & $(1, 0)$ & ``basketball'' & a Jordan-like input\\
    2 & $(0, 1)$ & ``golf'' & a Woods-like input\\
    3 & $(0.5, 0.5)$ & ``is a sport'' & any athlete\\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.3em}
  Input $x = (1,\; 0.2)$ - mostly Jordan:
  \[
    h = \operatorname{ReLU}\big(x \cdot \text{keys}\big) = (1,\;\; 0.2,\;\; 0.6)
    \quad\Rightarrow\quad
    \text{out} = 1\cdot\text{basketball} + 0.2\cdot\text{golf} + 0.6\cdot\text{sport}
  \]

  \vspace{0.3em}
  \keybox{\small The output is \textbf{mostly} ``basketball'', a bit of ``golf'', and a general
  ``sport'' push that every athlete gets. A real MLP does this with 3072 neurons at once - and
  that last column is the catch, as the next slide shows.}
\end{frame}

  \vspace{0.2em}

  unembedding - which tokens does that single write push up?

  \vspace{0.1em}
  \begin{center}
  \footnotesize
  \begin{tabular}{@{}rrl@{}}
    \toprule
    layer & rank of `` basketball'' & top tokens it pushes\\
    \midrule
    0 & 1,941 & ``Jordan'', ``Jordan'', ``Scion'', ``Amon''\\
    2 & 3,503 & ``arsity'', ``XI'', ``chio'', ``otto''\\
    4 & 17,132 & ``Mania'', ``ét'', ``Mi'', ``TD''\\
    6 & 498 & ``opponents'', ``enters'', ``defenders'', ``bats''\\
    8 & 22,689 & ``travels'', ``lashes'', ``suffers'', ``scrapped''\\
    10 & 12,932 & ``missed'', ``misses'', ``scored'', ``shoots''\\
    11 & 35,271 & ``averaged'', ``ranks'', ``leads'', ``joined''\\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.3em}
  \keybox{\small No layer writes `` basketball'' itself - its best rank is 498 of 50{,}257 (layer 6).
  What the MLPs write on the name is \textbf{being an athlete}: opponents, defenders (layer 6);
  missed, scored, averaged (layers 10-11). The name gets \emph{enriched}; the word ``basketball''
  is produced later, at the end of the sentence.}
\end{frame}

    neuron & fires & push & its value pushes\\

  \vspace{0.3em}
  \watchbox{\small And the neurons whose values push `` basketball'' hardest \emph{anywhere} in the
  model are about sport in general - one pushes ``championships, tennis, tournaments'' and fires
  on Tiger Woods as much as on Jordan. The fact is spread over many neurons, each doing something
  broader: the superposition story from the features lecture, inside one MLP.}
\end{frame}

% ============================================================ SECTION 2
\section{Causal tracing}
\sectiontransition{Causal tracing}{Patching, with noise instead of a second sentence}

\begin{frame}{The recipe}
  \vspace{0.4em}
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Clean run.} ``Michael Jordan plays the sport of'' - save every activation.
    \item \textbf{Corrupted run.} Add random noise to the \emph{name's} embeddings (twice the
      typical embedding size - why twice comes later). The model loses the fact: P(`` basketball'') falls from
      \textbf{0.60} to \textbf{0.028}, averaged over the 13 known facts.
    \item \textbf{Restore one state.} Run corrupted again, but at one layer and one position copy
      back the clean value - of the whole stream, or only an MLP's output, or only attention's.
    \item \textbf{Measure} how much of the lost probability comes back.
  \end{enumerate}

  \vspace{0.5em}
  \keybox{\small It is the patching of \emph{Does it actually do that?} with one change: the
  ``other input'' is noise, so we do not need a second fact of the same length.
  {\footnotesize (Meng et al., 2022, who named it \emph{causal tracing}.)}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The picture}
  \vspace{-0.3em}
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{fig/facts_tracing.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vspace{-0.8em}
  {\footnotesize Each cell: share of the lost probability brought back by restoring that one state,
  averaged over 13 facts (rows: word positions; columns: layers).}

  \vspace{0.1em}
  {\small Three things stand out. The whole stream at the \textbf{last name token} carries up to a third of the fact, from layer 1 to 3. The MLP effect sits in the \textbf{very first layers} (0-1), not the middle. Attention matters only at the \textbf{last word}, and only late (layers 8-10).}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Two sites}
  \vspace{0.4em}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \textbf{Early site: MLPs, on the name}

      \vspace{0.3em}
      {\small Restoring the MLPs of layers 0-1 at the last name token brings back about \textbf{30\%} of the lost probability on its own; from layer 2 on, restoring MLPs does almost nothing (at most 4\%). In GPT-2 small the lookup happens at the very start - in larger models the same site sits in the middle layers.}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \textbf{Late site: attention, on the last word}

      \vspace{0.3em}
      {\small Restoring attention at ``of'' brings back \textbf{26\%, 33\% and 41\%} at layers 8, 9 and 10, and almost nothing before layer 7. The attribute is moved to where it is needed only near the end.}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}

  \vspace{0.8em}
  \keybox{\small The story this suggests: MLPs sitting on the subject \textbf{look up} what is
  known about it, and attention at the end \textbf{moves} the attribute the question asks for to
  where the prediction is made. It is the same shape as the Jordan/Brady patch - now split by
  component.}

  \vspace{0.3em}
  {\footnotesize Geva et al.\ (2023) study this ``enrich the subject, then extract the attribute''
  pattern in detail.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The noise is a choice}
  \vspace{0.3em}
  The original paper adds noise at \textbf{3 times} the typical embedding size. On GPT-2 small, the
  same experiment for Jordan at three noise sizes:

  \vspace{0.4em}
  \begin{center}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{@{}lcccc@{}}
    \toprule
    noise & P left & stream at ``Jordan'' (L1 / L4 / L8) & MLPs L0-2 & attention L9-11, ``of''\\
    \midrule
    1x & 0.202 & 85\% / 81\% / 63\% & 91\% & 67\%\\
    2x & 0.041 & 54\% / 59\% / 37\% & 70\% & 58\%\\
    3x & 0.010 & 39\% / 23\% / 21\% & 20\% & 36\%\\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.4em}
  \watchbox{\small At 3x the noise wrecks the whole sentence, and restoring any one place barely
  helps - the picture goes blank. At 1x the model still half-knows the answer. We used \textbf{2x}:
  the fact is still almost entirely gone, and restoring can show where it lives. That is a choice,
  made after looking, and this slide exists so that you know it was made.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Before you trust the picture}
  \vspace{0.5em}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{The noise is a choice} - the previous slide. Every localisation picture in this
      field rests on one like it.
    \item \textbf{Restoring one state is a strong intervention} on a model that has otherwise lost
      the fact. A large effect says that state is \emph{sufficient} there - not that it is the only
      place the fact exists.
    \item \textbf{A small, weak model.} GPT-2 small knows 13 of these 18 facts, and knows
      them at 45-76\%. Large models know far more, and the picture is cleaner there.
  \end{itemize}

  \vspace{0.6em}
  \watchbox{\small It is still the best localisation tool we have for facts. The next section
  tests whether localisation is good for anything practical.}
\end{frame}

% ============================================================ SECTION 3
\section{Editing a fact}
\sectiontransition{Editing a fact}{If the MLP is a memory, write to it}

\begin{frame}{The plan}
  \vspace{0.5em}
  Make GPT-2 believe \textbf{Michael Jordan plays tennis} - by changing one matrix, $W_{\text{out}}$
  of the MLP at layer 0 (the layer the tracing pointed at), and nothing else.

  \vspace{0.6em}
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{The key} $k^*$: what that MLP's neurons do on `` Jordan'' - its 3072 hidden
      activations, averaged over a few different openings of the sentence.
    \item \textbf{The value change} $\delta$: a 768-number vector that, added to the MLP's output on
      `` Jordan'', makes the model say `` tennis''. Found by gradient descent on $\delta$ alone -
      the model's weights are frozen.
    \item \textbf{The weight change}: alter $W_{\text{out}}$ so that $k^*$ now produces $\delta$
      extra - while every \emph{other} key changes as little as possible.
  \end{enumerate}

  \vspace{0.4em}
  {\footnotesize This is ROME, rank-one model editing (Meng et al., 2022), written from scratch
  for GPT-2 small in this repo.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Step 3, derived}
  \vspace{0.1em}
  Find the smallest change $\Delta W$ (3072 $\times$ 768) such that the key $k^*$ writes $\delta$
  more:
  \[
    \min_{\Delta W}\; \mathbb{E}_{k}\,\big\| k\, \Delta W \big\|^2
    \quad\text{subject to}\quad k^*\, \Delta W = \delta
  \]
  where $k$ ranges over keys from ordinary text. With $C = \mathbb{E}[k^{\top} k]$ (3072 $\times$
  3072, estimated on 8{,}192 tokens of Wikipedia text):
  \begin{align*}
    \mathbb{E}\|k\,\Delta W\|^2 &= \operatorname{tr}\big(\Delta W^{\top} C\, \Delta W\big)
      && \text{expand the square}\\
    2\,C\,\Delta W &= k^{*\top} \lambda && \text{set the Lagrangian's gradient to zero}\\
    \Delta W &= \tfrac{1}{2}\, C^{-1} k^{*\top} \lambda,\;\;
      \lambda = \frac{2\,\delta}{k^* C^{-1} k^{*\top}} && \text{solve, then plug into the constraint}\\
    \Delta W &= \frac{C^{-1} k^{*\top}\; \delta}{k^*\, C^{-1}\, k^{*\top}} && \text{an outer product: rank one}
  \end{align*}

  \vspace{0.1em}
  {\footnotesize Check: $k^*\,\Delta W = \delta$. \; $C^{-1}$ steers the change away from directions
  that ordinary keys often take - that is what protects the other facts.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The edit, graded}
  \vspace{-0.3em}
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.74\textwidth]{fig/facts_edit_report.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vspace{-0.7em}
  {\small P(`` tennis'') before and after the edit, on the prompt we edited, on 3
  paraphrases of it, and on ``\{name\} plays the sport of'' for the 12 other known
  athletes.}

  \vspace{0.2em}
  {\small On the edited prompt P(`` tennis'') goes from 2\% to \textbf{95\%} and `` basketball'' from 50\% to 0\%. All three paraphrases now favour tennis, but by very different amounts (96\%, 46\%, 7\%). And the edit \textbf{leaked}: on the 12 other athletes P(`` tennis'') rose from 11\% to 37\%, and only \textbf{7 of 12} still get their own sport.}
\end{frame}

\section{What localisation does not tell you}
\sectiontransition{What localisation does not tell you}{The tracing pointed at a layer. Is it the right one to edit?}

\begin{frame}{Predict first}
  \vspace{1.0em}
  Causal tracing said the MLP effect on the name peaks at \textbf{layer 0}.

  \vspace{1.2em}
  \begin{center}
    {\large If we run the same edit at \textbf{every} layer,\\[0.3em]
    is layer 0 the one where it works best?}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{1.2em}
  \begin{center}
    {\footnotesize ``Works best'' = the new sport also shows up in the paraphrases, not only on the
    one sentence we optimised.}
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The same edit, at every layer}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.52\textwidth}
      \vspace{-0.3em}
      \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig/facts_layer_sweep.pdf}

      {\footnotesize Red: mean P of the new sport on paraphrases. Two edits averaged
      (Jordan $\rightarrow$ tennis, Brady $\rightarrow$ golf).}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.46\textwidth}
      \vspace{0.3em}
  {\small The layer tracing pointed at, layer 0, is the \textbf{worst} place to edit: it reaches the paraphrases as well as any other layer, but wrecks the other athletes - only 20\% still right. Layers 1-10 work about as well and do far less damage (70-100\% still right); layer 11 does not work at all. Tracing strength and edit quality correlate only weakly over layers ($+0.39$).}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What the literature found}
  \vspace{0.6em}
  Hase, Bansal, Kim and Ghandeharioun (2023) asked the same question on a much larger model, over
  thousands of facts: does the layer causal tracing points at predict where editing works?

  \vspace{0.6em}
  \keybox{\small Their answer: the correlation between the two is \textbf{near zero} - for ROME and for
  several other editing methods. Editing works; the localisation just does not tell you where.}

  \vspace{0.6em}
  \trapbox{\small The lesson is bigger than editing. ``We found where X is stored'' and ``we can
  change X there'' are different claims. A picture that localises cleanly is still only an
  answer to the question it asked - here, ``where does restoring help?'' - and not to ``where
  should I intervene?''}
\end{frame}

% ============================================================ RECAP
\begin{frame}{Recap}
  \vspace{0.3em}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item An MLP is a \textbf{key-value memory}: each neuron recognises a pattern (key) and adds a
      vector (value). On `` Jordan'' the writes add up to ``basketball'' - spread over many neurons.
    \item \textbf{Causal tracing} (patching with noise) puts facts in two places: MLPs on the
      subject, then attention at the last word.
    \item A \textbf{rank-one edit} of one MLP matrix can rewrite a fact: Jordan now plays tennis - on the prompt, in paraphrases, and in free text.
    \item But the edited world is \textbf{inconsistent}, and the layer tracing points at is
      \textbf{not} reliably the best one to edit.
  \end{itemize}

  \vspace{0.4em}
  \takebox{\small Locating something and controlling it are different skills - and the second one
  has to be tested on its own.}
\end{frame}
