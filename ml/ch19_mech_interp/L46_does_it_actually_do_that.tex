% ============================================================ SECTION 1
\section{Ablation}
\sectiontransition{Ablation}{Delete a component. See what breaks.}

\begin{frame}{The oldest idea in neuroscience}
  \vspace{0.7em}
  Remove a part, observe what stops working, conclude the part was responsible.

  \vspace{0.8em}
  In a network the removal is easy and exact: take a head's output and \textbf{replace it}
  before it reaches the residual stream.

  \vspace{0.9em}
  \keybox{You already know this move. In chapter 5, \textbf{permutation importance} shuffled an
  input column and watched the score fall. This is the same logic, one layer deeper: the thing
  being knocked out is now \emph{inside} the model.}

  \vspace{0.9em}
  The only question is what to replace it \emph{with}. It sounds like a detail. It is not.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Predict first}
  \vspace{1.0em}
  We take the three heads that contribute most to the answer - the ones we will name later - and
  delete them.

  \vspace{1.3em}
  \begin{center}
    {\large The logit difference is $+3.60$. Where does it go?}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{1.3em}
  \begin{center}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{@{}ccc@{}}
    down to roughly zero & down a little & somewhere else\\
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{1.2em}
  \begin{center}
    {\footnotesize Hold your answer. We will get three different ones on the next few slides -
    and that is the point.}
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Three ways to delete a head}
  \vspace{0.5em}
  \begin{center}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{@{}p{0.20\textwidth}p{0.34\textwidth}p{0.34\textwidth}@{}}
    \toprule
    & \textbf{What you replace it with} & \textbf{What you are asking}\\
    \midrule
    \textbf{zero}-ablation & the number 0 & what if this head output nothing?\\[0.5em]
    \textbf{mean}-ablation & its average output over many sentences & what if this head ignored
      \emph{this} sentence?\\[0.5em]
    \textbf{resample}-ablation & its output on a \emph{different} sentence & what if this head
      were reading someone else's input?\\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.9em}
  These sound like three flavours of the same thing.

  \vspace{0.5em}
  \keybox{They are three \textbf{different questions}, and on our task they give three different
  answers - including different \emph{signs}.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why zero is not neutral}
  \vspace{0.7em}
  Zero feels like the obvious choice: switch the head off, set its contribution to nothing.

  \vspace{0.9em}
  \trapbox{But a head's output is \textbf{never} zero in normal operation. Setting it to zero
  does not give the model ``no information from this head'' - it gives the model a vector it has
  \textbf{never seen in training}.}

  \vspace{0.9em}
  The model then behaves strangely, and you record that strangeness as the head's importance. You
  have measured your own corruption.

  \vspace{0.8em}
  \begin{center}
  {\small An analogy: to find out whether a violinist matters to an orchestra, you can ask them
  to stop playing - or you can replace them with someone playing \emph{static}. Only one of those
  measures the violinist.}
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Same three heads, three answers}
  \vspace{0.1em}
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.66\textwidth]{fig/ablation_compare.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vspace{-0.5em}
  {\footnotesize Deleting L9H9, L9H6 and L10H0 - the three largest positive contributors.
  \textbf{Zero and mean say the model got better. Resample says it got worse.}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What to take from that}
  \vspace{0.8em}
  \begin{center}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{@{}lrr@{}}
    \toprule
    & \textbf{logit difference} & \textbf{change}\\
    \midrule
    baseline & $+3.60$ & --\\
    zero-ablation & $+3.91$ & \textcolor{paramgreen}{$+0.31$ better}\\
    mean-ablation & $+3.70$ & \textcolor{paramgreen}{$+0.11$ better}\\
    resample-ablation & $+3.34$ & \textcolor{armred}{$-0.25$ worse}\\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{1.0em}
  \watchbox{If a paper tells you ``we ablated the head and performance dropped'', your first
  question is \textbf{which kind of ablation}. The word alone does not identify the experiment.}

  \vspace{0.9em}
  And a second thing should be bothering you: we deleted the three most important heads in the
  model and it \textbf{did not get worse}. Park that. It is the last section of this lecture.
\end{frame}

% ============================================================ SECTION 2
\section{Activation patching}
\sectiontransition{Activation patching}{Instead of destroying information, move it}

\begin{frame}{The problem with deleting}
  \vspace{0.8em}
  Ablation answers ``what happens if this component is broken?''

  \vspace{0.6em}
  That is rarely the question we care about. We want to know \textbf{which piece of information}
  is being carried, and \textbf{where}.

  \vspace{1.0em}
  \keybox{So instead of destroying a component's output, \textbf{swap in the output it would have
  had on a different input}. Nothing is broken. One specific piece of information is changed, and
  everything else is left alone.}

  \vspace{0.9em}
  This is the single most used technique in the field. It has two names - \textbf{activation
  patching} and \textbf{causal tracing} - which mean the same thing.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Two sentences, one word apart}
  \vspace{0.5em}
  \textbf{Clean} - the model gets it right:
  \promptbox{``Then, Alex and William went to the restaurant. \textcolor{armblue}{\textbf{Alex}}
  gave a book to \ldots'' \quad$\rightarrow$\quad `` William'' \quad ($+3.52$)}

  \vspace{0.2em}
  \textbf{Corrupted} - the names are swapped, so the correct answer flips:
  \promptbox{``Then, \textcolor{armred}{\textbf{William}} and
  \textcolor{armred}{\textbf{Alex}} went to the restaurant. \textcolor{armred}{\textbf{William}}
  gave a book to \ldots'' \quad$\rightarrow$\quad `` Alex'' \quad ($-4.14$)}

  \vspace{0.5em}
  \keybox{Measured against the \emph{clean} answer, the model goes from $+3.52$ to $-4.14$. That
  gap of \textbf{7.66} is our working space: every patch we make recovers some fraction of it.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The move, step by step}
  \vspace{0.5em}
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item Run the \textbf{clean} sentence. Save every activation.
    \item Run the \textbf{corrupted} sentence. It gets the answer wrong.
    \item Run the corrupted sentence \textbf{again} - but at one chosen layer and one chosen
      position, overwrite the activation with the value saved in step 1.
    \item Measure the logit difference.
  \end{enumerate}

  \vspace{1.0em}
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tikzpicture}[font=\scriptsize]
    \draw[line width=6pt, color=armred!25] (0,0) -- (7.6,0);
    \node[font=\scriptsize, anchor=east] at (-0.15,0) {corrupted run};
    \fill[armblue] (3.4,-0.16) rectangle (3.9,0.16);
    \draw[-{Latex[length=2mm]}, armblue, thick] (3.65,0.95) -- (3.65,0.22);
    \node[font=\scriptsize, armblue, align=center] at (3.65,1.28) {one activation,\\copied from
      the clean run};
  \end{tikzpicture}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.7em}
  \keybox{If that single value is enough to bring the correct answer back, then \textbf{that
  layer, at that position, is where the information lives}.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{One patch, by hand}
  \vspace{0.7em}
  Patch the residual stream at \textbf{layer 1}, at the position of the second name:

  \vspace{0.7em}
  \begin{center}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{@{}lr@{}}
    \toprule
    corrupted run, no patch & $-4.14$\\
    corrupted run, one activation patched & $+3.14$\\
    \midrule
    recovered & $7.28$\\
    the full gap & $7.66$\\
    \midrule
    \textbf{fraction recovered} & $\mathbf{0.95}$\\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.9em}
  \takebox{\textbf{95\% of the model's correct behaviour, restored by copying one vector.}
  Everything else in the run is still corrupted - and it barely matters.}

  \vspace{0.6em}
  Now do that for every layer and every position, and put the answers in a grid.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The whole grid}
  \vspace{0.1em}
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.72\textwidth]{fig/patching_heatmap.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vspace{-0.5em}
  {\footnotesize Each cell: patch the residual stream at that layer and position, measure the
  fraction of the gap recovered. Dark red = the information is here.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Reading it}
  \vspace{0.6em}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Early layers, at the two name positions} - patching there recovers almost
      everything. Of course: fix the names early and the whole circuit runs correctly.
    \item \textbf{The signal then moves.} By layer 8 the name positions stop mattering.
    \item \textbf{Late layers, at the final position} - the last column lights up. The answer has
      been assembled and now lives where the prediction is made.
  \end{itemize}

  \vspace{0.9em}
  \keybox{Read left to right and the picture shows \textbf{information travelling}: from the
  names, into the middle of the network, and out at the final token.}

  \vspace{0.8em}
  \watchbox{Notice what this does \textbf{not} tell us: \emph{which heads} carried it. The grid
  is layers and positions, not components. That is the next section.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Two directions, two questions}
  \vspace{0.6em}
  We patched \textbf{clean into corrupted}. You can also go the other way.

  \vspace{0.7em}
  \begin{center}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{@{}p{0.2\textwidth}p{0.35\textwidth}p{0.35\textwidth}@{}}
    \toprule
    & \textbf{Denoising} & \textbf{Noising}\\
    \midrule
    Direction & clean value into a corrupted run & corrupted value into a clean run\\[0.5em]
    Asks & is this \textbf{enough} to fix it? & is this \textbf{necessary}?\\[0.5em]
    Finds & sufficient components & necessary components\\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.9em}
  \trapbox{These are \textbf{different questions} and they can give different answers. A
  redundant component is necessary-free but sufficient; a bottleneck is necessary but not
  sufficient alone. Papers routinely report one and describe it in the language of the other.}

  \vspace{0.5em}
  {\footnotesize The clearest treatment is Heimersheim \& Nanda, \emph{How to Use and Interpret
  Activation Patching}.}
\end{frame}

% ============================================================ SECTION 3
\section{Direct logit attribution}
\sectiontransition{Direct logit attribution}{The one measurement that needs no intervention}

\begin{frame}{We already built this}
  \vspace{0.7em}
  Last lecture we split the logit difference into one number per component, and checked that the
  numbers add up exactly:

  \vspace{0.5em}
  \[
    \text{logit difference} \;=\; c_{\text{embed}} + c_{\text{head 0.0}} + \cdots +
      c_{\text{head 11.11}} + \cdots
  \]

  \vspace{0.8em}
  Each $c$ is that component's \textbf{direct logit attribution}: how hard it pushed toward the
  correct name.

  \vspace{0.9em}
  \keybox{No ablation, no patching, no second forward pass. One run, and every component's
  contribution falls out of the arithmetic - because the residual stream is a sum.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Every head, ranked}
  \vspace{0.1em}
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.80\textwidth]{fig/dla_per_head.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vspace{-0.5em}
  {\footnotesize The 12 largest contributors and the 4 most negative, out of 144 heads. Blue
  pushes toward the correct name, red pushes away.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Two things in that chart are strange}
  \vspace{0.7em}
  \textbf{One.} The work is done by a \textbf{handful} of heads. L9H9 alone accounts for
  $+2.80$ of a $+3.60$ answer. Most of the 144 do essentially nothing.

  \vspace{0.9em}
  \textbf{Two.} Some heads have \textbf{large negative} attribution. L10H7 contributes
  $-2.00$ - it is actively, powerfully pushing toward the \emph{wrong} name.

  \vspace{1.0em}
  \keybox{A component whose job appears to be making the model worse is not a bug and not noise.
  Remember it - it is the key to the last section of this lecture.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Now we can settle the cold open}
  \vspace{0.6em}
  \begin{center}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{@{}lrrr@{}}
    \toprule
    \textbf{head} & \textbf{attention to the answer} & \textbf{direct logit attribution} &
      \textbf{effect of deleting it}\\
    \midrule
    L9H9 & 0.749 & $+2.805$ & $+0.471$\\
    L9H6 & 0.664 & $+1.053$ & $-0.522$\\
    \textbf{L9H8} & \textbf{0.318} & $\mathbf{-0.001}$ & $\mathbf{-0.034}$\\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{1.0em}
  \trapbox{\textbf{L9H8} attends to the correct answer from nearly every position in the
  sentence - the most visually striking ``this head is about William'' pattern of the three - and
  its contribution is $\mathbf{-0.001}$. Not small. \textbf{Nothing.}}

  \vspace{0.9em}
  \takebox{Attention says where a head \textbf{looked}. It says nothing about what it
  \textbf{wrote}, and nothing about whether anything downstream \textbf{read} it.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What direct attribution cannot see}
  \vspace{0.8em}
  Direct logit attribution measures the \textbf{direct} path from a component to the output. That
  word is load-bearing.

  \vspace{0.9em}
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tikzpicture}[font=\small]
    \node[draw, rounded corners, fill=armblue!12, minimum width=2.3cm, minimum height=0.8cm]
      (a) at (0,0) {head A};
    \node[draw, rounded corners, fill=armblue!12, minimum width=2.3cm, minimum height=0.8cm]
      (b) at (4.0,0) {head B};
    \node[draw, rounded corners, fill=paramgreen!15, minimum width=2.3cm, minimum height=0.8cm]
      (o) at (8.0,0) {the logits};
    \draw[-{Latex[length=2.5mm]}, thick] (a) -- (b);
    \draw[-{Latex[length=2.5mm]}, thick] (b) -- (o);
    \draw[-{Latex[length=2.5mm]}, armred, thick, dashed] (a) to[bend right=32] (o);
    \node[font=\scriptsize, armred] at (4.0,-1.5) {this is the only path attribution measures};
  \end{tikzpicture}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.9em}
  \trapbox{If head A's entire job is to \textbf{set up} head B, its direct attribution is
  \textbf{zero} - and it is essential. Every head in the first half of our chart could be
  invisible to this method.}

  \vspace{0.5em}
  That gap is exactly what the next section exists to close.
\end{frame}

% ============================================================ SECTION 4
\section{Searching at scale}
\sectiontransition{Searching at scale}{144 heads is small. What happens at 70 billion parameters?}

\begin{frame}{Path patching}
  \vspace{0.8em}
  Ordinary patching replaces a \textbf{component's} output, which changes everything downstream
  at once.

  \vspace{0.7em}
  \textbf{Path patching} replaces a component's contribution \textbf{along one route only} -
  head A's effect \emph{on head B}, leaving head A's other effects untouched.

  \vspace{1.0em}
  \keybox{This is what lets you say ``head A matters \emph{because of} head B'' rather than just
  ``head A matters''. It is how the indirect, set-up-work heads get found.}

  \vspace{0.8em}
  The cost: the number of paths grows much faster than the number of components.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The arithmetic that forces the next idea}
  \vspace{0.7em}
  Our patching grid was 12 layers $\times$ 16 positions $=$ \textbf{192 forward passes}. On a
  laptop, about three minutes.

  \vspace{0.8em}
  \begin{center}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{@{}lrr@{}}
    \toprule
    & \textbf{components} & \textbf{a full sweep}\\
    \midrule
    GPT-2 small & 144 heads & minutes\\
    a 70B model & thousands of heads, long prompts & \textcolor{armred}{wildly impractical}\\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.9em}
  Patching is exact and it does not scale. So the field did the obvious thing: approximate it.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Attribution patching}
  \vspace{0.8em}
  Take a first-order Taylor approximation of what patching would have done. The gradient tells
  you, to first order, how much the output would move if an activation changed.

  \vspace{0.9em}
  \keybox{\textbf{One backward pass estimates every patch at once.} Thousands of forward passes
  collapse into a single gradient computation - the same backpropagation from chapter 5, used to
  ask a different question.}

  \vspace{0.9em}
  \watchbox{It is an \textbf{approximation}, and it is worst exactly where the effect is large -
  which is where you were looking. Standard practice: screen with attribution patching, then
  confirm the survivors with real patching.}

  \vspace{0.5em}
  {\footnotesize Nanda (2023); systematically evaluated by Kram\'ar et al.\ (Google DeepMind).}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Letting the search run itself}
  \vspace{0.8em}
  \textbf{Automated Circuit Discovery (ACDC)} takes the loop we have been running by hand -
  patch a component, see whether the behaviour survives, drop it if it does - and automates it
  into a search over the whole network.

  \vspace{0.9em}
  Give it a task, a metric and a model; get back a subgraph.

  \vspace{0.9em}
  \watchbox{It recovers known circuits reasonably well and it is not magic: the results depend
  heavily on the metric you choose and the threshold you set. It narrows the search. It does not
  do the understanding.}

  \vspace{0.5em}
  {\footnotesize Conmy et al.\ (2023).}
\end{frame}

% ============================================================ SECTION 5
\section{The circuit, end to end}
\sectiontransition{The circuit, end to end}{Everything so far, assembled into one answer}

\begin{frame}{The task, one more time}
  \vspace{0.6em}
  \promptbox{``Then, Alex and William went to the restaurant. Alex gave a book to \ldots''}

  \vspace{0.3em}
  Called \textbf{Indirect Object Identification (IOI)}: two names appear, one repeats, and the
  answer is the one that did not.

  \vspace{0.9em}
  In 2022 a team at Redwood Research and Berkeley reverse-engineered how GPT-2 small does this -
  end to end, with the methods in this lecture.

  \vspace{0.8em}
  \keybox{Their answer: \textbf{26 attention heads}, in \textbf{7 classes}. Out of 144 heads
  total.}

  \vspace{0.5em}
  {\footnotesize Wang, Variengien, Conmy, Shlegeris \& Steinhardt (2022),
  \emph{Interpretability in the Wild}.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The algorithm, in plain words}
  \vspace{1.0em}
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armblue!8, colframe=armblue, boxrule=0.8pt, arc=3pt,
                    width=0.86\textwidth]
    \centering\large
    Find the name that appears \textbf{twice}.\\[0.5em]
    \textbf{Suppress} attention to it.\\[0.5em]
    \textbf{Copy} whichever name is left.
  \end{tcolorbox}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{1.1em}
  Three steps. Notice the middle one: the model does not find the right answer directly. It finds
  the \textbf{wrong} one and rules it out.

  \vspace{0.9em}
  \takebox{That is not how a person would describe the task, and it is not what you would have
  guessed. It is what the interventions showed.}
\end{frame}

\section{What goes wrong}
\sectiontransition{What goes wrong}{The result that should make you distrust everything above}

\begin{frame}{Predict first}
  \vspace{0.9em}
  We now know which three heads write the answer: \textbf{L9H9}, \textbf{L9H6}, \textbf{L10H0}.
  Their direct contributions add up to $+4.44$ of a $+3.58$ logit difference.

  \vspace{1.2em}
  \begin{center}
    {\large Delete all three. What is left?}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{1.2em}
  \begin{center}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{@{}ccc@{}}
    near zero & clearly negative & barely changed\\
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{1.0em}
  \begin{center}
    {\footnotesize Attribution says the answer should collapse. Commit before the next slide.}
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{It gets better}
  \vspace{0.9em}
  \begin{center}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{@{}lr@{}}
    \toprule
    baseline logit difference & $+3.580$\\
    what attribution predicts after deleting the three & $-0.858$\\
    \midrule
    \textbf{what actually happens} & $\mathbf{+3.754}$\\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{1.1em}
  \trapbox{We deleted the three heads that provably do the task, and the model got
  \textbf{slightly better at it}.}

  \vspace{1.0em}
  Every method in this lecture pointed at those three heads. Every method was right about what
  they contribute. And removing them changed almost nothing.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Where did $+4.6$ come from?}
  \vspace{0.1em}
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.86\textwidth]{fig/self_repair.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vspace{-0.5em}
  {\footnotesize Left: prediction versus reality. Right: the direct contribution of five other
  heads, before and after the deletion.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Two mechanisms, not one}
  \vspace{0.5em}
  The $+4.61$ of compensation comes from two different places, and they are not the same kind of
  thing:

  \vspace{0.6em}
  \begin{center}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{@{}p{0.30\textwidth}rp{0.42\textwidth}@{}}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Source} & \textbf{Amount} & \textbf{What happened}\\
    \midrule
    \textcolor{armred}{L10H7 stops suppressing} & $+2.06$ (45\%) &
      the negative name mover: $-2.05 \rightarrow +0.02$\\[0.5em]
    \textcolor{paramgreen}{backup heads step up} & $+1.85$ (40\%) &
      L10H10, L10H2, L10H6, L10H1 all increase\\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.8em}
  \keybox{L10H7's job is to \textbf{suppress whatever the name movers boost}. Remove the name
  movers and there is nothing left to suppress, so it goes quiet on its own.}

  \vspace{0.7em}
  \takebox{So the network did not ``repair'' itself. \textbf{A brake came off at the same moment
  the engine was removed.} Only the second row is genuine redundancy.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The consequence}
  \vspace{1.0em}
  \trapbox{\textbf{Ablation understates importance whenever a backup or a suppressor exists.} A
  component can be doing the work and measure as unimportant.}

  \vspace{1.0em}
  This is not a rare edge case. It happened on the first task we tried, in the most studied
  circuit in the field, with the three best-understood heads in the model.

  \vspace{1.0em}
  \keybox{Read every ``we ablated X and nothing happened'' with this in mind - including your
  own. Nothing happening is \textbf{not} evidence that X does nothing.}

  \vspace{0.6em}
  {\footnotesize Named the \textbf{Hydra effect} by McGrath et al.\ (2023): cut off one head and
  another grows.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{And it is not the only way to be fooled}
  \vspace{0.6em}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Subspace patching illusions.} Patching inside a chosen subspace can produce a
      large, clean, entirely misleading effect - the direction you picked need not be the one the
      model uses. (Makelov et al.)
    \item \textbf{Metric dependence.} Logit difference, accuracy and loss can rank the same
      components differently. The circuit you find depends on the number you chose to watch.
    \item \textbf{Template dependence.} Our whole analysis used one sentence shape. Whether it
      survives a different phrasing is an empirical question, not an assumption.
  \end{itemize}

  \vspace{0.8em}
  \watchbox{\textbf{Causal scrubbing} (Redwood) is the field's most serious attempt at a rigorous
  standard for ``is this explanation actually correct?'' - it replaces everything the hypothesis
  says is irrelevant and checks the behaviour survives. Nobody finds it fully satisfying, which
  tells you where the field is.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{One place where it all worked}
  \vspace{0.7em}
  There is a setting where a circuit was reverse-engineered \textbf{completely}: a small
  transformer trained to do modular addition. The mechanism turned out to be trigonometric -
  the model learned to represent numbers as angles and add them by rotation.

  \vspace{0.5em}
  Fourier structure in the weights, an exact algorithm, ablations that behave. Everything this
  lecture wished for.

  \vspace{0.9em}
  \watchbox{And the field has largely \textbf{retired} this kind of work. The author of that
  paper now lists toy-model interpretability as a direction to avoid: it is too clean to tell you
  anything about real models.}

  \vspace{0.7em}
  \keybox{Both things are true. It is the best way to \textbf{learn} what a circuit is, and it is
  not where the answers about real models are going to come from.}

  \vspace{0.4em}
  {\footnotesize Nanda et al.\ (2023). The full story is linked on the chapter page - worth an
  hour.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Recap}
  \vspace{0.4em}
  \begin{center}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{@{}p{0.26\textwidth}p{0.32\textwidth}p{0.32\textwidth}@{}}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Method} & \textbf{Answers} & \textbf{Fails when}\\
    \midrule
