\section{Gradients as explanations}
\sectiontransition{Gradients as explanations}{Which pixels would change the answer most?}

\begin{frame}{The idea: a saliency map}
  \vspace{0.5em}
  The network computes a score for the class, $f(x)$, from the pixels $x$. Ask the calculus
  question: if I nudge one pixel, how much does the score move?
  \[
    \text{saliency}(\text{pixel } i) \;=\; \left|\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}\right|
  \]

  \vspace{0.4em}
  One backward pass gives it for every pixel at once - it is backpropagation, stopped at the
  input instead of the weights.

  \vspace{0.6em}
  \keybox{\small \textbf{By hand, for a linear model} $f(x) = w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2 + b$: the gradient
  is just $(w_1, w_2)$. The saliency map of a linear model is its coefficients - the glass-box
  reading from chapter 5. For a deep network the gradient changes from input to input, which is
  the whole point, and the whole difficulty.}

  \vspace{0.2em}
  {\footnotesize Simonyan, Vedaldi \& Zisserman (2013).}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Three heatmaps of the same decision}
  \vspace{-0.3em}
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.78\textwidth]{fig/vision_saliency.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vspace{-0.8em}
  {\small \textbf{Gradient}: speckled - one pixel's gradient is very noisy. \textbf{SmoothGrad}:
  the gradient averaged over 16 noisy copies of the photo - smoother (Smilkov et al., 2017).
  \textbf{Integrated gradients}: the next slides.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The flaw in a plain gradient}
  \vspace{0.4em}
  Take $f(x_1, x_2) = \min(1,\; x_1 + x_2)$ and the input $(1, 1)$. Both inputs clearly matter:
  set both to 0 and the output falls from 1 to 0.

  \vspace{0.5em}
  But at $(1,1)$ the sum is 2, well past the cap of 1, so nudging either input changes nothing:
  \[
    \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_1} = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_2} = 0 .
  \]

  \vspace{0.3em}
  \trapbox{\small The gradient says neither input matters. The network is \textbf{saturated} -
  exactly the situation of a confident deep network, whose ReLUs and softmax flatten out once it
  is sure.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Integrated gradients, by hand}
  \vspace{0.2em}
  Instead of one gradient at the input, \textbf{average the gradient along the straight path} from a
  baseline $x'$ (here $(0,0)$) to the input $x$, and multiply by how far each input moved:
  \[
    \text{IG}_i \;=\; (x_i - x'_i)\int_0^1 \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}\big(x' + t\,(x - x')\big)\,dt
  \]

  \vspace{0.2em}
  On the path, $x_1 + x_2 = 2t$. For $t < 0.5$ the cap is not hit and $\partial f/\partial x_1 = 1$;
  for $t > 0.5$ it is 0:
  \[
    \text{IG}_1 = (1 - 0)\,\Big(\int_0^{0.5} 1\,dt + \int_{0.5}^{1} 0\,dt\Big) = 0.5,
    \qquad \text{IG}_2 = 0.5 .
  \]

  \vspace{0.2em}
  \keybox{\small $\text{IG}_1 + \text{IG}_2 = 1 = f(1,1) - f(0,0)$. That is not a coincidence: the
  attributions always add up to the change in the output (\textbf{completeness}). It is the same
  idea as Shapley values in chapter 5 - split the total change fairly between the inputs.}

  \vspace{0.1em}
  {\footnotesize Sundararajan, Taly \& Yan (2017).}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Completeness, checked on ResNet-18}
  \vspace{0.4em}
  The integral is approximated with a finite number of steps along the path. With more steps the
  sum of all 150{,}528 pixel attributions should approach $f(x) - f(\text{black image})$:

  \vspace{0.5em}
  \begin{center}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{@{}rrrr@{}}
    \toprule
    steps & sum of attributions & $f(x) - f(\text{black})$ & off by\\
    \midrule
    8 & 22.66 & 18.45 & $+23\%$\\
    32 & 16.86 & 18.45 & $-9\%$\\
    128 & 18.56 & 18.45 & $+0.6\%$\\
    512 & 18.455 & 18.450 & $+0.03\%$\\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  {\footnotesize The pomegranate photo, score of the class ``pomegranate''. The two-puppies photo
  behaves the same way ($+0.5\%$ at 512 steps).}

  \vspace{0.2em}
  \watchbox{\small The \textbf{baseline is a choice} with consequences. With a black baseline, a
  pixel that is already black has $x_i - x'_i = 0$ and gets \textbf{zero} attribution, however
  much the network relies on it. A different baseline gives a different map.}
\end{frame}

% ============================================================ SECTION 2
\section{Can a heatmap be trusted?}
\sectiontransition{Can a heatmap be trusted?}{Destroy the network and see if the explanation notices}

\begin{frame}{The sanity check}
  \vspace{0.5em}
  An explanation of a model's decision should \textbf{depend on the model}. So:

  \vspace{0.4em}
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item compute the heatmap on the trained network;
    \item replace the weights of the \textbf{top} layer with random numbers, and compute it again;
    \item randomise the next layer down too, and again - until nothing trained is left.
  \end{enumerate}

  \vspace{0.6em}
  \keybox{\small If the heatmap stays the same while the network turns to noise, it was never
  showing what the network learned. It was showing something about the \emph{photo} - usually its
  edges.}

  \vspace{0.3em}
  {\footnotesize Adebayo et al.\ (2018), \emph{Sanity Checks for Saliency Maps}.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Predict first}
  \vspace{1.0em}
  Three methods: the plain gradient, integrated gradients, and \textbf{guided backprop} - a popular
  method that produces especially crisp, photo-like maps (it lets only positive gradients flow
  back through each ReLU).

  \vspace{1.2em}
  \begin{center}
    {\large Randomise only the \textbf{top} of the network - the classifier\\[0.2em]
    and the last stage, the part that makes the decision.\\[0.6em]
    Which of the three maps will barely change?}
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The result}
  \vspace{-0.3em}
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.66\textwidth]{fig/vision_sanity.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vspace{-0.9em}
  {\small Pomegranate photo, network randomised from the top down (fc = the classifier). Under each map: rank
  correlation with the trained map (1 = same pixel ranking); red = still over 0.5.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What that means}
  \vspace{0.2em}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Guided backprop} barely notices: with the classifier and then the whole last
      stage random, its map still matches the trained one at \textbf{0.97} and \textbf{0.89}. It was
      drawing the photo's edges, not the decision.
    \item The \textbf{plain gradient} changes at once (0.50), and is gone when nothing trained is
      left.
    \item \textbf{Integrated gradients} changes too, but keeps 0.36 with nothing trained left: it
      multiplies by the pixel values, so part of every IG map is the photo itself.
  \end{itemize}

  \vspace{0.3em}
  \trapbox{\small A heatmap can look sharp, plausible and object-shaped and still depend on
  \textbf{nothing the model learned}. Crispness is not evidence. The only test is the one from the
  introduction: \textbf{intervene} - here, on the weights - and see whether the explanation moves.}

  \vspace{0.3em}
  And even the heatmaps that pass answer only ``\emph{where} in the input''. The rest of this
  lecture asks the question heatmaps cannot: \textbf{what is the network detecting}, and how is it
  built?
\end{frame}

% ============================================================ SECTION 3
\section{Feature visualisation}
\sectiontransition{Feature visualisation}{Ask a neuron what it wants to see}

\begin{frame}{The idea}
  \vspace{0.5em}
  Saliency fixes the image and asks about the pixels. Turn it around: fix a \textbf{neuron} (one
  channel of one layer) and \textbf{optimise the image} to make that channel fire as strongly as
  possible.

  \vspace{0.4em}
  \[
    x^{\star} = \arg\max_{x}\; \text{activation}_{\text{channel}}(x)
  \]

  \vspace{0.3em}
  Gradient \emph{ascent} on the pixels, with the network frozen. Whatever picture comes out is
  what that channel is looking for.

  \vspace{0.5em}
  \keybox{\small It is saliency run backwards. Saliency asks which pixels move the output; this asks
  which picture moves one \emph{internal} unit - the first question in this lecture about the
  inside of the network rather than its input.}

  \vspace{0.2em}
  {\footnotesize Erhan et al.\ (2009); Olah, Mordvintsev \& Schubert (2017), \emph{Feature
  Visualization}.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Naive optimisation gives noise}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.54\textwidth}
      \vspace{0.2em}
      Optimise every pixel freely and the result is busy high-frequency texture - an
      \textbf{adversarial pattern} that excites the channel but is not a picture of anything.

      \vspace{0.2em}
      {\footnotesize Right: the same channel (layer3, 43) both ways.}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.44\textwidth}
      \vspace{-0.4em}
      \includegraphics[width=0.86\linewidth]{fig/vision_featviz_naive.pdf}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}

  \vspace{-0.3em}
  The standard fixes, used for every picture from here on:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item optimise a \textbf{smooth} image: its Fourier coefficients, with high frequencies scaled
      down;
    \item at every step, \textbf{jitter, rescale and rotate} the image slightly - the picture has
      to excite the channel from every nearby viewpoint, which noise cannot do.
  \end{itemize}
  \watchbox{\small These are \textbf{priors}, chosen by us. A visualisation shows what excites the
  channel \emph{among smooth, shift-robust images} - not everything that excites it.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Four stages, twelve random channels}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.57\textwidth}
      \vspace{-0.5em}
      \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig/vision_featviz_grid.pdf}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.41\textwidth}
      \vspace{0.2em}
      {\small Three channels per stage, \textbf{picked at random} (seeded), not chosen for looking
      good.}

      \vspace{0.4em}
      {\small layer1: colour and fine stripes. layer2: repeated textures - swirls, bands. layer3:

      \vspace{0.1em}
      \keybox{\smallaggedright Going deeper, the pictures get bigger and more structured - stripes, then
      textures, then parts. The deepest ones are the hardest to read.}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\section{From neurons to circuits}
\sectiontransition{From neurons to circuits}{A curve detector, and what it is built from}

\begin{frame}{The curve detectors}
  \vspace{0.5em}
  The most studied neurons in any vision model are the \textbf{curve detectors} of InceptionV1:
  channels that fire on a curve of one orientation, not on straight lines and not on curves facing
  the other way. Their circuit was traced by hand, neuron by neuron.

  \vspace{0.6em}
  \keybox{\small The universality claim from the introduction predicts that other networks trained
  on images grow curve detectors too. ResNet-18 was never studied that way. Can we find one in it
  ourselves?}

  \vspace{0.4em}
  {\footnotesize Cammarata et al.\ (2020), \emph{Curve Detectors}; Olah et al.\ (2020), \emph{Zoom
  In}.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The hunt}
  \vspace{0.4em}
  Show every channel of layer1 and layer2 two sets of synthetic images, 16 orientations each:

  \vspace{0.4em}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item a \textbf{quarter-circle arc} through the centre, facing each direction;
    \item a \textbf{straight line} through the centre, at each angle.
  \end{itemize}

  \vspace{0.4em}
  Call a channel \textbf{curve-like} if its best arc response is at least twice its best line
  response, it prefers some orientations clearly over others, and it responds strongly (top
  quarter of the stage).

  \vspace{0.4em}
  \begin{center}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{@{}lrrl@{}}
    \toprule
    stage & channels & curve-like & best one: arc vs line\\
    \midrule
    layer1 & 64 & 1 & channel 12: 5.27 vs 2.54\\
    layer2 & 128 & 6 & channel 23: 4.42 vs 1.15\\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.2em}
  {\small layer1's one candidate barely passes (2.1 times). layer2 has several clear ones.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The best candidate}
  \vspace{-0.3em}
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.94\textwidth]{fig/vision_curve.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vspace{-0.4em}
  {\small layer2, channel 23. Its best arc gives \textbf{4.42}, its best straight line
  \textbf{1.15} - almost four times less. And it is tuned: arcs facing one way (around 45$^\circ$)
  excite it, arcs facing the opposite way (180-270$^\circ$) barely do. Its feature visualisation is a
  stack of arcs, all bending the same way.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What is it built from?}
  \vspace{-0.2em}
  Show the curve detector its preferred arc, then \textbf{switch off one layer1 channel at a time}
  and measure how much its response falls.
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.86\textwidth]{fig/vision_circuit.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vspace{-0.6em}
  {\small Top: the five layer1 channels it depends on most, with their feature visualisations.
  Bottom: their responses to straight lines (grey) and arcs (red), by orientation.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Edges lying along the curve}
  \vspace{0.3em}
  \textbf{No single input carries it.} Removing any one layer1 channel costs at most 11\%; it takes
  8 of the 64 to remove half the response.

  \vspace{0.5em}
  \textbf{But the inputs are not random.} All top five are \textbf{edge detectors} - they swing
  strongly with the angle of a line. Which angles?

  \vspace{0.4em}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item The preferred arc faces 45$^\circ$. Along it, the direction of the curve turns from
      90$^\circ$ to 180$^\circ$ (its tangents).
    \item Four of the five top inputs prefer lines at \textbf{135$^\circ$ or 157.5$^\circ$} - inside
      that range. The fifth prefers 67.5$^\circ$, outside it.
  \end{itemize}

  \vspace{0.4em}
  \keybox{\small A curve assembled from small edges lying along it - the same recipe found by hand
  in InceptionV1, here spread over more inputs, with none worth more than 11\%.}
\end{frame}

  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.56\textwidth}
      \vspace{-0.4em}
      \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig/vision_polysemantic.pdf}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.42\textwidth}
      {\small Last-layer channels whose two strongest class votes (read straight off the final

  \keybox{\small Some units mean one thing, others several. A network with more concepts than
  channels has to share channels between concepts - \textbf{superposition}, which comes back in
  the features lecture.}
\end{frame}

% ============================================================ RECAP
\begin{frame}{Recap}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Saliency} = the gradient of the class score with respect to the pixels. Noisy;
      SmoothGrad averages the noise away.
    \item \textbf{Integrated gradients} averages the gradient along a path from a baseline, and its
      attributions add up to the change in the output - checked on ResNet-18.
    \item \textbf{Sanity checks}: guided backprop ignored a randomised top of the network (0.97);
      a crisp map is not evidence. Integrated gradients keeps part of the photo in every map.
    \item \textbf{Feature visualisation} asks a channel what it wants to see; the answers grow from
      edges to parts with depth, under priors we chose.
    \item \textbf{Circuits}: a curve detector in ResNet-18 (layer2, channel 23), built from edge
      detectors lying along the arc - but spread over many inputs, none worth more than 11\%.
  \end{itemize}

  \vspace{0.1em}
  \takebox{\small Heatmaps say \emph{where}. Neurons and circuits say \emph{what} and \emph{how} -
  and every claim still has to survive an intervention.}
\end{frame}
