\begin{frame}{Twenty lectures of black boxes}
  \vspace{0.5em}
  In chapter 5 we learned to explain a model without ever looking inside it.

  \vspace{0.8em}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item SHAP splits the prediction between the input features.
    \item Permutation importance shuffles a column and watches the score fall.
    \item Partial dependence sweeps one feature and plots the response.
  \end{itemize}

  \vspace{0.9em}
  \keybox{Every one of those methods treats the model as a \textbf{function you can only query}.
  Feed it inputs, read the outputs, infer what mattered. None of them ever reads a weight.}

  \vspace{0.7em}
  That was not a limitation of the methods. It was a decision - and for a gradient-boosted tree
  it is still the right one. In this chapter we make the other decision.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Predict first}
  \vspace{0.6em}
  We have complete access to GPT-2 small. Every one of its 124 million numbers is sitting on the
  disk in front of us, and we can read all of them.

  \vspace{1.1em}
  \begin{center}
    {\large Is knowing every weight the same as understanding the model?}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{1.1em}
  \pause
  No - and the reason is worth stating precisely.

  \vspace{0.5em}
  \trapbox{We have the weights the way you have a wiring diagram of a city's power grid: complete,
  correct, and no help at all in answering \emph{``why did that street go dark?''}
  \textbf{Access is not understanding.} The gap between them is the entire field.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What mechanistic interpretability is}
  \vspace{1.0em}
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armblue!8, colframe=armblue, boxrule=0.8pt, arc=3pt, width=0.9\textwidth]
    \centering\large
    Using a model's \textbf{internals} - its weights and activations -\\ to understand what it
    is doing.
  \end{tcolorbox}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{1.0em}
  Note what that definition does \textbf{not} say. It does not promise a complete
  reverse-engineering of the network, and the field has become deliberately pragmatic about
  this: the goal is useful understanding, not a full circuit diagram of a trillion-parameter
  model.

  \vspace{0.9em}
  \takebox{\textbf{The through-line of this chapter:} it is easy to form a story about what is
  happening inside a network, and hard to show that the story is true. Most of what we teach is
  how to tell those two apart.}
\end{frame}

\section{Why look inside?}
\sectiontransition{Why look inside?}{Four reasons, each with a case you can point to}

\begin{frame}{Reason 1 - debugging: right answer, wrong reason}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.5\textwidth}
      \vspace{0.3em}
      You met this in chapter 5. A classifier separates huskies from wolves, and LIME shows what
      it looks at: \textbf{the snow}.

      \vspace{0.8em}
      A black-box method got us that far, and it is a real result. It stops there:

      \vspace{0.3em}
      \begin{itemize}\small
        \item \emph{where} in the network does ``snow'' live?
        \item can we remove it without breaking the rest?
        \item what else was quietly relying on it?
      \end{itemize}

      \vspace{0.5em}
      {\small Those are questions about the \textbf{mechanism}, and only the inside can answer
      them.}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.47\textwidth}
      \begin{center}
        \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig/lime_husky.png}\\[-2pt]
        {\scriptsize Husky vs.\ wolf: LIME highlights snow pixels. LMU IML (CC BY 4.0) /
        Ribeiro et al.\ (2016).}
      \end{center}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Reason 2 - trust: does the explanation match the computation?}
  \vspace{0.6em}
  Every chat model you use can print its reasoning step by step. The natural assumption is that
  the text is a \textbf{transcript} of what the model computed.

  \vspace{0.8em}
  \trapbox{That assumption is testable, and it is sometimes false. In the features lecture we
  look at a traced case where the model's written reasoning describes a calculation the model did
  \textbf{not} do.}

  \vspace{0.8em}
  The stakes grow with what the model is allowed to do. In the agents lecture a model ran tools
  on its own; an agent whose reasoning you cannot check is an agent you are trusting on its word.

  \vspace{0.5em}
  {\footnotesize Lindsey et al.\ (2025), \emph{On the Biology of a Large Language Model}.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Reason 3 - science: what did it learn that nobody taught it?}
  \vspace{0.6em}
  \textbf{A board it was never shown.} A small transformer trained only on lists of legal Othello
  moves - never a picture of the board - turns out to keep an internal copy of the board anyway.
  You can read the state of every square out of its activations.
  {\footnotesize (Li et al., 2022)}

  \vspace{0.9em}
  \textbf{A plan it never wrote down.} Asked to write a rhyming couplet, a large model picks the
  rhyme word \emph{before} it starts writing the second line, and then writes a line that lands on
  it. {\footnotesize (Lindsey et al., 2025)}

  \vspace{1.0em}
  \keybox{Neither fact is visible in the output. Both were found by looking inside. The model
  learned things its training data only implied, and the only way to find out what is to look.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Reason 4 - control: if you can find it, you can turn it}
  \vspace{0.6em}
  In 2024 Anthropic found a direction inside Claude that switched on for the Golden Gate Bridge -
  in text, in photos, in any language.

  \vspace{0.7em}
  Then they \textbf{held it switched on} while the model talked. The result, ``Golden Gate
  Claude'', brought every conversation around to the bridge, and when asked what it was,
  answered that it \emph{was} the bridge.

  \vspace{0.9em}
  \keybox{It is a joke, and it is also evidence: changing one internal direction changed the model's
  behaviour in exactly the predicted way. Finding the part and being able to turn it are the same
  skill.}

  \vspace{0.4em}
  {\footnotesize Templeton et al.\ (2024), \emph{Scaling Monosemanticity}. We return to this -
  and to its limits - in the features lecture.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What this does not replace}
  \vspace{0.4em}
  \begin{center}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{@{}p{0.15\textwidth}p{0.36\textwidth}p{0.36\textwidth}@{}}
    \toprule
    & \textbf{Black box} (chapter 5) & \textbf{White box} (this chapter)\\
    \midrule
    Works on & \textbf{any} model - boosted trees, a support vector machine, an API you can
      only call & neural networks whose \textbf{weights you have}\\[0.25em]
    Needs & predictions & weights, activations, and a framework to hook them\\[0.25em]
    Answers & which \textbf{input} moved the output & what the \textbf{model did}\\[0.25em]
    Cost & seconds & the circuit in the causal-methods lecture took a research team months\\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.2em}
  \watchbox{Most of you will deploy gradient boosting. For that model SHAP is not the weaker
  tool - it is the \textbf{only} tool. This chapter is not an upgrade over chapter 5. It is a
  different instrument, for a different object.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The axes you already know}
  \vspace{0.1em}
  Chapter 5 sorted every method along a few questions:

  \vspace{0.2em}
  \begin{center}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{@{}p{0.28\textwidth}p{0.3\textwidth}p{0.3\textwidth}@{}}

    \textbf{Question} & \textbf{One answer} & \textbf{The other}\\

    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.3em}
  Mechanistic interpretability is \textbf{post hoc} and \textbf{model-specific}, and it works at
  both scales: one prompt, or a feature across all prompts.

  \vspace{0.2em}
  \keybox{What is genuinely new is a fourth question: \textbf{what is the explanation made of?}
  Chapter 5's answers are made of \emph{inputs}. This chapter's are made of \emph{parts of the
  model}.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The map}
  \vspace{-0.4em}
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.86\textwidth]{fig/intro_method_map.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vspace{-0.7em}
  {\footnotesize *You have done mechanistic interpretability before: reading a linear model's
  coefficients \emph{is} reading its weights. It is easy there because the model has one layer.
  This chapter is about what that habit costs on a model with twelve.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why not just use a glass box?}
  \vspace{0.6em}
  Chapter 5's first lecture made the case for models that are readable by design: a shallow tree,
  a sparse linear model, a rule list. When one of those is accurate enough, use it.

  \vspace{0.8em}
  For this chapter's models it is not an option:

  \vspace{0.3em}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item there is no glass-box model that writes a paragraph, translates, or answers questions;
    \item the models people actually deploy for language and vision are the ones we understand
      least.
  \end{itemize}

  \vspace{0.8em}
  \takebox{So the choice is not ``interpretable model or accurate model''. The model is already
  chosen, it is a neural network, and the only question left is how much of it we can read.}
\end{frame}

\section{A network small enough to read}
\sectiontransition{A network small enough to read}{Before GPT-2: the whole method, on a network with four neurons}

\begin{frame}{The task}
  \vspace{-0.2em}
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.74\textwidth]{fig/intro_toy_task.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vspace{-0.6em}
  {\small Is the point $(x, y)$ inside the diamond $|x| + |y| \le 1$? A network with \textbf{2
  inputs, 4 hidden ReLU units, 1 output}, trained here on a laptop in under a minute. It gets
  \textbf{99.8\%} of the plane right.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Predict first}
  \vspace{1.0em}
  Four hidden units. A diamond has four edges.

  \vspace{1.2em}
  \begin{center}
    {\large What do you think each hidden unit does?}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{1.2em}
  \pause
  Recall what one ReLU unit computes: $h = \max(0,\; w_1 x + w_2 y + b)$. It is \textbf{zero}
  on one side of the line $w_1 x + w_2 y + b = 0$ and grows linearly on the other.

  \vspace{0.6em}
  \keybox{So each unit is a \textbf{line} with an ``on'' side. Four units, four lines. Where would
  you put them?}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Each unit, over the whole plane}
  \vspace{-0.1em}
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig/intro_toy_units.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vspace{-0.4em}
  Darker blue means the unit fires harder. Each unit is \textbf{silent inside the diamond} and
  switches on only once you cross one particular edge.

  \vspace{0.5em}
  \keybox{We just named all four neurons: ``outside the top-right edge'', ``outside the top-left
  edge'', and so on. Each one means one thing, and we can say what.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Reading it off the weights}
  \vspace{0.2em}
  The same names, without drawing anything - just the numbers the network learned:

  \vspace{0.4em}
  \begin{center}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{@{}lrrrcr@{}}
    \toprule
    & $w_1$ & $w_2$ & $b$ & fires when & \textbf{output weight}\\
    \midrule
    unit 1 & $10.13$ & $10.17$ & $-9.59$ & $x + y > 0.94$ & $-9.24$\\
    unit 2 & $-8.62$ & $8.44$ & $-7.97$ & $-x + y > 0.93$ & $-10.44$\\
    unit 3 & $-9.64$ & $-9.77$ & $-8.87$ & $-x - y > 0.91$ & $-7.12$\\
    unit 4 & $8.75$ & $-8.81$ & $-8.12$ & $x - y > 0.92$ & $-9.06$\\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.3em}
  \[
    \text{output} \;=\; 6.07 \;-\; 9.24\,h_1 \;-\; 10.44\,h_2 \;-\; 7.12\,h_3 \;-\; 9.06\,h_4
    \qquad(\text{inside if } > 0)
  \]

  \vspace{0.2em}
  {\small Each ``fires when'' column is the unit's own inequality divided by its weight - e.g.\
  $10.13x + 10.17y > 9.59 \;\Rightarrow\; x + y \gtrsim 0.94$, the diamond's edge $x + y = 1$ moved
  slightly inward.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Two points, by hand}
  \vspace{0.3em}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \textbf{$(0.5,\; 0.25)$ - inside}

      \vspace{0.4em}
      {\small
      unit 1: $10.13(0.5) + 10.17(0.25) - 9.59 = -1.98$\\
      units 2, 3, 4: $-10.17,\; -16.13,\; -5.95$\\[0.4em]
      all negative, so after ReLU: $h = (0, 0, 0, 0)$\\[0.4em]
      output $= 6.07 - 0 = \mathbf{+6.07}$ \hfill \textcolor{paramgreen}{\textbf{inside}}}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \textbf{$(1,\; 0.5)$ - outside}

      \vspace{0.4em}
      {\small
      unit 1: $10.13(1) + 10.17(0.5) - 9.59 = +5.63$\\
      units 2, 3, 4: $-12.37,\; -23.39,\; -3.77$\\[0.4em]
      after ReLU: $h = (5.63, 0, 0, 0)$\\[0.4em]
      output $= 6.07 - 9.24 \times 5.63 = \mathbf{-45.9}$ \hfill
      \textcolor{armred}{\textbf{outside}}}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}

  \vspace{1.0em}
  \keybox{Inside the diamond every unit is silent, and the output is just the bias, $+6.07$. Step
  past an edge and that edge's unit fires, and its large negative weight drags the output below
  zero.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The circuit, in one sentence}
  \vspace{1.0em}
  \begin{center}
    {\Large \emph{``If you are past any edge, you are out.''}}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{1.1em}
  That is the whole algorithm, and we got it without guessing: four \textbf{features} (one edge
  each), combined by the output unit into a \textbf{circuit} (an AND of four ``not past this
  edge'' conditions).

  \vspace{0.9em}
  \takebox{Those two words - \textbf{feature} and \textbf{circuit} - are the whole vocabulary of
  this chapter. A feature is something the network represents. A circuit is features wired
  together to compute something.}

  \vspace{0.6em}
  {\small But so far we have only \emph{looked}. The reading could still be wrong.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Now test it}
  \vspace{-0.1em}
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig/intro_toy_ablation.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vspace{-0.4em}
  {\small Delete one unit (set it to zero). Any one deletion opens up \textbf{exactly its edge}:
  about \textbf{15.5\%} of the plane becomes ``inside'', and only \textbf{0.1\%} of the true diamond

  \vspace{0.2em}
  \keybox{The reading \textbf{predicted} this before we ran it. That is the difference between a

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Train it again}
  \vspace{-0.3em}
  \begin{center}

  \end{center}
  \vspace{-0.6em}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The stuck network is readable too}
  \vspace{-0.3em}
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.66\textwidth]{fig/intro_toy_stuck.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vspace{-0.6em}
  {\small Seed 509: three of its four units \textbf{never fire} anywhere in the plane - dead ReLUs,

  \vspace{0.3em}
  \trapbox{\small Its accuracy is \textbf{87.3\%} - exactly the share of the plane that is outside

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why this does not scale}
  \vspace{0.4em}
  \begin{center}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{@{}p{0.3\textwidth}p{0.2\textwidth}p{0.42\textwidth}@{}}
    \toprule
    & \textbf{our toy} & \textbf{GPT-2 small}\\
    \midrule
    hidden units & 4 & 36{,}864 MLP neurons, plus 144 attention heads\\[0.3em]
    inputs per unit & 2 - you can plot them & 768 - you cannot\\[0.3em]
    does each unit mean one thing? & yes & mostly \textbf{no}\\[0.3em]
    can you check a reading? & delete a unit, look & delete a unit - and the model may
      compensate\\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.8em}
  \watchbox{Every hard problem in this field is in that table. We cannot draw the units, so we need
  other ways to read them. Units mean several things at once, so the neuron may be the wrong thing
  to read. And deleting a part does not always reveal what it did. Each of those gets a lecture.}
\end{frame}

% ============================================================ SECTION 4
\section{Features, circuits, universality}
\sectiontransition{Features, circuits, universality}{The three claims the field is built on}

\begin{frame}{Three claims}
  \vspace{0.4em}
  In 2020 a group at OpenAI wrote down the working assumptions of the field as three claims -
  deliberately labelled \emph{speculative}:

  \vspace{0.6em}
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Features.} Networks represent meaningful things, and those representations can be
      found and understood.\\
      {\small\color{gray} Our toy: four edges.}
    \item \textbf{Circuits.} Features are connected by weights into small, readable algorithms.\\
      {\small\color{gray} Our toy: ``past any edge, you are out''.}
    \item \textbf{Universality.} Similar features and circuits show up in different networks
      trained on similar tasks.\\
      {\small\color{gray} Our toy: 4 of 8 runs found the same four edges.}
  \end{enumerate}

  \vspace{0.6em}
  {\footnotesize Olah et al.\ (2020), \emph{Zoom In: An Introduction to Circuits}.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{A feature is a direction}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.5\textwidth}
      \vspace{-0.4em}
      \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig/intro_feature_direction.pdf}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.47\textwidth}
      \vspace{0.3em}
      GPT-2 small stores each token as 768 numbers. Take 16 male/female word pairs and average the
      16 ``female minus male'' differences into one direction.

      \vspace{0.6em}
      Every pair moves the same way along it.

      \vspace{0.6em}
      {\small \textbf{Not circular:} rebuild the direction \emph{without} a pair, and that pair
      still moves the right way - \textbf{16 of 16}. Each pair's difference has cosine
      \textbf{0.60} with the shared direction; a random direction gets \textbf{0.03}.}

      \vspace{0.6em}
      \keybox{\small Nobody told the model about gender. It found a direction for it.}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Arithmetic with directions}
  \vspace{0.5em}
  If meanings are directions, you can add and subtract them. Nearest tokens to the result, in
  GPT-2 small's own embeddings:

  \vspace{0.6em}
  \begin{center}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
    \toprule
    \textbf{sum} & \textbf{nearest token} & \textbf{cosine}\\
    \midrule
    king $-$ man $+$ woman & \textbf{queen} & 0.71\\
    walked $-$ walk $+$ run & \textbf{ran} & 0.74\\
    cats $-$ cat $+$ dog & \textbf{dogs} & 0.85\\
    bigger $-$ big $+$ small & \textbf{smaller} & 0.80\\
    \midrule
    Paris $-$ France $+$ Italy & \textcolor{armred}{Paris} (again), then Italy & 0.63\\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.6em}
  \watchbox{\small The last row is kept on purpose. ``Rome'' is fifth. Directions are real, but
  they are not magic, and a slide that only shows the analogies that worked is the first
  cherry-pick of the chapter.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{A circuit: features wired together}
  \vspace{0.6em}
  In the toy, the circuit was four edge-features feeding one output. In a real language model the
  first circuit you will meet is the one behind the middle panel of the opening slide - copying a
  pattern it has never seen:

  \vspace{0.8em}
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tikzpicture}[font=\small]
    \node[draw, rounded corners, fill=armblue!10, minimum width=4.0cm, minimum height=1.1cm,
          align=center] (a) at (0,0) {\textbf{head A}\\[0.1em]\scriptsize ``the token before me was X''};
    \node[draw, rounded corners, fill=paramgreen!10, minimum width=4.0cm, minimum height=1.1cm,
          align=center] (b) at (7.2,0) {\textbf{head B}\\[0.1em]\scriptsize ``find where X was, copy what came next''};
    \draw[-{Latex[length=2.5mm]}, thick] (a) -- (b);
  \end{tikzpicture}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.8em}
  Neither head does the task alone. The behaviour lives in the \textbf{connection}. How it works is
  in the next core lecture; how to read it straight off the weights is in an optional one.

  \vspace{0.4em}
  {\footnotesize Olsson et al.\ (2022), \emph{In-context Learning and Induction Heads}.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Universality}
  \vspace{0.6em}
  Why should a lecture on GPT-2 small tell you anything about any other model? Because of the third
  claim.

  \vspace{0.7em}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Edge and curve detectors} have been reported in many different image networks,
      of different architectures, trained separately.
    \item \textbf{The copying circuit} from the last slide turned up in every model size the
      original study checked, from one-layer toys to large models, and in many models since.
    \item Our toy, in miniature: different runs, same four edges.
  \end{itemize}

  \vspace{0.8em}
  \watchbox{Universality is an \textbf{empirical} claim, and only partly true - our toy found the
  clean solution in half its runs, not all. But it is the reason studying small models is worth
  anything, and the reason the field bothers to name what it finds.}

  \vspace{0.3em}
  {\footnotesize Olah et al.\ (2020); Olsson et al.\ (2022).}
\end{frame}

% ============================================================ SECTION 5
\section{How the field works}
\sectiontransition{How the field works}{Look, guess, intervene - and believe only the last one}

\begin{frame}{The loop}
  \vspace{0.3em}
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tikzpicture}[font=\small, node distance=1.0cm]
    \node[draw, rounded corners, fill=lightbg, minimum width=3.4cm, minimum height=1.0cm,
          align=center] (look) at (0, 1.6) {\textbf{look}\\\scriptsize logit lens, probes,\\[-0.2em]
          \scriptsize attention patterns, top examples};
    \node[draw, rounded corners, fill=armblue!10, minimum width=3.4cm, minimum height=1.0cm,
          align=center] (hyp) at (6.2, 1.6) {\textbf{hypothesis}\\\scriptsize ``this head copies
          the name''};
    \node[draw, rounded corners, fill=armorange!15, minimum width=3.4cm, minimum height=1.0cm,
          align=center] (int) at (6.2, -0.9) {\textbf{intervene}\\\scriptsize delete it, swap it,
          push it};
    \node[draw, rounded corners, fill=paramgreen!12, minimum width=3.4cm, minimum height=1.0cm,
          align=center] (ev) at (0, -0.9) {\textbf{evidence}\\\scriptsize did the behaviour change
          the way\\[-0.2em]\scriptsize the hypothesis said?};
    \draw[-{Latex[length=2.5mm]}, thick] (look) -- (hyp);
    \draw[-{Latex[length=2.5mm]}, thick] (hyp) -- (int);
    \draw[-{Latex[length=2.5mm]}, thick] (int) -- (ev);
    \draw[-{Latex[length=2.5mm]}, thick, dashed] (ev) -- (look);
  \end{tikzpicture}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.6em}
  \keybox{The toy network went round this loop once: we \emph{looked} at the units, guessed
  ``each is an edge'', \emph{deleted} one, and the edge opened. The rest of the chapter is the
  same loop on models where every step is harder.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What each kind of evidence proves}
  \vspace{0.5em}
  \begin{center}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{@{}p{0.46\textwidth}p{0.44\textwidth}@{}}
    \toprule
    \textbf{What we see} & \textbf{What it proves}\\
    \midrule
    a head attends to the right word & \textbf{nothing yet} - it may write nothing\\[0.3em]
    a probe reads the information out & it is \textbf{present}, not that it is \textbf{used}\\[0.3em]
    a neuron's top examples share a theme & maybe - or a \textbf{cherry-pick}\\[0.3em]
    deleting a part changes nothing & \textbf{not} that the part is useless - something may cover
      for it\\[0.3em]
    copying a part's value into another run moves the answer & the information \textbf{is} there,
      and it matters\\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.7em}
  \takebox{This table is the chapter. Each row gets measured on a real model before we are done,
  including the fourth, which is the strangest result we will see.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Two analogies, and where they break}
  \vspace{0.3em}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \textbf{Reverse-engineering a program}

      \vspace{0.3em}
      {\small The weights are a compiled binary: complete and executable, but unreadable. We want
      the source code - variables (features) and functions (circuits).}

      \vspace{0.4em}
      {\small\color{armred} Where it breaks: nobody wrote this program, so there may be no clean
      source code to recover.}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \textbf{Neuroscience, with superpowers}

      \vspace{0.3em}
      {\small A neuroscientist would give anything to record every neuron at once, change any of
      them precisely, and run a thousand identical copies of the same brain. We can do all three.}

      \vspace{0.4em}
      {\small\color{armred} Where it breaks: a brain is one fixed object; we can retrain a
      network and get a different one.}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}

  \vspace{1.0em}
  \keybox{Both analogies say the same thing: the problem is not \textbf{access}, it is
  \textbf{translation} - from numbers into concepts a person can check.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why a small, old model}
  \vspace{0.4em}
  The language lectures run on GPT-2 small: \textbf{12 layers}, \textbf{12 heads per layer},
  \textbf{144 heads} in total, and a residual stream of width \textbf{768}. It is seven years old.

  \vspace{0.8em}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \textbf{Why it is the right choice}
      \begin{itemize}\small
        \item every interpretability tool targets it;
        \item it is where the field's best-documented circuit was found;
        \item it runs on a laptop, so \textbf{every figure in these lectures is one you can
              reproduce}.
      \end{itemize}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \textbf{What transfers, and what does not}
      \begin{itemize}\small
        \item[\textcolor{paramgreen}{\ding{51}}] the residual-stream picture;
        \item[\textcolor{paramgreen}{\ding{51}}] the causal methods, all of them;
        \item[\textcolor{paramgreen}{\ding{51}}] the standards of evidence;
        \item[\textcolor{armred}{\ding{55}}] the \textbf{specific circuits}. What we find is a
              fact about GPT-2, not about every model.
      \end{itemize}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}

  \vspace{0.4em}
  \watchbox{\small The field has largely \textbf{retired} toy-model interpretability as a research
  direction, for the reason in the right-hand column. It is still the only sane place to
  \emph{learn} it. Training ground, not frontier.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What you will be able to do}
  \vspace{0.5em}
  After the four core lectures:

  \vspace{0.5em}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item split a model's output into exact per-component contributions, and say which parts
      mattered;
    \item test a claim about a component by intervening, and choose the right intervention;
    \item recognise the four standard ways an interpretability claim fools its author;
    \item train a probe on activations - the technique most likely to be useful in a job;
    \item open a real open-weight model's features in a browser and read them critically.
  \end{itemize}

  \vspace{0.8em}
  \trapbox{\small What you will \textbf{not} be able to do: explain how a frontier model answers a
  question. Nobody can yet. A lecture that promised it would be lying to you.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Two tools}
  \vspace{0.6em}
  \toolcard{TransformerLens}{2022 \textperiodcentered{} Neel Nanda}{github.com/TransformerLensOrg/TransformerLens}

  \vspace{0.2em}
  {\small A Python library that runs a model and keeps \textbf{every} intermediate value instead of
  throwing it away, with hooks to read or change any of them mid-run. All the language-model
  figures in this chapter come from it.}

  \vspace{0.9em}
  \toolcard{Neuronpedia}{Johnny Lin \textperiodcentered{} Decode Research}{neuronpedia.org}

  \vspace{0.2em}
  {\small A website for browsing the features of real open models - what each one fires on, what it
  pushes the output towards. Nothing to install, no GPU. The cheapest way to start.}
\end{frame}

% ============================================================ RECAP
\begin{frame}{Recap}
  \vspace{0.4em}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Mechanistic interpretability} explains a model with its \emph{parts} - weights and
      activations - instead of its inputs. Different instrument, not a better one.
    \item \textbf{Four reasons} to look inside: debugging, trust, science, control.
    \item A \textbf{four-neuron network} can be read completely: four features (edges), one circuit
      (``past any edge, you are out''), confirmed by deleting a unit.
    \item The vocabulary: \textbf{features} (often directions), \textbf{circuits},
      \textbf{universality}.
    \item The method: \textbf{look, hypothesise, intervene} - and only the last step is evidence.
  \end{itemize}

  \vspace{0.6em}
  \trapbox{\small And the warning from the stuck network: a number that looks good (87\%
  accuracy) can hide a model that learned nothing. Reading the inside is how you find out.}
\end{frame}
