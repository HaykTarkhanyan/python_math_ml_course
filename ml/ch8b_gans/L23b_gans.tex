\begin{frame}{A different bet entirely}
  \small
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.54\textwidth}
    L23 ended with a working generative model and an honest complaint: the VAE's samples
    are \textbf{blurry}. The cause was structural - we maximized a likelihood bound, and
    an averaged prediction scores well on it.
    \vskip 5pt
    \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\small
      \textbf{The GAN's bet:} stop measuring likelihood altogether. Instead, train a
      \emph{second} network to tell real images from generated ones - and use its verdict
      as the loss.}}
    \column{0.46\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\small
      If a critic cannot distinguish your output from real data, your output is
      indistinguishable from real data. \textbf{That is the whole idea}, and it is a
      remarkably direct answer to "what makes an image look real?"}}
    \vskip 5pt
    \footnotesize
    \textcolor{gray}{\textbf{Generative Adversarial Networks} (Goodfellow et al., 2014).}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\section{The adversarial idea}

\begin{frame}[plain]
  \vfill
  \begin{center}
    {\Large\color{popblue}\textbf{The adversarial idea}}\\[0.9em]
    \begin{minipage}{0.82\textwidth}\centering\small
      Replace the loss function with an opponent.
    \end{minipage}
  \end{center}
  \vfill
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The forger and the inspector}
  \small
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.46\textwidth}
    \begin{center}
      \includegraphics[width=0.92\linewidth]{lmu_counterfeiters.png}\\[2pt]
      {\scriptsize\color{gray} LMU I2DL, CC BY 4.0}
    \end{center}
    \column{0.54\textwidth}
    \footnotesize
    \textbf{Generator} $G$: takes a random vector $\mathbf{z}$ and produces an image. It
    never sees the training data. It only ever learns from the critic's reaction.
    \vskip 4pt
    \textbf{Discriminator} $D$: takes an image and outputs one number - the probability
    that this image is real.
    \vskip 5pt
    \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      They are trained \textbf{alternately} and want opposite things. $D$ improves at
      spotting fakes; $G$ improves at fooling $D$. Each one's progress is the other's
      problem.}}
    \vskip 4pt
    \textcolor{gray}{Note what is absent: no reconstruction term, no likelihood, no
    encoder. $G$ is judged only on whether its output passes.}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{One training step, concretely}
  \small
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.52\textwidth}
    \textbf{Update $D$}: show it a batch of real images (label 1) and a batch of generated
    ones (label 0). Plain binary classification.
    \vskip 4pt
    \textbf{Update $G$}: generate a fresh batch, push it through $D$, and change $G$ so
    that $D$ calls them \emph{real}.
    \vskip 6pt
    \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!12}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      The gradient reaching $G$ flows \textbf{through} $D$. That is why $D$ must be
      trained too, and why a $D$ that is too good or too weak both break learning.}}
    \column{0.48\textwidth}
    \begin{center}
      \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{lmu_gan_pseudocode.png}\\[2pt]
      {\scriptsize\color{gray} LMU I2DL, CC BY 4.0}
    \end{center}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\section{What is actually being optimized}

\begin{frame}[plain]
  \vfill
  \begin{center}
    {\Large\color{popblue}\textbf{What is actually being optimized}}\\[0.9em]
    \begin{minipage}{0.82\textwidth}\centering\small
      A game has no loss function. It has an equilibrium.
    \end{minipage}
  \end{center}
  \vfill
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The minimax objective}
  \small
  \[
    \min_{G}\ \max_{D}\quad
      \E_{\xv \sim p_{\text{data}}}\big[\log D(\xv)\big]
      \;+\;
      \E_{\mathbf{z} \sim p(\mathbf{z})}\big[\log\big(1 - D(G(\mathbf{z}))\big)\big]
  \]
  \vskip 4pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      Read it in two halves. $D$ wants the first term large (call real images real) and
      the second large (call fakes fake). $G$ can only touch the second term, and wants
      it \emph{small}.}}
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      \textbf{Notice what this is not.} There is no single quantity being minimized. Two
      players move in opposite directions on the same number.
      \vskip 3pt
      Everything difficult about GANs follows from that one fact.}}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{If the discriminator were perfect}
  \small
  Hold $G$ fixed and solve for the best possible $D$. It is a calculus exercise, and the
  answer is clean:
  \[
    D^{*}(\xv) \;=\; \frac{p_{\text{data}}(\xv)}{p_{\text{data}}(\xv) + p_{G}(\xv)}
  \]
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \footnotesize
    Sanity-check it. Where only real data lives, $p_G = 0$ and $D^*=1$. Where only fakes
    live, $D^*=0$. Where the two distributions agree, $D^* = \tfrac{1}{2}$ - the critic
    is reduced to guessing.
    \vskip 4pt
    Substituting $D^*$ back in, the generator turns out to be minimizing the
    \textbf{Jensen-Shannon divergence} between $p_{\text{data}}$ and $p_G$ - a symmetric
    cousin of the KL divergence from L23.
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      \textbf{So GANs do minimize a divergence after all} - just never one you can
      evaluate. The loss you watch during training is not it.}}
    \vskip 4pt
    \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!12}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      \textbf{And the theory quietly assumes too much:} that $D$ is optimal at every step,
      and that $G$ can represent any distribution. Neither holds. The equilibrium argument
      is a guide, not a guarantee.}}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The first thing that breaks}
  \small
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    Early in training $G$ is terrible, so $D$ rejects everything and
    $D(G(\mathbf{z}))\approx 0$.
    \vskip 4pt
    Look at what happens to $G$'s gradient there: $\log(1-D(G(\mathbf{z})))$ is
    \textbf{flat} near $0$. The generator is losing badly and learning nothing - exactly
    when it most needs signal.
    \vskip 5pt
    \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      \textbf{The fix (same paper):} have $G$ \emph{maximize} $\log D(G(\mathbf{z}))$
      instead of minimizing $\log(1-D(G(\mathbf{z})))$. Same fixed point, steep gradient
      where it matters. This is the \textbf{non-saturating} loss, and essentially every
      implementation uses it.}}
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \begin{center}
      \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{lmu_ns_loss.png}\\[2pt]
      {\scriptsize\color{gray} LMU I2DL, CC BY 4.0}
    \end{center}
    \vskip -2pt
    \footnotesize
    \textcolor{gray}{Our own implementation uses it too - see the comment in
    \texttt{py\_src/gan\_digits.py}.}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\section{Why this is hard: our own run}

\begin{frame}[plain]
  \vfill
  \begin{center}
    {\Large\color{popblue}\textbf{Why this is hard: our own run}}\\[0.9em]
    \begin{minipage}{0.82\textwidth}\centering\small
      Everything from here is measured on a GAN trained for these slides.
    \end{minipage}
  \end{center}
  \vfill
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The loss tells you nothing}
  \small
  A GAN on 8x8 handwritten digits - the same dataset a later chapter will reuse, so the
  two families can be compared on identical data - 8000 steps on a laptop:
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{gan_training.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vskip -3pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \footnotesize
    \textbf{Left:} the two losses wander and cross. Neither descends. If you are used to
    watching a training curve to decide when to stop, that instinct is now useless.
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \footnotesize
    \textbf{Right:} discriminator accuracy is the one readable signal. Theory says it
    should settle at $0.5$. Ours peaks at $\mathbf{0.92}$, then drifts down to $0.67$ -
    and never reaches equilibrium.
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Mode collapse, as it happens}
  \small
  The same ten latent vectors $\mathbf{z}$, decoded at four points in training:
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.78\linewidth]{gan_samples.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vskip -3pt
  \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.985\linewidth}{\footnotesize
    \textbf{Look at step 500.} Ten \emph{different} inputs produce essentially the
    \emph{same} output. The generator has found one image that fools $D$ and stopped
    exploring - textbook \textbf{mode collapse}. By step 2000 it has recovered to roughly
    two recurring shapes - better, still far short of ten digits. By 8000 it has escaped
    into real variety.
    \vskip 3pt
    Compare with the accuracy curve: the collapse sits exactly where $D$ was dominating
    at $0.92$. \textbf{An overpowering critic causes collapse.}}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Mode collapse you cannot see}
  \small
  Escaping visible collapse is not the same as covering the data. Train a plain classifier
  on the \emph{real} digits (accuracy $0.996$), then use it to label 2000 generated ones:
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.80\linewidth]{gan_coverage.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vskip -3pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.52\textwidth}
    \footnotesize
    Overall coverage is decent - total variation $0.079$. This run did \textbf{not}
    catastrophically collapse, and I am not going to pretend it did.
    \column{0.48\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!12}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      But look at digit \textbf{8}: $0.060$ generated against $0.097$ real. Roughly
      \textbf{38\% of the 8s are missing}, and no amount of staring at the samples
      would have told you.}}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The failure modes, named}
  \small
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      \textbf{Mode collapse.} $G$ maps many $\mathbf{z}$ to few outputs. Sharp images,
      no diversity. Ours did it at step 500 and partly recovered.}}
    \vskip 4pt
    \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      \textbf{Non-convergence.} The pair oscillates instead of settling. $G$ fixes what
      $D$ just learned, $D$ learns the new tell, forever.}}
    \vskip 4pt
    \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      \textbf{Vanishing gradient.} $D$ wins outright, $G$ gets no signal. The
      non-saturating loss helps but does not eliminate it.}}
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \footnotesize
    \textbf{The simplest possible example.} Take the toy game $V(x,y) = xy$, where one
    player lowers $V$ by moving $x$ and the other raises it by moving $y$. The unique
    equilibrium is $(0,0)$.
    \vskip 3pt
    Follow gradient steps and you get this: five states, and state 5 is state 1 again.
    \begin{center}
      \includegraphics[width=0.99\linewidth]{lmu_nonconverge.png}\\[1pt]
      {\scriptsize\color{gray} LMU I2DL, CC BY 4.0}
    \end{center}
    \vskip -1pt
    \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\scriptsize
      It \textbf{orbits the solution forever} - in a game with two variables and no
      networks at all. All three failures share one root: \textbf{no fixed objective to
      descend}.}}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What actually makes it train}
  \small
  A decade of empirical folklore, most of it from \textbf{DCGAN} - \textbf{Deep
  Convolutional GAN} (Radford et al., 2015):
  \vskip 4pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.52\textwidth}
    \footnotesize
    \begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{2pt}
      \item strided convolutions instead of pooling, both directions
      \item batch normalization in both networks
      \item \textbf{LeakyReLU} in $D$ - a dead ReLU kills the gradient $G$ depends on
      \item Adam with $\beta_1 = 0.5$, not the usual $0.9$
      \item balance the two updates; never let one race ahead
    \end{itemize}
    \vskip 4pt
    \textcolor{gray}{Our run uses the LeakyReLU, dropout and $\beta_1=0.5$ items. They are
    not optional decoration - $\beta_1=0.9$ diverges.}
    \column{0.48\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!12}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      \textbf{This list is the tell.} A method that needs five architectural
      superstitions to converge is a method fighting its own formulation.
      \vskip 3pt
      Later work attacked the root instead - Wasserstein GAN (Arjovsky et al., 2017)
      replaces the divergence; spectral normalization constrains $D$.}}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Recap}
  \small
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.54\textwidth}
    \begin{enumerate}\setlength\itemsep{2pt}
      \item A GAN replaces the loss function with a second network.
      \item The objective is a \textbf{minimax game}, not a minimization - there is no
        number that going down means progress.
      \item With an optimal $D$, $G$ minimizes the Jensen-Shannon divergence - which you
        can never evaluate.
      \item The naive generator loss saturates; everyone uses the non-saturating form.
      \item Our own run: losses that wander, $D$ accuracy peaking at $0.92$, visible mode
        collapse at step 500, and $38\%$ of the 8s still missing at the end.
      \item Training is stabilized by a list of tricks, which is itself a diagnosis.
    \end{enumerate}
    \column{0.46\textwidth}
    \vskip 8pt
    \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\small
      \textbf{Next:} everything above is the unconditional case - a GAN that draws
      \emph{something}.
      \vskip 4pt
      L23c is what people actually built with these: asking for a specific class, turning
      sketches into photographs, editing faces by moving in latent space - and how you
      evaluate any of it when likelihood is unavailable.}}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}
