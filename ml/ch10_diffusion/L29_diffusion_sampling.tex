\section{The sampler, and a question}

\begin{frame}[plain]
  \vfill
  \begin{center}
    {\Large\color{popblue}\textbf{The sampler, and a question}}\\[0.9em]
    \begin{minipage}{0.82\textwidth}\centering\small
      Read the algorithm carefully. Something in it should bother you.
    \end{minipage}
  \end{center}
  \vfill
\end{frame}

\wlslide{wl_algorithms.jpg}

\begin{frame}{The DDPM sampler}
  \small
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.42\textwidth}
    \footnotesize
    The two algorithms from Ho et al.\ (2020), side by side on the previous slide.
    \vskip 4pt
    \textbf{Algorithm 1} is training - the loop we wrote in L28.
    \vskip 4pt
    \textbf{Algorithm 2} is sampling, and it is the one to read carefully.
    \column{0.58\textwidth}
    \footnotesize
    Reading the sampling algorithm, one step:
    \[
      \xv_{t-1} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\left(\xv_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}}\ \eps_{\thetav}(\xv_t,t)\right)
        \;+\; \sigma_t \mathbf{z}
    \]
    \vskip 3pt
    The bracket is exactly $\bm\mu_{\thetav}$ from L28 - the model's best guess at where
    the previous image was.
    \vskip 5pt
    \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      Then there is $+\ \sigma_t\mathbf{z}$, with $\mathbf{z}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\id)$
      drawn fresh, and $\sigma_t^2=\beta_t$ as fixed in L28.
      \textbf{The sampler adds new random noise at every step.}}}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Hold that thought}
  \small
  \textbf{Predict first.} Our network was trained for one job: remove noise. The sampler
  then puts noise \emph{back}, a thousand times over.
  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{center}
    \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.86\linewidth}{\centering
      What happens if we simply delete that term?}}
  \end{center}
  \vskip 8pt
  \small
  Most people's first guess is "nothing much, maybe slightly less variety." The truth is
  more interesting, and it tells us what the model actually learned.
  \vskip 6pt
  \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.985\linewidth}{\small
    \textbf{We will answer this in Section 3.} First we need a picture of what
    $\eps_{\thetav}$ has learned - and for that we shrink images down to two pixels.}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{By hand: one step of the sampler}
  \small
  Take the constant $\beta=0.02$ from L27, so $\alpha_t = 0.98$ and
  $\bar\alpha_t = 0.98^{\,t}$. We are at $t=100$. One pixel of $\xv_{100}$ reads
  $1.300$, and the network predicts $\eps_{\thetav} = 1.150$ for it.
  \vskip 3pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.56\textwidth}
    {\footnotesize
    \begin{tabular}{@{}ll@{}}
      $\bar\alpha_{100} = 0.98^{100}$ & $= 0.13262$ \\
      $\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_{100}}$ & $= 0.93133$ \\
      $\beta_t / \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}$ & $= 0.02/0.93133 = 0.02147$ \\[3pt]
      $\xv_t - 0.02147 \times 1.150$ & $= 1.27530$ \\
      $\div \sqrt{0.98}$ & $= 1.28825$ \quad\textcolor{gray}{\tiny(model's move)} \\[3pt]
      $\sigma_t = \sqrt{\beta_t}$ & $= 0.14142$ \\
      draw $\mathbf{z} = -0.30$, add $\sigma_t \mathbf{z}$ & $= -0.04243$ \\[2pt]
      \hline
      $\xv_{99}$ & $= \mathbf{1.246}$ \\
    \end{tabular}}
    \column{0.44\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      \textbf{Look at the two moves.}
      \vskip 3pt
      The model shifted the pixel by $1.288 - 1.300 = \mathbf{-0.012}$.
      \vskip 3pt
      The noise shifted it by $\mathbf{-0.042}$ - about \textbf{3.5 times further}.}}
    \vskip 5pt
    \footnotesize
    At this noise level the random term is not a small correction on top of the model's
    answer. It \emph{dominates} the step.
    \vskip 4pt
    \textcolor{gray}{Which makes the question on the last slide sharper, not softer: why
    would you let noise do most of the moving in a process whose job is removing noise?}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{It does work}
  \small
  The sampler, run on the little digit model we trained in L28:
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{digits_samples.pdf}

\section{What the model actually learned}

\begin{frame}[plain]
  \vfill
  \begin{center}
    {\Large\color{popblue}\textbf{What the model actually learned}}\\[0.9em]
    \begin{minipage}{0.82\textwidth}\centering\small
      Two pixels instead of a million, so we can draw the whole thing.
    \end{minipage}
  \end{center}
  \vfill
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Shrink the image until you can plot it}
  \small
  An image is a point in pixel space. With two pixels, that space is a sheet of paper: the
  first pixel's brightness on one axis, the second on the other.
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{spiral_data.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vskip -3pt
  \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.985\linewidth}{\footnotesize
    Real images occupy a thin, highly structured set inside a huge space. We fake that
    with a spiral: most of the plane is not data, and the data is curved and connected.
    \textbf{Every claim from here on is visible.}}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Adding noise is a random walk with a leash}
  \small
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.56\textwidth}
    In the middle panel, one point takes 300 forward steps. Each step is
    \[ \xv_t = \sqrt{1-\beta_t}\ \xv_{t-1} + \sqrt{\beta_t}\ \eps \]
    which is a random jump \emph{plus} a small pull back toward the origin.
    \vskip 6pt
    \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!12}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      \textbf{A precise word on names.} Pure Brownian motion has no pull - that is the
      variance-exploding process we rejected in L27. With the $\sqrt{1-\beta}$ drift this
      is an \textbf{Ornstein-Uhlenbeck} process: it wanders, but it is on a leash, so it
      settles into $\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\id)$ instead of drifting forever.}}
    \column{0.44\textwidth}
    \footnotesize
    "Diffusion" is still the right name - the field takes it from
    \textbf{Sohl-Dickstein et al.\ (2015)}, who built the idea on non-equilibrium
    thermodynamics.
    \vskip 6pt
    \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      Generating an image is asking: given a point that has wandered off, \textbf{which way
      is back?}}}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The network is a direction field}
  \small
  Ask $\eps_{\thetav}$ at every point on a grid, and plot which way it says to move:
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{spiral_score_field.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vskip -4pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \footnotesize
    \textbf{High noise} ($t=900$): a broad field pointing at the data in general. It has no
    idea which arm you belong to, and it should not.
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \footnotesize
    \textbf{Low noise} ($t=50$): the field has resolved into the spiral's fine structure and
    points at the nearest arm.
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{That field has a name}
  \small
  Up to a constant, the direction the model gives is the \textbf{score} of the noisy data
  distribution:
  \[
    \nabla_{\xv_t} \log p(\xv_t)
    \;=\; -\,\frac{\eps_{\thetav}(\xv_t, t)}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}}
  \]
  \vskip 2pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.52\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      The gradient of the log-density: the direction in which data becomes \emph{more
      likely}. Note the \textbf{minus sign} - moving away from the predicted noise is
      moving toward the data - and the $1/\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}$ scale.}}
    \column{0.48\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      \textbf{Why this matters:} predicting noise, predicting the score, and predicting the
      clean image are the same task in different coordinates. It also connects DDPM to a
      separate line of work (Song \& Ermon, 2019) - and we use that connection at the end
      of this lecture.}}
  \end{columns}
  \vskip 5pt
  \footnotesize
  \textcolor{gray}{This is also why conditioning on $t$ was essential: one network has to
  represent all of these fields, and they are very different at $t=900$ and $t=50$.}
\end{frame}

\section{Why the noise comes back}

\begin{frame}[plain]
  \vfill
  \begin{center}
    {\Large\color{popblue}\textbf{Why the noise comes back}}\\[0.9em]
    \begin{minipage}{0.82\textwidth}\centering\small
      The question from Section 1, answered.
    \end{minipage}
  \end{center}
  \vfill
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Run it three ways}
  \small
  Same trained model, same starting noise. Only the sampler changes.
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{spiral_samplers.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vskip -4pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \footnotesize
    \textbf{Left:} the real sampler. Points land across the whole spiral.
    Measured spread: $0.68$.
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \footnotesize
    \textbf{Middle:} the noise term deleted. Every one of the 700 points lands in
    essentially the same place. Measured spread: $\mathbf{0.003}$.
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The same failure, on a real model}
  \small
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.32\textwidth}
    \begin{center}
      \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{wl_tree_good.jpg}\\[3pt]
      {\scriptsize with the noise step}
    \end{center}
    \column{0.32\textwidth}
    \begin{center}
      \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{wl_tree_blurry.jpg}\\[3pt]
      {\scriptsize noise step deleted}
    \end{center}
    \column{0.36\textwidth}
    \vskip 6pt
    \footnotesize
    Stable Diffusion, same prompt, same seed. Delete the noise term and you get a brown
    smear.
    \vskip 5pt
    \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      The spiral collapsed to a point. The image collapses to a \textbf{blur} - which is
      what the average of many images looks like.}}
    \vskip 4pt
    {\scriptsize\color{gray} Welch Labs / 3Blue1Brown (2025)}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why: the denoiser is being fed inputs it never saw}
  \small
  The network was trained at specific noise levels. At step $t$ it expects its input to
  carry exactly the amount of noise the forward process puts there, $1-\bar\alpha_t$.
  \vskip 5pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      Delete the $\sigma_t\mathbf{z}$ term and $\xv_{t-1}$ comes out \textbf{cleaner} than
      $\sigma_{t-1}$ demands. On the next call the network sees an input that is less noisy
      than anything in its training set.}}
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!12}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      Believing there is more noise than there is, it \textbf{removes too much} - and
      "remove as much as possible" points at the average of the training data. Repeat a
      thousand times and every trajectory ends up at that average.}}
  \end{columns}
  \vskip 6pt
  \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.985\linewidth}{\small
    \textbf{The noise is not decoration - it is bookkeeping.} It keeps each step's input at
    the noise level the next step was trained for. That, and not any deep need for
    randomness, is why it is there.}}
  \vskip 3pt
  \footnotesize
  \textcolor{gray}{Keep that phrasing in mind: it tells us immediately how to build a
  \emph{deterministic} sampler that does not blur.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{One honest caveat about the toy}
  \small
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.52\textwidth}
    On the spiral, the collapsed points still land \emph{on} the data - just all in one
    spot. So the toy shows lost \textbf{variety}, not lost \textbf{realism}.
    \vskip 5pt
    In the space of real images, the average of many trees is not a tree at all. There the
    collapse leaves the set of realistic images entirely, which is why the output is a
    smear rather than one particular tree.
    \column{0.48\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!12}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      \textbf{Where the analogy stops.} A 2-D picture cannot show what "off the manifold"
      means in a million dimensions. Take the mechanism from the spiral, take the severity
      from the tree.}}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What this looks like when you get it wrong}
  \small
  You will implement this. Here is the same correctly-trained digit model, sampled three
  wrong ways - every fault below is at \textbf{sampling} time, not in the weights.
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.90\linewidth]{digits_failures.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vskip -4pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.52\textwidth}
    \scriptsize
    \textbf{Skipping steps} and \textbf{too much noise} are the fault we just diagnosed,
    in the other direction: the input reaches a noise level the network never trained on,
    and it breaks loudly.
    \column{0.48\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\scriptsize
      \textbf{The middle panel is the dangerous one.} The wrong schedule gives clean,
      plausible digits - \emph{different} ones, but nothing looks broken. A bug you can
      see costs an afternoon; a bug you cannot see ships.}}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\section{Deterministic sampling}

\begin{frame}[plain]
  \vfill
  \begin{center}
    {\Large\color{popblue}\textbf{Deterministic sampling}}\\[0.9em]
    \begin{minipage}{0.82\textwidth}\centering\small
      A thousand network calls per image is a problem worth solving.
    \end{minipage}
  \end{center}
  \vfill
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The cost problem}
  \small
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.52\textwidth}
    The DDPM sampler runs the full chain: with $T=1000$, that is \textbf{one thousand
    forward passes} of a large network per image.
    \vskip 5pt
    \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      For a while this was the reason diffusion stayed a research curiosity while GANs -
      one forward pass - shipped in products.}}
    \column{0.48\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!12}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      \textbf{Do not attach "1000 steps" to Stable Diffusion.} Shipping models use 20-50.
      The rest of this section is how they got there.}}
  \end{columns}
  \vskip 8pt
  \textbf{Predict first:} we just spent a whole section proving that removing the noise
  ruins everything. So how can a \emph{deterministic} sampler possibly work?
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The resolution}
  \small
  \pause
  \begin{center}
    \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.9\linewidth}{\centering
      Do not \emph{delete} the noise. \textbf{Rescale the step} so the noise level stays
      where the network expects it.}}
  \end{center}
  \vskip 6pt
  \small
  Our failed experiment kept the DDPM step size and dropped the noise, so the noise level
  fell out of sync. Instead, jump directly between consistent noise levels: predict the
  clean image, then re-noise it to exactly the level of the step you are moving to.
  \[
    \hat{\xv}_0 = \frac{\xv_t - \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\ \eps_{\thetav}}{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}},
    \qquad
    \xv_{t-1} = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\ \hat{\xv}_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\ \eps_{\thetav}
  \]
  \vskip 2pt
  \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.985\linewidth}{\footnotesize
    No random draw anywhere, yet every input is at a noise level the network trained on.
    This is \textbf{DDIM} - \textbf{Denoising Diffusion Implicit Models} (Song et al., 2020).
    \textbf{It needs no retraining}: same weights, different sampler.}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{It works, and it skips}
  \small
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.46\textwidth}
    Because consecutive $\bar\alpha$ values no longer have to be adjacent, you can take
    \textbf{25 steps instead of 1000} by striding through the schedule.
    \vskip 5pt
    In the right-hand panel of the sampler figure: 25 steps, deterministic, measured spread
    $0.64$ against DDPM's $0.68$.
    \vskip 5pt
    \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      \textbf{40x fewer network calls, same distribution, same weights.} This is what made
      diffusion practical.}}
    \column{0.54\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!12}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      \textbf{Two caveats worth stating.}
      \begin{enumerate}\setlength\itemsep{2pt}
        \item The theory promises the same \emph{distribution}, not the same image. A
          given starting noise gives different pictures under the two samplers.
        \item The guarantee holds in the continuous limit. At 20 steps the
          discretization error is real and visible - very-low-step samples have a
          recognizable look.
      \end{enumerate}}}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\wlslide{wl_sde_ode.jpg}

\begin{frame}{A note on where the theory came from}
  \small
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.52\textwidth}
    You will often see DDIM introduced through \textbf{stochastic differential equations},
    as on the previous slide: the DDPM sampler is an SDE; a result from statistical
    mechanics (the Fokker-Planck equation) gives an \textbf{ordinary} differential
    equation - the \emph{probability-flow ODE} - whose distribution at every time is the
    same.
    \vskip 5pt
    \footnotesize
    Both update rules end up looking almost identical. The stochastic one has a random
    term; the deterministic one has a different step scaling instead.
    \column{0.48\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!12}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      \textbf{That is not how DDIM was found.} Song et al.\ (2020) derived it from a
      non-Markovian forward process, with no SDE anywhere.
      \vskip 3pt
      The ODE connection came from Song et al.\ (2021), \emph{afterwards}, and showed
      DDIM is roughly a first-order discretization of it.}}
    \vskip 5pt
    \footnotesize
    \textcolor{gray}{Worth knowing both framings, and worth not confusing the order they
    happened in.}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{How would we even know which sampler is better?}
  \small
  We have been saying "better samples" for two lectures while judging by eye.
  \vskip 5pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.52\textwidth}
    The standard answer is \textbf{FID} (\textbf{Fr\'echet Inception Distance}): push real
    and generated images through a pretrained vision network, fit a Gaussian to each set of
    features, and measure the distance between them.
    \vskip 5pt
    \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      It rewards both \textbf{realism} and \textbf{variety}: a model producing one perfect
      image every time scores badly, which is exactly the failure we just watched.}}
    \column{0.48\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!12}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      \textbf{What it misses:} it says nothing about whether the image matches a
      \emph{prompt}, it depends on the feature network, and it is unstable on small
      sample counts.}}
    \vskip 5pt
    \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      This closes the loop on L28: dropping the weights from the loss made the likelihood
      \emph{worse} and FID \emph{better}. Different objectives, and the field chose the
      one matching what people look at.}}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

% ================================================================
\begin{frame}{Recap}
  \small
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.54\textwidth}
    \begin{enumerate}\setlength\itemsep{2pt}
      \item The DDPM sampler subtracts predicted noise, then adds fresh noise back.
      \item Shrunk to two pixels, the trained network is a \textbf{direction field} -
        the score - that sharpens as $t\to 0$.
      \item Delete the added noise and everything collapses to the data mean: blurry.
      \item Because the denoiser is then fed inputs \textbf{cleaner than it was trained
        for}, and over-denoises.
      \item DDIM fixes it by rescaling the step, not deleting the noise: deterministic,
        25 steps, no retraining.
      \item FID is how the field measures any of this.
    \end{enumerate}
    \column{0.46\textwidth}
    \vskip 8pt
    \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\small
      \textbf{Next:} we can generate good images, quickly, from noise.
      \vskip 4pt
      We still have no way to ask for anything. Every image so far has been whatever the
      model felt like producing.
      \vskip 4pt
      L30 connects text to pixels.}}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}
