\section{Generation is sampling}

\begin{frame}[plain]
  \vfill
  \begin{center}
    {\Large\color{popblue}\textbf{Generation is sampling}}\\[0.9em]
    \begin{minipage}{0.82\textwidth}\centering\small
      We want to draw from a distribution nobody can write down.
    \end{minipage}
  \end{center}
  \vfill
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The problem, stated honestly}
  \small
  Call $p(\xv)$ the distribution of \textbf{natural images}: the tiny corner of
  $\R^{512\times512\times3}$ where photographs live, and the vast rest is static.
  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\small
      \textbf{Generating an image} $=$ drawing a fresh sample from $p(\xv)$.}}
    \vskip 8pt
    But we have no formula for $p(\xv)$, and there probably is not one. All we have is a
    pile of samples: the training set.
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\small
      So: \textbf{sample from a distribution you cannot write down, evaluate, or
      normalize.} Every generative model is an answer to this one question.}}
    \vskip 8pt
    \footnotesize
    L23's answer: learn a decoder from a simple latent. It worked, but blurred.
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The diffusion bet}
  \small
  Instead of modelling $p(\xv)$ directly, destroy it in a way we control completely.
  \vskip 5pt
  \begin{center}
    \begin{tikzpicture}[node distance=1.5cm, every node/.style={font=\scriptsize}]
      \node[draw, rounded corners, fill=armblue!12, minimum width=1.5cm, minimum height=0.8cm] (a) {image};
      \node[draw, rounded corners, fill=armblue!8, right=of a, minimum width=1.5cm, minimum height=0.8cm] (b) {a bit noisy};
      \node[draw, rounded corners, fill=armred!8, right=of b, minimum width=1.5cm, minimum height=0.8cm] (c) {very noisy};
      \node[draw, rounded corners, fill=armred!14, right=of c, minimum width=1.5cm, minimum height=0.8cm] (d) {pure noise};
      \draw[-{Latex}, thick, armred] (a) -- node[above]{$+$ noise} (b);
      \draw[-{Latex}, thick, armred] (b) -- node[above]{$+$ noise} (c);
      \draw[-{Latex}, thick, armred] (c) -- node[above]{$+$ noise} (d);
      \draw[-{Latex}, thick, paramgreen] ($(d.south)+(0,-0.15)$) to[bend left=22]
        node[below, font=\scriptsize\bfseries]{learn to walk this back} ($(a.south)+(0,-0.15)$);
    \end{tikzpicture}
  \end{center}
  \vskip 4pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.55\textwidth}
    \footnotesize
    The forward direction is \textbf{ours}: we choose the noise, so we know it exactly.
    The reverse direction is the only thing we have to learn.
    \column{0.45\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      \textbf{Sohl-Dickstein et al.\ (2015)} had this idea and the name. It sat mostly
      ignored for five years while everyone worked on GANs.}}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\section{The forward process}

\begin{frame}[plain]
  \vfill
  \begin{center}
    {\Large\color{popblue}\textbf{The forward process}}\\[0.9em]
    \begin{minipage}{0.82\textwidth}\centering\small
      Break it correctly, or the whole scheme fails before training starts.
    \end{minipage}
  \end{center}
  \vfill
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{One step of noising}
  \small
  Write $\xv_0$ for a clean image. One step adds Gaussian noise scaled by $\beta$:
  \[
    \xv_1 = \xv_0 + \sqrt{\beta}\,\eps, \qquad \eps \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\id)
  \]
  \vskip -4pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.55\textwidth}
    Equivalently, and more usefully:
    \[ q(\xv_1 \mid \xv_0) = \mathcal{N}(\xv_0,\ \beta\id) \]
    \vskip 4pt
    \footnotesize
    "Adding noise to $\xv_0$" and "sampling from a Gaussian centred on $\xv_0$" are the
    same sentence. The second form is what lets us do algebra later.
    \column{0.45\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\small
      Independent Gaussian noise \textbf{adds in variance}. Two steps of $\beta$ is one
      step of $2\beta$:
      \[ q(\xv_t \mid \xv_0) = \mathcal{N}(\xv_0,\ t\beta\id) \]}}
    \vskip 4pt
    \footnotesize
    So we can jump straight to step $t$ without simulating the ones in between.
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Does that actually reach pure noise?}
  \small
  We wanted the forward process to end at $\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\id)$, so that at
  generation time we can start from a random draw.
  \vskip 4pt
  \textbf{Predict first:} with $q(\xv_t \mid \xv_0) = \mathcal{N}(\xv_0,\ t\beta\id)$,
  does it?
  \vskip 5pt
  \pause
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{ve_vs_vp.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vskip -2pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      \textbf{No.} The mean never leaves $\xv_0$ and the variance grows without bound.
      This is called \textbf{variance exploding} (VE).}}
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \footnotesize
    You never arrive at a fixed distribution, so there is nothing to sample from when
    you want to run the process backwards.
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The fix: shrink the mean as you add noise}
  \small
  Scale the previous image down by $\sqrt{1-\beta}$ before adding the noise:
  \[
    \xv_t = \sqrt{1-\beta}\ \xv_{t-1} + \sqrt{\beta}\,\eps
  \]
  Name the surviving fraction of each step $\alpha_t := 1-\beta_t$, and its running product
  $\bar\alpha_t := \prod_{s=1}^{t}\alpha_s$. Unrolling back to $\xv_0$ then gives the
  formula we will use constantly:
  \vskip -6pt
  \[
    \boxed{\ q(\xv_t \mid \xv_0) = \mathcal{N}\!\left(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\ \xv_0,\
      (1-\bar\alpha_t)\id\right)}
    \qquad\text{with}\quad \bar\alpha_t = (1-\beta)^t \ \text{for constant } \beta
  \]
  \vskip -4pt
  \begin{center}\footnotesize
    Three symbols, and you need all of them:
    \ $\beta_t$ = noise added at step $t$; \
    \ $\alpha_t = 1-\beta_t$ = signal kept at step $t$; \
    \ $\bar\alpha_t$ = signal kept \emph{in total}.
    \ Note $\bar\alpha_t = \alpha_t\,\bar\alpha_{t-1}$.
  \end{center}
  \vskip 2pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.52\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      Since $0<\beta<1$, we get $\bar\alpha_t \to 0$. So the mean coefficient
      $\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\to 0$ and the variance $1-\bar\alpha_t \to 1$: the process
      lands exactly on $\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\id)$. This is \textbf{variance
      preserving} (VP).}}
    \column{0.48\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!12}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      Two things to keep straight:
      \begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{1pt}
        \item $\bar\alpha_t=(1-\beta)^t$ holds for \textbf{constant} $\beta$. In a moment
          $\beta$ will vary and it becomes a product.
        \item "Variance preserved at 1" assumes the data was scaled to $[-1,1]$ first.
          DDPM does exactly that.
      \end{itemize}}}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Before we move on: variance exploding is not a mistake}
  \small
  We discarded VE because it did not suit us, not because it is wrong.
  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\small
      \textbf{VE is a real, working family.} Score matching (Song \& Ermon, 2019) is
      built on it, and Song et al.\ (2021) showed VE and VP are two members of one
      continuous family.}}
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\small
      \textbf{Why DDPM picked VP:} it converges to a \emph{fixed} $\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\id)$
      that you can draw from directly. With VE you must track how wide the noise has become.}}
  \end{columns}
  \vskip 8pt
  \footnotesize
  \textbf{DDPM} is \textbf{Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models} (Ho, Jain \& Abbeel, 2020):
  the paper that took diffusion from a curiosity to the state of the art, and the spine of
  the next two lectures.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Watch it happen}
  \small
  A deliberately awkward data distribution, pushed through the VP forward process:
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.96\linewidth]{forward_1d.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vskip -2pt
  \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.985\linewidth}{\footnotesize
    Nothing here is learned. Two bumps of unequal mass and width are dragged toward the
    origin and flattened, and by $t=199$ the red dashed $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$ sits exactly on
    top. \textbf{Whatever your data was, the forward process forgets it.}}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Noise schedules: $\beta$ does not have to be constant}
  \small
  In practice $\beta$ varies with $t$, and then
  $\bar\alpha_t = \prod_{s=1}^{t}(1-\beta_s)$.
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.96\linewidth]{noise_schedules.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vskip -3pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \footnotesize
    \textbf{Linear} (Ho et al., 2020) destroys the image well before $t=T$, so the late
    steps teach the model almost nothing.
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \footnotesize
    \textbf{Cosine} (Nichol \& Dhariwal, 2021) holds signal longer and spends the
    schedule where there is still something to learn.
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{By hand: how fast does the image actually die?}
  \small
  Take a constant $\beta = 0.02$, so $\bar\alpha_t = 0.98^{\,t}$, and the surviving
  fraction of the original image is $\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}$.
  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{center}\footnotesize
    \begin{tabular}{r|c|c|l}
      $t$ & $\bar\alpha_t = 0.98^{\,t}$ & $\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}$ & what is left \\\hline
      $10$   & $0.98^{10} = 0.817$      & $0.904$ & barely touched \\
      $100$  & $0.98^{100} = 0.133$     & $0.364$ & recognizable, very grainy \\
      $500$  & $0.98^{500} = 4.1\times10^{-5}$ & $0.0064$ & essentially gone \\
      $1000$ & $0.98^{1000} = 1.7\times10^{-9}$ & $0.00004$ & noise \\
    \end{tabular}
  \end{center}
  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.55\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!12}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      The decay is \textbf{geometric}, so "how many steps" is much less interesting than
      "what is $\bar\alpha_t$". Papers quote $\bar\alpha$, not $t$, for this reason.}}
    \column{0.45\textwidth}
    \footnotesize
    Note $\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{1000}} \approx 4\times 10^{-5}$: at that point $\xv_T$ really
    is a draw from $\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\id)$, to any precision that matters.
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why the closed form matters so much}
  \small
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.52\textwidth}
    To train on step $t$, the naive route is to simulate:
    \vskip 3pt
    {\footnotesize
    \texttt{x = x0}\\
    \texttt{for s in range(t): x = step(x)}}
    \vskip 6pt
    That is $t$ operations per training example, with $t$ up to $1000$.
    \vskip 6pt
    The closed form does it in \textbf{one}:
    \[ \xv_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,\xv_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\ \eps \]
    \column{0.48\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\small
      \textbf{This is the frame to remember.} Pick a random $t$, draw one $\eps$, get
      $\xv_t$ immediately. It is what makes diffusion training cheap enough to be
      practical, and you will see it again in every equation for the next two lectures.}}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\section{Turning around}

\begin{frame}[plain]
  \vfill
  \begin{center}
    {\Large\color{popblue}\textbf{Turning around}}\\[0.9em]
    \begin{minipage}{0.82\textwidth}\centering\small
      Destroying was free. Now the part that needs a network.
    \end{minipage}
  \end{center}
  \vfill
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The reverse step is where the parameters live}
  \small
  The forward process $q$ has \textbf{no learned parameters at all} - just $\beta$ and
  a random draw. Everything we do not know sits in the reverse direction:
  \[
    p_{\thetav}(\xv_{t-1} \mid \xv_t)
  \]
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      $\thetav$ are the weights of a neural network. If we can learn one reverse step,
      we can chain it: start at $\xv_T \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\id)$ and run down to
      $\xv_0$.}}
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!12}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      Note the asymmetry: $q$ needs $\xv_0$ to be defined, $p_{\thetav}$ must not - at
      generation time $\xv_0$ is exactly the thing we are trying to produce. This
      tension drives the whole of next lecture.}}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The obvious plan, and why it is not the one used}
  \small
  \textbf{Predict first:} train the network to undo one step - given $\xv_t$, predict
  $\xv_{t-1}$. Then chain it $T$ times. What goes wrong?
  \vskip 4pt
  \pause
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.44\textwidth}
    \begin{center}
      \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{wl_noising_chain.jpg}\\[2pt]
      {\scriptsize\color{gray} Welch Labs / 3Blue1Brown (2025)}
    \end{center}
    \column{0.56\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\small
      It \textbf{does not work well}, and essentially no modern model is built this way.}}
    \vskip 6pt
    \footnotesize
    The single step from $\xv_t$ to $\xv_{t-1}$ is almost entirely the random draw we just
    made. Asking a network to predict it is asking it to predict noise from noise: the
    target is nearly pure variance, and the learnable part is a thin signal underneath.
    \vskip 5pt
    There is a target with the \emph{same} information and far less variance. Finding it
    is what the next lecture derives.
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Recap}
  \small
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.52\textwidth}
    \begin{enumerate}\setlength\itemsep{3pt}
      \item Generating $=$ sampling from a $p(\xv)$ we cannot write down.
      \item Diffusion destroys the data on purpose, then learns the way back.
      \item Adding noise naively makes the variance explode and never reaches a fixed
        distribution.
      \item Shrinking the mean by $\sqrt{1-\beta}$ fixes it: the variance-preserving
        process lands on $\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\id)$.
      \item $\xv_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\xv_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\eps$ gives any
        step in one shot.
    \end{enumerate}
    \column{0.48\textwidth}
    \vskip 12pt
    \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\small
      \textbf{Next:} we know how to break an image, and we know that "undo one step" is
      the wrong training target.
      \vskip 4pt
      L28 starts from maximum likelihood, follows the algebra where it goes, and lands on
      a loss function that fits on one line - and is just a mean squared error.}}
    \vskip 5pt
    \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!12}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      \textbf{One prompt to hold onto:} \emph{"a lone tree standing in the middle of a
      desert, casting a long shadow on the sand."} We come back to it in every remaining
      lecture - it goes blurry in L29, loses its tree in L30, and gets cheap in L31.}}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}
