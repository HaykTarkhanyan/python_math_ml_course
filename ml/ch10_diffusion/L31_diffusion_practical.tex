\begin{frame}{Let us count what we have built}
  \small
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.54\textwidth}
    A guided sample at $512\times512$ costs:
    \vskip 4pt
    \begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{2pt}
      \item $25$ sampling steps (DDIM, L29)
      \item $\times\ 2$ forward passes each, for guidance (L30)
      \item $=\ 50$ passes of a network operating on $786{,}432$ numbers
    \end{itemize}

      \vskip 4pt

  \end{columns}

\section{Latent diffusion}

\begin{frame}[plain]
  \vfill
  \begin{center}
    {\Large\color{popblue}\textbf{Latent diffusion}}\\[0.9em]
    \begin{minipage}{0.82\textwidth}\centering\small
      The chapter that looked like a detour turns out to be load-bearing.
    \end{minipage}
  \end{center}
  \vfill
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{You already know the punchline}
  \small
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.55\textwidth}
    In L23 we closed the autoencoder chapter with a frame titled \emph{"the VAE is inside
    modern image generators"}, and this claim:
    \vskip 5pt
    \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\small
      The VAE compresses a $512\times512\times3$ image to $64\times64\times4$ - about
      $48\times$ fewer numbers. The diffusion model works in \emph{that}, and the VAE
      decoder paints the final image.
      \vskip 4pt
      \textbf{The VAE did not lose to diffusion. It became the compression layer inside
      it.}}}
    \column{0.45\textwidth}
    \vskip 6pt
    \footnotesize
    At the time that was a promise. Now you have everything needed to see why it works, and
    why it is the difference between a research result and a product.
    \vskip 6pt
    \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      \textbf{Latent diffusion} (Rombach et al., 2022): run the entire process of L27-L30
      inside the autoencoder's latent space instead of on pixels.
      \vskip 3pt
      Nothing about the maths changes. Only where it happens.}}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What L23 did not tell you: the latent is an image}
  \small
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{latent_spatial.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vskip -3pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \footnotesize
    The MNIST autoencoder in L22 squeezed a digit to \textbf{two numbers} - a flat code
    with no geometry. Latent diffusion does not do that.
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \footnotesize
    Its latent is a small \textbf{feature map}: neighbouring cells still correspond to
    neighbouring patches of the picture.
  \end{columns}
  \vskip 4pt
  \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.985\linewidth}{\footnotesize
    \textbf{That is the whole trick.} Because the latent keeps its spatial layout,
    convolutions and cross-attention still mean something inside it - so the UNet from L28
    works unchanged. Compress to a flat vector and none of it would.}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Where the saving actually comes from}
  \small
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.86\linewidth]{cost_breakdown.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vskip -3pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      Element count drops $48\times$. That is the number usually quoted, and it is the
      \emph{smaller} of the two effects.}}
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      Attention cost grows with the \textbf{square} of the number of positions. Going from
      $262{,}144$ positions to $4{,}096$ cuts it by about $\mathbf{4096\times}$.}}
  \end{columns}
  \vskip 4pt
  \footnotesize
  \textcolor{gray}{This is why full-resolution pixel-space attention was never really on
  the table, and why the encoder is not just an optimization but an enabler.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The full picture, and a loose end}
  \small
  \begin{center}
    \begin{tikzpicture}[every node/.style={font=\scriptsize}, node distance=0.55cm]
      \node[draw, rounded corners, fill=armorange!16, minimum height=0.75cm, text width=1.5cm, align=center] (txt) {prompt};

    \vskip 4pt
    The decoder runs \textbf{once}, at the very end.
    \column{0.45\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      \textbf{A loose end from L27.} The "pure noise" video in our opening slide did not
      look like television static. Now you know why: it was noise \emph{in the latent},
      pushed through the decoder.}}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\section{Flow matching}

\begin{frame}[plain]
  \vfill
  \begin{center}
    {\Large\color{popblue}\textbf{Flow matching}}\\[0.9em]
    \begin{minipage}{0.82\textwidth}\centering\small
      If the path is going to be deterministic, why not make it straight?
    \end{minipage}
  \end{center}
  \vfill
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Straighten the path}
  \small
  DDIM showed the reverse process can be a deterministic trajectory from noise to data.
  But it is a \textbf{curved} one - it inherits its shape from the noise schedule, and
  curvature is what forces many small steps.
  \vskip 5pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.52\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      \textbf{Flow matching} (Lipman et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2022) trains the network to
      predict a \textbf{velocity} - which way and how fast to move - rather than the noise,
      and picks a target path that is as close to a straight line as possible.}}
    \column{0.48\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      A straight path can be crossed in a few large steps without much error. Fewer steps,
      same quality.}}
  \end{columns}
  \vskip 6pt
  \footnotesize
  The training loop barely changes: sample a time, interpolate between noise and data,
  regress onto the target velocity. Still a mean squared error, still no adversarial game.
  \vskip 4pt
  \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!12}{\parbox{0.985\linewidth}{\footnotesize
    \textbf{This is not a historical footnote.} Stable Diffusion 3, Flux and Wan are all
    flow-matching models. If you read a 2025-onward paper, expect this formulation rather
    than the DDPM one - which is exactly why we derived DDPM first: flow matching is the
    same skeleton with a different target.}}
\end{frame}

\section{Video}

\begin{frame}[plain]
  \vfill
  \begin{center}
    {\Large\color{popblue}\textbf{Video}}\\[0.9em]
    \begin{minipage}{0.82\textwidth}\centering\small
      One more axis on the tensor, and remarkably little else.
    \end{minipage}
  \end{center}
  \vfill
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Video is the same loop with another dimension}
  \small
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    An image is $H\times W\times C$. A video is $F\times H\times W\times C$. Noise the whole
    tensor, denoise the whole tensor.
    \vskip 5pt
    \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      Everything transfers: the closed-form forward process, $\mathcal{L}_{\text{simple}}$,
      the sampler, cross-attention on the prompt, classifier-free guidance.}}
    \vskip 5pt
    \footnotesize
    What changes is that the network must attend \textbf{across frames} as well as within
    them - otherwise each frame is a plausible image and the sequence flickers. That is
    where the engineering effort goes, and it is why video models are so much more
    expensive.
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \begin{center}
      \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{wl_negative_prompt.jpg}\\[2pt]
      {\scriptsize\color{gray} Welch Labs / 3Blue1Brown (2025)}
    \end{center}
    \vskip -2pt
    \footnotesize
    Wan 2.1's shipped negative prompt is a list of everything that tends to go wrong:
    extra fingers, deformed limbs, cluttered backgrounds, people walking backwards.
    \vskip 3pt
    \textcolor{gray}{A reminder that a large part of production quality is guidance
    engineering, not architecture.}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\section{Where this leaves us}

\begin{frame}[plain]
  \vfill
  \begin{center}
    {\Large\color{popblue}\textbf{Where this leaves us}}\\[0.9em]
    \begin{minipage}{0.82\textwidth}\centering\small
      The trade the field made, and the parts that are not technical.
    \end{minipage}
  \end{center}
  \vfill
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The generative landscape}
  \small
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.84\linewidth]{gen_landscape.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vskip -4pt
  \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.985\linewidth}{\footnotesize
    L23 compared these families on \emph{what they are}. This is the axis that explains the
    \textbf{history}: VAEs and GANs generate in one pass, diffusion needed a thousand and
    bought quality with them - then DDIM and flow matching won most of that cost back
    without giving up the quality.}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why images, and not text?}
  \small
  A fair question after a whole chapter on transformers: language models generate one token
  at a time, image models denoise. Why not diffuse text?
  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.52\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      Everything in L27-L28 rests on \textbf{continuous} data: add a little Gaussian noise,
      get something slightly different but still meaningful.
      \vskip 3pt
      Tokens are \textbf{discrete}. "Slightly noisy" is not a word. There is no
      halfway point between \emph{cat} and \emph{dog}.}}
    \column{0.48\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!12}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      There is active work on discrete diffusion and on diffusing in embedding space, and
      some genuinely promising results. But it is not why your chatbot works.
      \vskip 3pt
      \textbf{Continuous data: diffusion. Discrete sequences: autoregression.} That split
      still holds.}}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What it actually costs}
  \small
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.52\textwidth}
    Our thesis has been "diffusion buys quality with compute". It is worth putting a number
    on that.
    \vskip 5pt
    \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      Stable Diffusion 1.x was reported by its authors as roughly \textbf{150{,}000
      A100-hours} of training - about $17$ GPU-years, run in parallel.
      \vskip 3pt
      Inference is cheap; \textbf{training is not}, and it is concentrated in very few
      hands.}}
    \column{0.48\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      Which is also why open weights matter so much here. Almost nobody can train one of
      these; a great many people can fine-tune, sample from, and study one.
      \vskip 3pt
      Everything we did this chapter used a released model or a toy we trained in minutes.}}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The parts that are not technical}
  \small
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!12}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      \textbf{Where the data came from.} These models are trained on hundreds of millions
      of images scraped from the web, largely without the consent of the people who made
      them. Whether that is fair use is being litigated right now, in several countries,
      with no settled answer.}}
    \vskip 5pt
    \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      \textbf{What they are used for.} The same guidance machinery that puts a tree in a
      desert puts a real person in a video they were never in. Detection is losing to
      generation.}}
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      \textbf{Why this is in a technical lecture.} You now know how these systems work
      well enough to build one. That is exactly the point at which the question stops
      being someone else's.
      \vskip 4pt
      I am not going to tell you what to conclude. I am telling you not to skip the
      question.}}
    \vskip 5pt
    \footnotesize
    \textcolor{gray}{Watermarking, provenance standards and dataset opt-outs all exist and
    are all partial. This is unsettled, and you will be working in it.}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Check yourself}
  \small
  There is no homework for this chapter, so here is how to find out whether it landed. If
  you can answer these without looking, you have it.
  \vskip 5pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \footnotesize
    \begin{enumerate}\setlength\itemsep{3pt}
      \item Why does the forward process shrink the mean by $\sqrt{1-\beta_t}$ instead of
        just adding noise?
      \item The training loss is $\|\eps-\eps_{\thetav}\|^2$. Where did the likelihood go?
      \item The sampler removes noise, then adds fresh noise. Why is that not a
        contradiction?
      \item DDIM has no noise term at all. Why does \emph{it} not blur?
    \end{enumerate}
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \footnotesize
    \begin{enumerate}\setlength\itemsep{3pt}
      \setcounter{enumi}{4}
      \item In classifier-free guidance, what does $\alpha=1$ do, and why is that the
        neutral value rather than $\alpha=0$?
      \item Conditioning already tells the model the prompt. So what is guidance for?
      \item Why does latent diffusion need the latent to be \textbf{spatial}?
      \item Where does the $4096\times$ come from, given the latent is only $48\times$
        smaller?
    \end{enumerate}
  \end{columns}
  \vskip 6pt
  \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.985\linewidth}{\footnotesize
    If you want to go further than answering them: take the sampling loop from L29, get it
    running on any small dataset, then deliberately break it in the three ways from that
    lecture's failure slide. You will learn more from the broken runs than the working one.}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The chapter, end to end}
  \small
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.56\textwidth}
    \begin{description}\footnotesize\setlength\itemsep{3pt}
      \item[L27] Destroy an image in closed form, in a way that reliably lands on
        $\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\id)$.
      \item[L28] Start from maximum likelihood, follow the algebra, arrive at
        $\|\eps-\eps_{\thetav}\|^2$.
      \item[L29] Sample - and discover the added noise is keeping the input at the level
        the network was trained for. Then remove it properly, with DDIM.
      \item[L30] Put a prompt in with cross-attention, and make the model obey it with
        classifier-free guidance.
      \item[L31] Do all of it in a compressed latent, and it fits on a laptop.
    \end{description}
    \column{0.44\textwidth}
    \vskip 6pt
    \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\small
      Five lectures ago, a text-to-video model was a black box.
      \vskip 4pt
      There is no step in it now that you have not seen derived, drawn, or run.}}
    \vskip 6pt
    \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      And the thing worth carrying forward is not DDPM specifically. It is the move:
      \textbf{when a distribution is impossible to model directly, find a process that
      destroys it in a way you control, and learn to run that process backwards.}}}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}
