\begin{frame}{Where we stand}
  \small
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.52\textwidth}
    Last lecture we built the forward process, and it has \textbf{no learned parameters}:
    \[ \xv_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,\xv_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\ \eps \]
    \vskip 4pt
    We also found that the obvious training target - predict $\xv_{t-1}$ from $\xv_t$ -
    is nearly pure noise and trains badly.
    \column{0.48\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\small
      \textbf{Today:} start from the one principle we always start from - maximum
      likelihood - and follow the algebra without shortcuts.
      \vskip 4pt
      It ends somewhere absurd: a plain mean squared error against the noise we
      ourselves added.}}
  \end{columns}
  \vskip 6pt
  \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!12}{\parbox{0.985\linewidth}{\footnotesize
    \textbf{Notation, all of it, from L27.} $\beta_t$ is the noise added at step $t$;
    $\alpha_t = 1-\beta_t$ is the signal kept at that step; $\bar\alpha_t =
    \prod_{s\le t}\alpha_s$ is the signal kept in total, so
    $\bar\alpha_t = \alpha_t\,\bar\alpha_{t-1}$.
    \textbf{All three appear today} - the per-step ones show up as soon as we write a
    single reverse step, and that last identity is what makes the algebra collapse.}}
  \vskip 6pt
  \footnotesize
  \textcolor{gray}{This is the most mathematical lecture of the chapter. Every step is
  either something you have seen in L23, or one line of algebra.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Where this ends up (so you can stop worrying about it)}
  \small
  Long derivations are easier to follow when you already know the destination. So here it
  is, before we start:
  \vskip 4pt
  \[
    \boxed{\ \mathcal{L} = \Big\|\ \eps - \eps_{\thetav}(\xv_t,\ t)\ \Big\|^{2}\ }
  \]
  \vskip -2pt
  \begin{center}\footnotesize
    Add noise to a training image. Ask the network which noise you added. Square the error.
  \end{center}
  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      That is the entire training objective. No adversarial game, no sampling from the
      model during training, no likelihood evaluation.}}
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!12}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      \textbf{So why spend a lecture deriving it?} Because "predict the noise" is not
      obviously the right thing to do, and the derivation is what tells us it is - plus
      \emph{exactly} which approximations we accepted along the way. Two of them come back
      to bite us in L29.}}
  \end{columns}
  \vskip 5pt
  \footnotesize
  \textcolor{gray}{If you get lost in the middle, come back to this slide. Everything
  between here and frame 21 is a road from maximum likelihood to that one line.}
\end{frame}

\section{Maximum likelihood, and why it fails}

\begin{frame}[plain]
  \vfill
  \begin{center}
    {\Large\color{popblue}\textbf{Maximum likelihood, and why it fails}}\\[0.9em]
    \begin{minipage}{0.82\textwidth}\centering\small
      The right objective, in a form nobody can compute.
    \end{minipage}
  \end{center}
  \vfill
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The objective we always want}
  \small
  Fit $\thetav$ so that real images are likely under our model. As in L23, minimize the
  negative log-likelihood:
  \[ \min_{\thetav}\ -\log p_{\thetav}(\xv_0) \]
  \vskip 4pt
  Our model generates by running the whole reverse chain, so $p_{\thetav}$ is defined
  over the entire path $\xv_{0:T}$:
  \[
    p_{\thetav}(\xv_{0:T}) = p(\xv_T)\prod_{t=1}^{T} p_{\thetav}(\xv_{t-1}\mid \xv_t),
    \qquad p(\xv_T) = \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\id)
  \]
  \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.985\linewidth}{\footnotesize
    We only care about $\xv_0$, the finished image. So to get $p_{\thetav}(\xv_0)$ we must
    \textbf{marginalize away} every intermediate step $\xv_1,\dots,\xv_T$.}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Marginalizing is hopeless}
  \small
  \[
    p_{\thetav}(\xv_0) = \int p_{\thetav}(\xv_{0:T})\ d\xv_{1:T}
  \]
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.46\textwidth}
    \begin{center}
      \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{dp_paths.jpg}\\[2pt]
      {\scriptsize\color{gray} Deepia (2025)}
    \end{center}
    \column{0.54\textwidth}
    \vskip 4pt
    Read the integral literally: to score \emph{one} image, sum over \textbf{every possible
    route} from noise to that image.
    \vskip 6pt
    \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\small
      There are unimaginably many such paths. The integral is \textbf{intractable} - not
      slow, not approximate, simply not computable.}}
    \vskip 6pt
    \footnotesize
    This is exactly the wall L23 hit with the VAE. And we get past it the same way.
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\section{A bound we can actually optimize}

\begin{frame}[plain]
  \vfill
  \begin{center}
    {\Large\color{popblue}\textbf{A bound we can actually optimize}}\\[0.9em]
    \begin{minipage}{0.82\textwidth}\centering\small
      If you cannot compute the thing, compute something that hugs it from below.
    \end{minipage}
  \end{center}
  \vfill
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The same trick as L23: multiply by one}
  \small
  The forward process $q$ is something we know exactly. Insert it, top and bottom:
  \[
    p_{\thetav}(\xv_0)
    = \int q(\xv_{1:T}\mid \xv_0)\ \frac{p_{\thetav}(\xv_{0:T})}{q(\xv_{1:T}\mid \xv_0)}\ d\xv_{1:T}
    = \E_{q}\!\left[\frac{p_{\thetav}(\xv_{0:T})}{q(\xv_{1:T}\mid \xv_0)}\right]
  \]
  \vskip 4pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      Nothing changed - we multiplied by $q/q$. But the intractable integral is now an
      \textbf{expectation under a distribution we can sample from}, which is progress.}}
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      \textbf{Recognize this.} It is move one of the VAE derivation in L23. The variable
      names changed; the idea did not.}}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The bound, derived the way we did it in L23}
  \small
  Take logs and split off a divergence term. Exactly as in L23:
  \[
    \log p_{\thetav}(\xv_0)
    \;=\;
    \underbrace{\E_q\!\left[\log \frac{p_{\thetav}(\xv_{0:T})}{q(\xv_{1:T}\mid \xv_0)}\right]}_{\displaystyle \text{ELBO}}
    \;+\;
    \underbrace{D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(q(\xv_{1:T}\mid \xv_0)\ \|\ p_{\thetav}(\xv_{1:T}\mid \xv_0)\right)}_{\displaystyle \ \geq\ 0\ \text{always}}
  \]
  \vskip 4pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.55\textwidth}
    A KL divergence is never negative. Drop it and the equality becomes an inequality:
    \[ \log p_{\thetav}(\xv_0) \ \geq\ \text{ELBO} \]
    \footnotesize
    So maximizing the ELBO pushes up a \emph{floor} underneath the log-likelihood. The
    gap we threw away is precisely how wrong our reverse process is.
    \column{0.45\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!12}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      You may see this bound obtained in one line from \textbf{Jensen's inequality}
      instead (that is how the DDPM paper writes it, and how you met Jensen in the
      probability module).
      \vskip 3pt
      Same bound, same place. We use the decomposition because it also tells us
      \emph{what} we gave up.}}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What the bound looks like once you expand it}
  \small
  Substituting the two Markov factorizations and grinding through Bayes' rule - the DDPM
  paper does this in its appendix - the ELBO turns into:
  \[
    -\text{ELBO} = \E_q\Bigg[
      \underbrace{D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q(\xv_T\mid \xv_0)\,\|\,p(\xv_T)\big)}_{\text{(1) no }\thetav}
      + \sum_{t>1}\underbrace{D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q(\xv_{t-1}\mid \xv_t,\xv_0)\,\|\,p_{\thetav}(\xv_{t-1}\mid \xv_t)\big)}_{\text{(2) the real work}}
      - \underbrace{\log p_{\thetav}(\xv_0\mid \xv_1)}_{\text{(3) reconstruction}}
    \Bigg]
  \]
  \vskip 4pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.33\textwidth}
    \footnotesize
    \textbf{(1)} compares "fully noised data" with $\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\id)$. No
    parameters in it. \textbf{Drop.}
    \column{0.33\textwidth}
    \footnotesize
    \textbf{(3)} the very last denoising step, from $\xv_1$ to $\xv_0$. Almost no noise
    is left; contributes little. \textbf{Drop.}
    \column{0.34\textwidth}
    \footnotesize
    \textbf{(2)} a sum of $T-1$ KL divergences. Everything we care about is here.
  \end{columns}
  \vskip 5pt
  \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.985\linewidth}{\footnotesize
    The whole training objective is: \textbf{make each learned reverse step match some
    reference reverse step}. Next question - what is that reference?}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{A reminder of what KL measures}
  \small
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.92\linewidth]{kl_gaussians.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vskip -2pt
  \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.985\linewidth}{\footnotesize
    $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\,\|\,p)$ is a measure of how far apart two distributions are: zero
    exactly when they are identical, larger as they separate. It is not symmetric and it
    is not a metric, but here it behaves like one - \textbf{because we are about to fix
    both variances to be equal.}}}
\end{frame}

\section{The true posterior}

\begin{frame}[plain]
  \vfill
  \begin{center}
    {\Large\color{popblue}\textbf{The true posterior}}\\[0.9em]
    \begin{minipage}{0.82\textwidth}\centering\small
      There is an exact answer. It just cheats.
    \end{minipage}
  \end{center}
  \vfill
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The exact reverse step, in closed form}
  \small
  The reference distribution inside term (2) is
  $q(\xv_{t-1}\mid \xv_t, \xv_0)$ - the reverse step \emph{given the clean image}. Unlike
  the reverse process in general, this one has an exact formula:
  \[
    q(\xv_{t-1}\mid \xv_t, \xv_0) = \mathcal{N}\!\big(\tilde{\bm\mu}_t(\xv_t,\xv_0),\ \tilde\beta_t \id\big)
  \]
  \vskip -2pt
  \[
    \tilde{\bm\mu}_t = \frac{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,\beta_t}{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\xv_0
      \;+\; \frac{\sqrt{\alpha_t}\,(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1})}{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\xv_t,
    \qquad
    \tilde\beta_t = \frac{1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}}{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\beta_t
  \]
  \vskip 4pt
  \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.985\linewidth}{\footnotesize
    Both forward Gaussians are known, so Bayes' rule gives this exactly. It is a Gaussian
    whose mean is a weighted blend of \textbf{where you are} ($\xv_t$) and \textbf{where
    you came from} ($\xv_0$).}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{So why do we need a network at all?}
  \small
  \textbf{Predict first:} we just wrote down the exact reverse step. Why not simply use it
  to generate images?
  \vskip 5pt
  \pause
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\small
      Because it needs $\xv_0$ - and at generation time $\xv_0$ is \textbf{exactly what we
      are trying to produce}. Using it would be circular.}}
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\small
      But during \textbf{training} we do have $\xv_0$: it is the training image. So we can
      use the true posterior as a \textbf{teacher}, and train the network to imitate it
      from $\xv_t$ alone.}}
  \end{columns}
  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{center}\small
    Training: teacher sees $\xv_0$. \quad Generation: student does not. \quad
    \textcolor{paramgreen}{\textbf{That gap is the entire job.}}
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Choosing the student's form}
  \small
  We make $p_{\thetav}$ Gaussian too - the reverse of a Gaussian Markov chain is Gaussian
  for small steps, and it keeps the algebra finite:
  \[
    p_{\thetav}(\xv_{t-1}\mid \xv_t) = \mathcal{N}\!\big(\bm\mu_{\thetav}(\xv_t,t),\ \sigma_t^2\id\big)
  \]
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!12}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      \textbf{And we fix the variance.} $\sigma_t^2$ is set from the schedule, not learned.
      So the network's only job is the \textbf{mean}.
      \vskip 3pt
      Concretely, \textbf{take $\sigma_t^2 = \beta_t$} - the simpler of the two choices in
      Ho et al.\ (2020). Their other choice is $\tilde\beta_t$ from the previous slide;
      both work, and you need one of them the moment you actually sample.}}
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \footnotesize
    Two Gaussians, same fixed variance, different means. The KL between them collapses to
    something very simple.
  \end{columns}
  \vskip 6pt
  \[
    D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q(\xv_{t-1}\mid \xv_t,\xv_0)\,\|\,p_{\thetav}(\xv_{t-1}\mid \xv_t)\big)
    \;=\;
    \frac{1}{2\sigma_t^2}\,\big\|\tilde{\bm\mu}_t - \bm\mu_{\thetav}(\xv_t,t)\big\|^2
  \]
  \vskip 2pt
  \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.985\linewidth}{\footnotesize
    Keep the $\tfrac{1}{2\sigma_t^2}$ in view - it comes back at the end and we will
    deliberately throw it away. \textbf{The objective is now a squared distance between
    two means.}}}
\end{frame}

\section{Reparameterization: the collapse}

\begin{frame}[plain]
  \vfill
  \begin{center}
    {\Large\color{popblue}\textbf{Reparameterization: the collapse}}\\[0.9em]
    \begin{minipage}{0.82\textwidth}\centering\small
      Four lines of algebra turn a variational bound into a mean squared error.
    \end{minipage}
  \end{center}
  \vfill
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The teacher's mean is in awkward coordinates}
  \small
  \[
    \tilde{\bm\mu}_t = \frac{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,\beta_t}{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\xv_0
      + \frac{\sqrt{\alpha_t}(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1})}{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\xv_t
  \]
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    It depends on $\xv_0$, which the student never sees. Asking the network to match this
    directly means asking it to reconstruct $\xv_0$.
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      \textbf{The idea:} rewrite $\tilde{\bm\mu}_t$ in terms of things the student
      \emph{does} see. It sees $\xv_t$. So express everything with $\xv_t$ and the noise.}}
  \end{columns}
  \vskip 8pt
  Recall the forward closed form from L27 - the \textbf{reparameterization trick}, the
  same one that made the VAE trainable:
  \[
    \xv_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,\xv_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\ \eps
    \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
    \xv_0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}}\Big(\xv_t - \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\ \eps\Big)
  \]
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Substituting, and watching it simplify}
  \small
  Put that expression for $\xv_0$ into $\tilde{\bm\mu}_t$. The $\xv_t$ terms combine, and
  after simplification everything collapses to:
  \[
    \boxed{\ \tilde{\bm\mu}_t
      = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\left(\xv_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}}\ \eps\right)}
  \]
  \vskip 4pt
  \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.985\linewidth}{\footnotesize
    Look at what survived. The teacher's mean is $\xv_t$ - which the student already has -
    \textbf{minus a multiple of the noise $\eps$}. The only unknown left in the whole
    expression is $\eps$.}}
  \vskip 6pt
  So give the student the same shape, with a network in place of the unknown noise:
  \[
    \bm\mu_{\thetav}(\xv_t,t)
      = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\left(\xv_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}}\ \eps_{\thetav}(\xv_t,t)\right)
  \]
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The cancellation}
  \small
  Now subtract the two means. Everything matches except one factor:
  \[
    \tilde{\bm\mu}_t - \bm\mu_{\thetav}(\xv_t,t)
    = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\cdot\frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}}
      \Big(\eps_{\thetav}(\xv_t,t) - \eps\Big)
  \]
  \vskip -2pt
  Square it, put back the $\tfrac{1}{2\sigma_t^2}$, and the $t$-th term of the objective is
  \[
    \frac{\beta_t^{2}}{2\sigma_t^{2}\,\alpha_t\,(1-\bar\alpha_t)}
    \Big\|\ \eps - \eps_{\thetav}(\xv_t,t)\ \Big\|^{2}
  \]
  \vskip 4pt
  \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.985\linewidth}{\small
    \textbf{The network's job is to look at a noisy image and say which noise was added.}
    Every $\xv_t$ cancelled. What began as an intractable likelihood over all paths is a
    squared error against a vector we drew ourselves.}}
\end{frame}

\section{From objective to practice}

\begin{frame}[plain]
  \vfill
  \begin{center}
    {\Large\color{popblue}\textbf{From objective to practice}}\\[0.9em]
    \begin{minipage}{0.82\textwidth}\centering\small
      Two more simplifications. One is free, one is not.
    \end{minipage}
  \end{center}
  \vfill
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Simplification 1: stop summing over $t$}
  \small
  The objective sums $T-1$ terms per training example, with $T$ around $1000$. Instead,
  \textbf{sample one $t$ uniformly per example}:
  \[
    \E_{t\sim \mathcal{U}\{1,\dots,T\}}\big[\,\cdot\,\big]
    \quad\text{estimated with a single draw}
  \]
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      \textbf{This one is free.} It is an unbiased Monte-Carlo estimate of the same
      objective. Over many minibatches it converges to the full sum, at $1/T$ the cost.}}
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \footnotesize
    This is why the closed-form $\xv_t$ from L27 mattered so much: a random $t$ is
    useless if reaching it costs $t$ simulation steps.
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Simplification 2: throw away the weights}
  \small
  Each term carries the prefactor
  $\tfrac{\beta_t^2}{2\sigma_t^2\alpha_t(1-\bar\alpha_t)}$. Ho et al.\ set it to $1$:
  \[
    \boxed{\ \mathcal{L}_{\text{simple}}
      = \E_{t,\ \xv_0,\ \eps}
      \Big\|\ \eps - \eps_{\thetav}\big(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\xv_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\eps,\ t\big)\Big\|^{2}\ }
  \]
  \vskip 4pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      \textbf{This one is not free.} With the weights gone, $\mathcal{L}_{\text{simple}}$
      is \textbf{no longer the ELBO}. We are no longer maximizing a likelihood bound.}}
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!12}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      And it is \textbf{better} - for samples. Ho et al.\ report visibly better images and
      \emph{worse} likelihood. The reweighting quietly tells the model to care more about
      the noisy steps, which is where perceptual quality is decided.}}
  \end{columns}
  \vskip 5pt
  \footnotesize
  \textcolor{gray}{We take the trade deliberately. In L29 we come back and ask how anyone
  measures "better images" in the first place.}
\end{frame}

\section{It is twelve lines of code}

\begin{frame}[plain]
  \vfill
  \begin{center}
    {\Large\color{popblue}\textbf{It is twelve lines of code}}\\[0.9em]
    \begin{minipage}{0.82\textwidth}\centering\small
      The payoff for sitting through the algebra.
    \end{minipage}
  \end{center}
  \vfill
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[fragile]{The training loop, in full}
  \small
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.56\textwidth}
\begin{semiverbatim}\scriptsize
\textcolor{gray}{# betas: the schedule. abar = cumprod(1 - betas)}
for step in range(n_steps):
    x0  = next_batch()
    t   = randint(0, T, size=len(x0))
    eps = randn_like(x0)

    a  = abar[t]
    xt = sqrt(a)*x0 + sqrt(1 - a)*eps

    loss = mse(eps, model(xt, t))

    loss.backward()
    opt.step(); opt.zero\_grad()
\end{semiverbatim}
    \column{0.44\textwidth}
    \vskip 10pt
    \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\small
      That is the entire derivation, implemented. Lines 7-8 are the L27 closed form; line
      10 is $\mathcal{L}_{\text{simple}}$.
      \vskip 4pt
      \textbf{No adversarial game, no discriminator, no balancing act.} Just a regression
      onto noise.}}
    \vskip 6pt
    \footnotesize
    \textcolor{gray}{Compare with training a GAN, where the loss going down tells you
    almost nothing about whether the samples are improving.}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What is $\eps_{\thetav}$ actually?}
  \small
  We have said "a network" for two lectures. It has to satisfy three constraints:
  \vskip 4pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.55\textwidth}
    \footnotesize
    \begin{enumerate}\setlength\itemsep{2pt}
      \item \textbf{Image in, image out} at the same resolution - the output $\eps$ has the
        shape of the input.
      \item \textbf{Conditioned on $t$} - one network must serve every noise level.
      \item Later: \textbf{a place to inject text}.
    \end{enumerate}
    \vskip 6pt
    The standard answer is a \textbf{UNet}: downsample through convolutions, process, upsample
    back, with \textbf{skip connections} joining matching resolutions so fine detail is not
    lost in the bottleneck.
    \column{0.45\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      \textbf{You have met every piece:}
      \begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{1pt}
        \item convolutions and transposed convolutions - L16, L19
        \item skip connections - L17 (ResNet)
        \item encoder-decoder with a bottleneck - L22
        \item sinusoidal position encoding, reused here for $t$ - ch9
      \end{itemize}}}
    \vskip 4pt
    \footnotesize
    \textcolor{gray}{The digits model above is a miniature of exactly this.}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why "just an MSE" is a big deal}
  \small
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      \textbf{Recall L23b.} Training a GAN is a two-player game: the generator improves
      against a discriminator that is itself moving.
      \begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{1pt}
        \item no stationary loss to watch - we saw both curves wander and cross
        \item \textbf{mode collapse} - visible at step 500 of our own run
        \item no reliable signal for when to stop; discriminator accuracy never settled
      \end{itemize}}}
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      \textbf{Training a diffusion model} is a regression onto a fixed target.
      \begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{1pt}
        \item the loss is stationary and interpretable
        \item more compute reliably helps
        \item essentially no instability to manage
      \end{itemize}}}
    \vskip 6pt
    \footnotesize
    This stability, more than any single quality result, is why the field switched.
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Recap}
  \small
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.54\textwidth}
    \begin{enumerate}\setlength\itemsep{2pt}
      \item Maximum likelihood needs an integral over all paths: intractable.
      \item Insert $q/q$, drop a non-negative KL, get the ELBO - the L23 move.
      \item Expanded, it is a sum of KLs against the \textbf{true posterior}, which is
        exact but needs $\xv_0$.
      \item Fix the variance: KL becomes a squared distance of means.
      \item Reparameterize; the $\xv_t$ terms cancel; what is left is
        $\|\eps-\eps_{\thetav}\|^2$.
      \item Sample one $t$ (free), drop the weights (not free, but better samples).
    \end{enumerate}
    \column{0.46\textwidth}
    \vskip 6pt
    \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\small
      \textbf{Next:} we can train. We cannot yet \emph{generate} - running the chain
      backwards has a surprise in it.
      \vskip 4pt
      L29 shows the sampler, and asks why it insists on adding fresh random noise at every
      step when its whole job is removing noise.}}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}
