\begin{frame}{Where we left off}
  From L13b: \textbf{Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection} (\textbf{UMAP}) builds a
  \textbf{fuzzy nearest-neighbor graph}, then finds a 2-D layout whose graph matches. Faster than
  t-SNE, keeps more global structure. That was \emph{what it gives you}.
  \vfill
  One thing we did not say, and it is the reason UMAP exists:
  \vskip 4pt
  \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.97\linewidth}{\small
    t-SNE minimizes $\mathrm{KL}(P\|Q) = \sum p_{ij}\log\frac{p_{ij}}{q_{ij}}$. Look at what
    that punishes. If $p_{ij}$ is \textbf{large} and $q_{ij}$ small --- true neighbors torn
    apart --- the term blows up. But if $p_{ij}$ is \textbf{near zero}, the whole term is near
    zero \emph{whatever $q_{ij}$ does}. Collapsing two distant points on top of each other is
    \textbf{free}.}}
  \vskip 6pt
  So t-SNE guards local structure and leaves global structure unprotected. UMAP's loss pays for
  both. Everything else today is machinery in service of that.
\end{frame}

\section{The manifold assumption}
\sectiontransition{The manifold assumption}{What UMAP believes about your data before it looks
at any of it.}

\begin{frame}{What is a manifold?}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.54\textwidth}
    A \textbf{manifold} is a space that \emph{locally} looks like ordinary flat space, even when
    its \emph{global} shape is curved.
    \vskip 5pt
    \textbf{Earth's surface.} Globally a sphere. But the room you are in is flat, and a street map
    of Yerevan needs no curvature correction.
    \vskip 5pt
    \textbf{Face photos.} A $64\times64$ photo is 4096 numbers, but real faces vary along a few
    knobs --- pose, lighting, expression. The photos live on a thin, curved sheet inside that
    4096-dimensional space.
    \vskip 5pt
    \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\scriptsize
      \textbf{The assumption:} the data is sampled \emph{uniformly} from a manifold --- with
      respect to a \textbf{locally varying} metric.}}
    \column{0.46\textwidth}
    \begin{center}
      \begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.82]
        \draw[armblue!70, very thick, fill=armblue!12]
          plot[smooth cycle, tension=0.75] coordinates {
            (0,0.8) (0.8,0) (2.2,0.1) (3.2,0.6) (3.4,1.6) (2.8,2.6) (1.5,2.8) (0.3,2.2)};
        \draw[armblue!25, thick]
          plot[smooth, tension=0.9] coordinates {(0.4,0.5) (1.2,0.3) (2.0,0.4) (2.8,0.7)};
        \draw[armblue!20, thick]
          plot[smooth, tension=0.9] coordinates {(0.3,1.5) (1.0,1.2) (1.8,1.3) (2.6,1.6)};
        \draw[armred, very thick, fill=armred!30, fill opacity=0.7]
          (2.2,0.6) -- (3.0,0.7) -- (2.95,1.2) -- (2.15,1.1) -- cycle;
        \draw[armred, thick, ->] (3.9,1.7) -- (3.05,1.15);
        \node[armred, font=\scriptsize, right] at (3.7,1.9) {locally flat};
        \foreach \p in {(0.6,0.7), (1.3,0.4), (2.5,0.85), (1.0,1.5),
                        (1.8,1.8), (2.6,2.0), (0.7,2.0), (1.5,2.4)}
          \fill[black] \p circle (1.8pt);
        \node[gray, font=\scriptsize, align=center] at (1.7,-0.45)
          {a 2-D sheet living in\\a high-dimensional space};
      \end{tikzpicture}
    \end{center}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why ``locally varying'' is the whole trick}
  \textbf{Predict-first:} your data is dense in one region and sparse in another. If you insist
  the sampling is \emph{uniform} everywhere, what must you conclude about distance?
  \pause
  \vskip 6pt
  That your ruler is wrong --- and wrong \textbf{differently in different places}.
  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.48\textwidth}
    In a \textbf{sparse} region, points are far apart in raw distance. If the sampling is really
    uniform, those gaps must be \emph{shrunk}: the neighbors are closer than they look.
    \column{0.48\textwidth}
    In a \textbf{dense} region, points are packed together. Those gaps must be \emph{stretched}:
    the neighbors are farther than they look.
  \end{columns}
  \vfill
  \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.97\linewidth}{\small
    So each point gets its \textbf{own} distance scale. That is not a hack bolted on for
    robustness --- it falls straight out of the uniformity assumption, and it is exactly what
    $\rho_i$ and $\sigma_i$ compute in the next section. It is also why UMAP copes with varying
    density where a fixed-radius neighborhood does not.}}
\end{frame}

\section{Step 1: building the fuzzy graph}
\sectiontransition{Step 1 of 2}{Turn the data into a weighted graph, using a different ruler at
every point.}

\begin{frame}{The running toy}
  Six points, two clusters, $k=3$ neighbors. Every number on the next five frames is computed
  from these coordinates.
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.86\textwidth]{fig/umap_toy_points.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vskip -2pt
  \footnotesize Within a cluster, distances are around $0.83$ to $1.00$. Between clusters, at
  least $4.0$. The apexes (C, F) sit slightly off-centre on purpose --- a perfectly symmetric
  triangle ties two neighbors at the same distance and breaks the $\sigma$ search on the next
  frames.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{$\rho_i$: nobody is left alone}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.52\textwidth}
    \[ \rho_i = \min_{j \in \mathcal{N}_i} d(x_i, x_j) \]
    the distance to your \textbf{nearest} neighbor.
    \vskip 5pt
    Every distance is then measured \emph{from $\rho_i$ outward}: your closest neighbor sits at
    shifted distance $0$, and so gets weight exactly $\mathbf{1}$.
    \vskip 5pt
    \textbf{Why do this at all?} It guarantees every point is connected to something with full
    strength --- even a point stranded in an empty region. Without it, isolated points would
    detach from the graph entirely and the layout would have nothing to hold them.
    \vskip 5pt
    \footnotesize This is the ``shrink the sparse regions'' half of the previous frame.
    \column{0.48\textwidth}
    \begin{center}\small
      \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.25}
      \begin{tabular}{c l c}
        \toprule
        \textbf{point} & \textbf{3 nearest} & $\bm{\rho_i}$ \\
        \midrule
        A & C, B, D & 0.918 \\
        B & C, A, D & 0.971 \\
        C & A, B, D & 0.918 \\
        D & F, E, B & 0.832 \\
        E & F, D, B & 0.890 \\
        F & D, E, B & 0.832 \\
        \bottomrule
      \end{tabular}
    \end{center}
    \vskip 6pt
    {\footnotesize Note every point's third neighbor is \emph{across} the gap --- with $k=3$ in a
    3-point cluster it has to be. Watch what the weights do to it.}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{$\sigma_i$: how fast the weight falls off}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.46\textwidth}
    \[ p_{j|i} = \exp\!\left(-\frac{\max(d(x_i,x_j)-\rho_i,\,0)}{\sigma_i}\right) \]
    $\sigma_i$ is the \textbf{local scale}: large $\sigma$ means the weight decays slowly (a wide
    neighborhood), small $\sigma$ means it drops off a cliff.
    \vskip 5pt
    It is not a hyperparameter. It is solved for, per point, by \textbf{binary search} on
    \[ \sum_{j \in \mathcal{N}_i} p_{j|i} = \log_2 k. \]
    \vskip 2pt
    \footnotesize Every point ends up with the same ``amount of neighborhood''
    ($\log_2 3 = 1.585$), whatever the local density --- the \textbf{stretch the dense regions}
    half.
    \column{0.54\textwidth}
    \begin{center}
      \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig/umap_rho_sigma.pdf}
    \end{center}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{By hand: point A's three weights}
  With $\rho_A = 0.9179$ and the search returning $\sigma_A = 0.1532$:
  \vskip 4pt
  \begin{center}\small
    \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.4}
    \begin{tabular}{c c c c l}
      \toprule
      \textbf{neighbor} & $d(A,j)$ & $d - \rho_A$ & $\bm{p_{j|A}}$ & \\
      \midrule
      C & 0.9179 & 0 & $e^{0} = \mathbf{1.000}$ & nearest, by construction \\
      B & 1.0000 & 0.0821 & $e^{-0.0821/0.1532} = \mathbf{0.585}$ & clearly a neighbor \\
      D & 5.0000 & 4.0821 & $e^{-4.0821/0.1532} = \mathbf{2.6\!\times\!10^{-12}}$ & a neighbor
        on paper only \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
  \end{center}
  \vskip 6pt
  Check the constraint: $1.000 + 0.585 + 0.000 = \mathbf{1.585} = \log_2 3$. \checkmark
  \vfill
  \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.97\linewidth}{\small
    This is how the two clusters separate \emph{without anyone telling UMAP there are two}. D is
    on A's neighbor list, because with $k=3$ it has to be. But the exponential gives it a weight
    of $10^{-12}$, so the edge exists on paper and carries nothing.}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Symmetrization: whose opinion counts?}
  $p_{j|i}$ is \textbf{directional}. A can think B is a close neighbor while B, sitting in a
  denser patch, does not return the compliment. UMAP resolves it with a \textbf{fuzzy union}
  (a probabilistic OR):
  \[ p_{ij} = p_{j|i} + p_{i|j} - p_{j|i}\,p_{i|j} \]
  \begin{center}
    \begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.72, every node/.style={font=\small}]
      \node[circle, draw, fill=armblue!20, minimum size=7mm] (i) at (0,0) {$i$};
      \node[circle, draw, fill=armblue!20, minimum size=7mm] (j) at (2.6,0) {$j$};
      \draw[->, very thick, armblue] (i) to[bend left=20]
        node[above, font=\scriptsize] {$p_{j\mid i}$} (j);
      \draw[->, very thick, armred] (j) to[bend left=20]
        node[below, font=\scriptsize] {$p_{i\mid j}$} (i);
      \node[font=\scriptsize, gray] at (1.3,-1.35) {two directed weights};
      \draw[->, thick, gray] (3.6,0) -- (5.0,0);
      \node[circle, draw, fill=armblue!20, minimum size=7mm] (i2) at (6.0,0) {$i$};
      \node[circle, draw, fill=armblue!20, minimum size=7mm] (j2) at (8.6,0) {$j$};
      \draw[very thick, violet1] (i2) -- node[above, font=\scriptsize] {$p_{ij}$} (j2);
      \node[font=\scriptsize, gray] at (7.3,-1.35) {one undirected weight};
    \end{tikzpicture}
  \end{center}
  \vskip 2pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.48\textwidth}
    \small \textbf{Read it as:} the edge is strong if \emph{either} endpoint considers the other
    a neighbor.
    \column{0.50\textwidth}
    \small On the toy $p_{B|A} = p_{A|B} = 0.585$, so
    \[ p_{AB} = 0.585 + 0.585 - 0.585^2 = \mathbf{0.828}, \]
    stronger than either direction alone. And $p_{AD} = \mathbf{0.0000}$.
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The finished graph}
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig/umap_toy_graph.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vskip -6pt
  \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.97\linewidth}{\small
    \textbf{This weighted graph is the entire high-dimensional input from here on.} UMAP never
    looks at the original coordinates again. Whatever the graph failed to capture is gone --- so
    if $k$ was badly chosen, no amount of optimizing can recover it.}}
\end{frame}

\section{Step 2: finding the layout}
\sectiontransition{Step 2 of 2}{Lay the graph out in 2-D, and let attraction and repulsion fight
to a draw.}

\begin{frame}{The low-dimensional kernel}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.48\textwidth}
    Put the points at positions $y_i \in \R^2$ and define the low-dimensional similarity
    \[ q_{ij} = \frac{1}{1 + a\,\|y_i - y_j\|^{2b}} \]
    \vskip 3pt
    $a$ and $b$ are \textbf{not} yours to set. UMAP fits them so the curve matches the shape you
    asked for with \texttt{min\_dist}:
    \vskip 3pt
    {\footnotesize
    \begin{tabular}{l c c}
      \toprule
      \texttt{min\_dist} & $a$ & $b$ \\
      \midrule
      0.0 & 1.933 & 0.791 \\
      0.8 & 0.232 & 1.681 \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}}
    \vskip 5pt
    \footnotesize A heavy tail, like t-SNE's Student-$t$: distant pairs are allowed to be
    genuinely distant without the loss exploding.
    \column{0.52\textwidth}
    \begin{center}
      \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig/umap_lowdim_kernel.pdf}
    \end{center}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The loss: cross-entropy, and its two halves}
  \[
    \mathcal{L} = -\sum_{i<j}\Big[\underbrace{p_{ij}\log q_{ij}}_{\text{attraction}}
    + \underbrace{(1-p_{ij})\log(1-q_{ij})}_{\text{repulsion}}\Big]
  \]
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.46\textwidth}
    \vskip -4pt
    \begin{center}
      \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig/umap_attract_repel.pdf}
    \end{center}
    \column{0.52\textwidth}
    \textbf{Attraction} bites when $p_{ij}$ is large and $q_{ij}$ small: true neighbors placed
    far apart. Pulls them together.
    \vskip 4pt
    \textbf{Repulsion} bites when $p_{ij}$ is small and $q_{ij}$ large: strangers placed on top
    of each other. Pushes them apart.
    \vskip 6pt
    \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\scriptsize
      \textbf{This is the payoff from frame 2.} t-SNE's KL has only the first term. Its second
      term is missing, so nothing in t-SNE's objective objects to two unrelated clusters landing
      next to each other. UMAP's does, and that is where the extra global structure comes from.}}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Intuition: springs and magnets}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.54\textwidth}
    \textbf{Springs.} Every strong edge is a spring pulling its two points together. Strength
    $\propto p_{ij}$.
    \vskip 4pt
    \textbf{Magnets.} Every pair repels, gently. Without this the whole layout collapses to a
    point, which would make the loss's first term perfectly happy.
    \vskip 6pt
    \textbf{Solved by SGD}, and here is the trick that makes it fast:
    \begin{itemize}\small
      \item sample \textbf{positive} edges in proportion to $p_{ij}$ --- so weak edges are
        mostly skipped;
      \item for repulsion, do not sum over all pairs. Sample a handful of \textbf{random}
        pairs and push those apart (\textbf{negative sampling}).
    \end{itemize}
    \vskip 4pt
    \footnotesize That is what buys UMAP its scaling. The exact loss is $O(n^2)$ in pairs;
    negative sampling estimates it in roughly $O(n)$ work per epoch.
    \column{0.44\textwidth}
    \begin{center}
      \begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.82]
        \node[circle, draw=armblue, fill=armblue!30, minimum size=8mm] (A) at (0,0) {A};
        \node[circle, draw=armblue, fill=armblue!30, minimum size=8mm] (B) at (1.7,0) {B};
        \node[circle, draw=armblue, fill=armblue!30, minimum size=8mm] (C) at (0.85,1.5) {C};
        \node[circle, draw=armred, fill=armred!30, minimum size=8mm] (D) at (4.3,0.6) {D};
        \draw[armblue, very thick, decorate,
              decoration={zigzag, segment length=3mm, amplitude=1mm}] (A) -- (B);
        \draw[armblue, very thick, decorate,
              decoration={zigzag, segment length=3mm, amplitude=1mm}] (A) -- (C);
        \draw[armblue, very thick, decorate,
              decoration={zigzag, segment length=3mm, amplitude=1mm}] (B) -- (C);
        \draw[armred, thick, dashed, <->] (C) -- (D)
          node[midway, above, font=\scriptsize] {repel};
        \node[below, armblue, font=\scriptsize] at (0.85,-0.6) {springs (attract)};
      \end{tikzpicture}
    \end{center}
    \vskip 8pt
    {\footnotesize Forces balance, and the layout stops moving. That equilibrium \emph{is} the
    embedding.}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Watching it settle}
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.97\textwidth]{fig/umap_epochs.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vskip -2pt
  \footnotesize Fashion-MNIST, same data and settings, stopped at increasing epoch counts. The

\end{frame}

\section{Using it, and reading it}
\sectiontransition{Using it}{Two knobs that matter, and a short list of what the picture is not
telling you.}

\begin{frame}{\texttt{n\_neighbors}: how far you look}
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.97\textwidth]{fig/umap_n_neighbors.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vskip -2pt
  \footnotesize It sets $k$ --- and therefore how much of the data each point's ruler is
  calibrated against. \textbf{Small} ($\sim$5): fine local detail, many small islands, and a
  real risk of fragmenting one true cluster into several. \textbf{Large} ($\sim$50): each point
  sees more context, so the layout preserves more of the global arrangement and smooths detail
  away. Default 15.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{\texttt{min\_dist}: how tightly points may pack}
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.97\textwidth]{fig/umap_min_dist.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vskip -2pt
  \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!12}{\parbox{0.97\linewidth}{\scriptsize
    \textbf{Watch out:} \texttt{min\_dist} is \emph{purely cosmetic in the topological sense}. It
    changes $a$ and $b$, so it changes how tightly a cluster is allowed to compress --- but it
    does not change the graph, so it does not change who is whose neighbor. Small values look
    more ``clustered'' without the data being any more clustered. Do not read the tightness of a
    blob as evidence.}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Predict-first: same data, same settings, new seed}
  Nothing changes except \texttt{random\_state}. How much of the picture survives?
  \pause
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.97\textwidth]{fig/umap_seeds.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vskip -2pt
  \footnotesize The \textbf{groups} survive. Their \textbf{positions, orientation and mirroring}
  do not. If you describe your result as ``cluster 3 sits between cluster 1 and cluster 5'', you
  have described this seed and nothing more.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{How to read a UMAP plot}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.49\textwidth}
    \textbf{You can trust:}
    \begin{itemize}\small
      \item \textcolor{paramgreen}{\textbf{Local neighborhoods}} --- points near each other in
        high-D usually stay near each other.
      \item \textcolor{paramgreen}{\textbf{Cluster membership}} --- which points group with which.
      \item \textcolor{paramgreen}{\textbf{Connected components}} --- genuinely separated groups
        stay separated.
    \end{itemize}
    \column{0.49\textwidth}
    \textbf{You cannot trust:}
    \begin{itemize}\small
      \item \textcolor{armred}{\textbf{The axes}} --- they mean nothing; rotation and reflection
        are arbitrary.
      \item \textcolor{armred}{\textbf{Cluster sizes}} --- visual area is not sample count, and
        \texttt{min\_dist} sets it anyway.
      \item \textcolor{armred}{\textbf{Between-cluster distances}} --- ``far apart'' is not ``very
        different''.
      \item \textcolor{armred}{\textbf{Small clusters at low \texttt{n\_neighbors}}} --- often
        fragments, not findings.
    \end{itemize}
  \end{columns}
  \vfill
  \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.97\linewidth}{\small
    \textbf{The test that costs you nothing:} rerun with a few seeds and a few
    \texttt{n\_neighbors}. If the pattern you are about to write down survives, it is real. If it
    does not, you were reading the optimizer.}}
\end{frame}

% [fragile] is mandatory: the lstlisting below contains "#" comments, and without it
% Beamer's frame scanning reads them as macro parameters ("Illegal parameter number
% in definition of \iterate").
\begin{frame}[fragile]{In practice}
\begin{lstlisting}
import umap

reducer = umap.UMAP(
    n_neighbors=15,      # local <-> global
    min_dist=0.1,        # packing only
    metric="cosine",     # cosine for embeddings
    random_state=509,    # fixes the seed (and disables parallelism)
)
Z = reducer.fit_transform(X)
\end{lstlisting}
  \vskip 2pt
  \begin{itemize}\small
    \item \textbf{Standardize first} if your features have different units --- UMAP inherits
      PCA's scale sensitivity through the distance metric.
    \item \textbf{Pick the metric deliberately.} \texttt{cosine} for text or CLIP embeddings,
      \texttt{euclidean} for physical measurements, \texttt{hamming} for binary. This matters more
      than either knob above.
    \item On wide data, \textbf{PCA to $\sim$50 first} --- faster, and it strips noise the graph
      would otherwise take seriously.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The four mistakes people actually make}
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Wrong metric} --- Euclidean on text embeddings. The graph is then built from
      distances that do not mean what you think, and everything downstream inherits it.
    \item \textbf{Unstandardized features} --- one large-range column dominates every distance,
      exactly as it hijacks PC1.
    \item \textbf{Trusting a single run} --- see the previous frames.
    \item \textbf{Feeding the 2-D embedding to a model.} This is the worst one. The embedding
      threw away almost everything to make a picture. Cluster or classify on the
      \emph{high-dimensional} data (or on PCA components); use UMAP for your eyes.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{frame}

\section*{Wrap-up}

\begin{frame}{Recap}
  \begin{itemize}\small
    \item \textbf{The assumption:} data is uniformly sampled from a manifold under a
      \emph{locally varying} metric. Everything else follows from it.
    \item \textbf{$\rho_i$} connects every point to its nearest neighbor at full strength;
      \textbf{$\sigma_i$} is solved per point so all neighborhoods carry the same fuzzy mass
      $\log_2 k$. Together they give each point its own ruler.
    \item \textbf{Fuzzy union} turns two directed opinions into one undirected edge. The result
      is a weighted graph, and from there UMAP never sees your data again.
    \item \textbf{Cross-entropy} has an attraction term \emph{and} a repulsion term. t-SNE's KL
      has only the first, which is why UMAP keeps more global structure.
    \item \textbf{Read it carefully:} neighborhoods and membership are real; axes, sizes and
      between-cluster distances are not.
  \end{itemize}
  \vfill
  \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.97\linewidth}{\small
    \textbf{That closes unsupervised learning} (clustering $+$ dimensionality reduction). Next
    chapter: a new family of models.}}
\end{frame}
