  \pause

  \vskip 8pt

\begin{frame}{How many hours of robot data?}
  \small
  A robot picks and places objects it has never seen, in a laboratory whose data the model
  was never trained on, with \textbf{no task-specific training and no reward function}.
  \vskip 8pt
  Guess the size of its robot training set.
  \vskip 6pt
  \pause
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.62\textwidth]{data_asymmetry.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vskip -4pt
  \footnotesize\centering
  Under \textbf{62 hours} of robot video - beside \textbf{a million hours} of ordinary
  internet video. Four orders of magnitude.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The claim}
  \small
  \begin{center}
    \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.88\linewidth}{\centering\small
      \textbf{Physics can be learned by watching.}\\
      Only the mapping from \emph{commands} to \emph{consequences} needs a robot.}}
  \end{center}
  \vskip 12pt
  If that is true, it dissolves chapter 15's central problem. There is no internet of robot
  data and there never will be - but there is an internet of \textbf{video}, and video is
  full of objects falling, rolling, colliding and being picked up.
  \vskip 10pt
  \footnotesize
  Chapter 15's answer to data scarcity was to pool across robots and squeeze more from
  every demonstration. This is a different answer: \textbf{stop needing the demonstrations
  for the physics}.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The map for today}
  \small
  A VLA is built on a vision-language model. A vision-language model is built on a vision
  encoder. \textbf{JEPA has a candidate at all three levels.}
  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{center}
    \begin{tabular}{@{}p{1.4cm}p{4.4cm}p{4.6cm}@{}}
      & \textbf{Mainstream} & \textbf{JEPA alternative} \\[3pt]
      \hline\rule{0pt}{2.8ex}
      Level 3 & VLA (ch15) & LeWorldModel, V-JEPA 2-AC \\[4pt]
      Level 2 & vision-language model (ch12) & VL-JEPA \\[4pt]
      Level 1 & CLIP-style vision encoder (ch12) & V-JEPA 2 \\
    \end{tabular}
  \end{center}
  \vskip 10pt
  We climb it from the bottom. \textbf{Watch the evidence as we go} - it does not stay the
  same strength at every level, and that turns out to be the most important thing in this
  lecture.
\end{frame}

\section{Level 1: the vision encoder}

\begin{frame}[plain]
  \vfill
  \begin{center}
    {\Large\color{popblue}\textbf{Level 1: the vision encoder}}\\[0.9em]
    \begin{minipage}{0.82\textwidth}\centering\small
      Start at the bottom. Replace CLIP.
    \end{minipage}
  \end{center}
  \vfill
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{V-JEPA: mask in space \emph{and} time}
  \small
  Last lecture's recipe, with a time axis added. Corrupt a video clip by removing
  \textbf{spatio-temporal} regions; predict the representations of what was removed.
  \vskip 10pt
  \begin{center}
    \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.88\linewidth}{\small\centering
      The bet: by learning to fill in the missing pieces of video, the model has to learn
      \textbf{how things move} - and by proxy, how the world works.}}
  \end{center}
  \vskip 10pt
  This is the first system in the chapter for which "predictive" means anything like
  "predicts the future".
\end{frame}

\wlslide{borrowed/welchlabs/vjepa2_masked_video.jpg}

\begin{frame}{Why Something-Something v2 is the honest benchmark}
  \small
  Most video benchmarks can be solved by \textbf{looking at one frame}. Recognise a
  swimming pool, guess "swimming".
  \vskip 8pt
  Something-Something v2 is built to defeat that. Its classes are defined by
  \textbf{motion}:
  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{center}
    \emph{"pushing something from left to right"} \quad vs \quad
    \emph{"pushing something from right to left"}
  \end{center}
  \vskip 6pt
  Identical objects, identical scene, identical single frame. Only the direction differs.
  \vskip 10pt
  \footnotesize
  A model that has only learned appearance scores at chance here. That is why it is the
  number worth quoting for a video model - and why V-JEPA 2's \textbf{77.3} is meaningful.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{V-JEPA 2, and the contrast that matters}
  \small
  \textbf{V-JEPA 2} (Meta, June 2025): \textbf{1.2B} parameters, over \textbf{one million
  hours} of internet video plus a million images.
  \vskip 10pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.47\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\small
      \textbf{CLIP} is trained so image embeddings match \textbf{caption} embeddings. Its
      representation is \textbf{pinned to language}: it learns the distinctions that people
      bothered to write down.}}
    \column{0.47\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\small
      \textbf{V-JEPA} never sees a word. It may represent "cat" however it likes, as long
      as that helps fill in missing video. \textbf{Unconstrained, and unaided.}}}
  \end{columns}
  \vskip 10pt
  \centering\small
  \textbf{AMI Labs} - the company LeCun leaves Meta to found, and which we come back to at
  the end of this lecture - puts the philosophy on its landing page:\\
  \emph{"Real intelligence does not start in language. It starts in the world."}
\end{frame}

\wlslide{borrowed/welchlabs/vjepa_vs_clip.jpg}

\begin{frame}{So does a language-free encoder even talk to a language model?}
  \small
  It is a real worry. If nothing ever forced the representation to line up with words,
  why would a language model be able to read it?
  \vskip 8pt
  \pause
  It works - and the authors flag it as \emph{"contrary to conventional wisdom"}:
  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{center}
    \begin{tabular}{@{}lr@{}}
      Something-Something v2 (motion) & \textbf{77.3} \\
      Epic-Kitchens-100 action anticipation & \textbf{39.7} recall@5 \\
      PerceptionTest (with an 8B language model) & \textbf{84.0} \\
      TempCompass (with an 8B language model) & \textbf{76.9} \\
    \end{tabular}
  \end{center}
  \vskip 8pt
  \footnotesize
  \color{armred}Note what the last two require: the encoder is \textbf{aligned to a large
  language model} to produce them. At level 1 the JEPA alternative wins by
  \textbf{joining} the mainstream stack, not by replacing it. Hold that thought.
\end{frame}

\section{Level 2: the vision-language model}

\begin{frame}[plain]
  \vfill
  \begin{center}
    {\Large\color{popblue}\textbf{Level 2: the vision-language model}}\\[0.9em]
    \begin{minipage}{0.82\textwidth}\centering\small
      Now replace the whole thing.
    \end{minipage}
  \end{center}
  \vfill
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The one-line change}
  \small
  A standard vision-language model emits \textbf{text, one token at a time}. Everything
  else about it - vision encoder in, prompt conditioning, a transformer in the middle - is
  already the shape of a JEPA.
  \vskip 10pt
  \begin{center}
    \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.88\linewidth}{\small\centering
      \textbf{VL-JEPA} keeps all of that and changes the target: instead of generating the
      answer, it \textbf{predicts the embedding of the answer}, in one shot.}}
  \end{center}
  \vskip 10pt
  The prompt becomes conditioning on the predictor. The answer text goes through its own
  encoder to produce the target. That is the entire modification.
\end{frame}

\wlslide{borrowed/welchlabs/vlm_to_jepa_mapping.jpg}

\begin{frame}{Why that should help: the mushroom}
  \small
  Show the model a mushroom and ask whether it is safe to eat. Suppose the training data
  says \emph{"do not eat this mushroom"}.
  \vskip 8pt
  Your model answers: \emph{"this mushroom is not safe to eat."}
  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.47\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\small
      \textbf{Generative VLM.} Wrong tokens in the wrong order. \textbf{Penalised} - for
      an answer that is completely correct.}}
    \column{0.47\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\small
      \textbf{VL-JEPA.} Both sentences encode to \textbf{nearly the same embedding}. The
      surface wording was never the target.}}
  \end{columns}
  \vskip 10pt
  \begin{center}
    \small\color{armred}\textbf{This is the leaves argument, moved from pixels to
    language.}
  \end{center}
  \footnotesize\centering
  Unpredictable surface detail - which leaf, which phrasing - is exactly what a learned
  target is free to discard.
\end{frame}

\wlslide{borrowed/welchlabs/mushroom_phrasing.jpg}

\begin{frame}{The controlled comparison}
  \small
  Same vision encoder. Same data. Same training configuration. Only the objective differs.
  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{center}
    \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.72\linewidth}{\centering\small
      After 5 million training examples, zero-shot video classification:\\[4pt]
      \textbf{VL-JEPA 35\%} \quad vs \quad \textbf{standard VLM 20\%}}}
  \end{center}
  \vskip 8pt
  And the efficiency turns into capability: roughly \textbf{1.6B} parameters outperforming
  \textbf{7B-13B} models on \textbf{GQA} (questions needing several reasoning steps over one
  image - \emph{"is there fruit to the left of the tray the cup is on?"}), and
  \textbf{65.7\%} on \textbf{WorldPrediction-WM} (pick what plausibly happens next) against
  GPT-4o's 53.3 and Gemini-2.0's 55.6.
  \vskip 8pt
  \footnotesize\color{gray}
  Sourcing, since it matters: the 35/20 comparison is reported in the Welch Labs
  documentary, not in a paper I have read. The GQA and WorldPrediction numbers come from
  press coverage of the VL-JEPA paper rather than the paper itself.
\end{frame}

\wlslide{borrowed/welchlabs/vljepa_learning_curve.jpg}

\begin{frame}{The wrinkle. Do not skip it.}
  \small
  VL-JEPA predicts an embedding. Embeddings are not sentences.
  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{center}
    \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!10}{\parbox{0.88\linewidth}{\small
      \textbf{It cannot answer a question by default.} To score those benchmarks, all
      candidate answers are encoded and the nearest one to the prediction is chosen -
      \textbf{multiple choice}. To make it speak at all, the team trained a separate
      \textbf{text decoder} to turn predictions back into words.}}
  \end{center}
  \vskip 10pt
  So the comparison to a 7B generative model is not quite like-for-like: one of them was
  asked to \emph{write} an answer, the other to \emph{pick} one.
  \vskip 8pt
  \footnotesize
  \color{armred}This is last lecture's closing point arriving as a bill. JEPA discards what
  a decoder would need - so the moment you want words back, you must bolt one on.
\end{frame}

\section{Level 3: the robot, and what a world model is}

\begin{frame}[plain]
  \vfill
  \begin{center}
    {\Large\color{popblue}\textbf{Level 3: the robot}}\\[0.9em]
    \begin{minipage}{0.82\textwidth}\centering\small
      Top of the stack. This is where the disagreement is real.
    \end{minipage}
  \end{center}
  \vfill
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{"World model" means two incompatible things}
  \small
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.47\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\small
      \textbf{A. Generative}\\[3pt]
      Given a state and an action, produce \textbf{the observation you would see}.\\[4pt]
      Sora, Genie, Cosmos.\\[4pt]
      \emph{You can watch its predictions.}}}
    \column{0.47\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\small
      \textbf{B. Latent predictive}\\[3pt]
      Given a state and an action, produce \textbf{the next state in its own
      representation}.\\[4pt]
      JEPA.\\[4pt]
      \emph{You cannot watch it. It is judged by whether you can plan with it.}}}
  \end{columns}
  \vskip 10pt
  \centering\small
  When someone says "world model", ask which one they mean. Half the disagreements in this
  area are people arguing past each other across this line.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The argument for B, and the honest counter}
  \small
  \textbf{For B:} rendering an imagined future commits you to detail you cannot know and
  do not need. Deciding whether to reach left or right does not require the texture of the
  tablecloth in the imagined future - and being wrong about it should not cost you.
  \vskip 10pt
  \begin{center}
    \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!10}{\parbox{0.88\linewidth}{\small
      \textbf{Against B:} a generative world model can be \textbf{inspected}. You can watch
      Sora's prediction and see the spoon pass through the bowl. A latent world model that
      has learned something wrong gives you \textbf{no such handle}, and debugging it is
      genuinely harder.}}
  \end{center}
  \vskip 8pt
  \footnotesize
  That is the price of last lecture's closing point, paid a second time: the thing that
  makes it efficient is the thing that makes it opaque.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{New material: learning the dynamics}
  \small
  Chapter 11 had \textbf{model-based} reinforcement learning - but there, the model was
  \textbf{handed to you}: you knew $P(s'|s,a)$ and $R$, and planning was computation.
  \vskip 8pt
  A line of work asks what happens when you must \textbf{learn} the dynamics instead, and
  learn them in a \textbf{latent space} rather than in pixels:
  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{itemize}\small
    \item \textbf{World Models} (Ha and Schmidhuber, 2018) - encode frames, learn a
      recurrent dynamics model, train the policy "in imagination".
    \item \textbf{PlaNet / Dreamer} - latent dynamics, still carrying a decoder and a
      reconstruction term.
    \item \textbf{TD-MPC} - latent dynamics with \textbf{no reconstruction at all}.
  \end{itemize}
  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{center}
    \small\color{armred}So "predict in latent space" is not new. LeCun says so himself.
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What is actually new}
  \small
  LeCun's own framing of the lineage, which is more honest than most:
  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{center}
    \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.9\linewidth}{\small
      Predicting the state at $t{+}1$ from the state at $t$ and an action, then optimising
      over action sequences to reach an outcome - \emph{"this is classical optimal control.
      This is going back to the late '50s in the Soviet Union, early '60s in the West.
      Very classical stuff.\\[3pt]
      What is not classical is you \textbf{learn} the model \dots\ what is even less
      classical is you learn a \textbf{representation} of the input that computes an
      abstract state, and you learn the model in that state. And that's JEPA."}}}
  \end{center}
  \vskip 10pt
  \footnotesize\centering
  Three claims, only the third of which is new. Worth noticing how rarely a field's
  champion says that out loud.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The difference that explains all of last lecture}
  \small
  In model-based reinforcement learning, the latent state \textbf{cannot collapse} - and
  nobody had to invent machinery to stop it.
  \vskip 8pt
  Why not? Because the latent is also required to predict \textbf{reward} and
  \textbf{value}. Those are external, grounded signals. A constant representation predicts
  reward terribly, so the objective rules it out for free.
  \vskip 10pt
  \begin{center}
    \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.88\linewidth}{\small\centering
      \textbf{JEPA has no reward.} It learns from passive video with nothing external
      anchoring the representation.\\[5pt]
      Every anti-collapse device in L39 - the EMA teacher, the stop-gradient, VICReg,
      SIGReg - is \textbf{the price of dropping reward.}}}
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Action conditioning}
  \small
  Two stages, and the asymmetry between them is the whole design:
  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{enumerate}\small
    \item \textbf{Stage 1, actionless.} Over a million hours of internet video. The model
      learns how the world moves, from watching. No actions, no labels, no rewards.
    \item \textbf{Stage 2, action-conditioned.} Freeze that, and learn
      $s_{t+1} = P(s_t, a_t)$ from under 62 hours of unlabelled robot video with its
      recorded actions.
  \end{enumerate}
  \vskip 10pt
  \begin{center}
    \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.88\linewidth}{\small\centering
      Essentially all the knowledge comes from passive observation. The robot data only
      has to teach it \textbf{what your commands do} - not what the world is.}}
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Two different systems. Do not confuse them.}
  \small
  The rest of this lecture draws on \textbf{two} systems that share one idea. They are
  wildly different in scale, and nearly everything that follows depends on which is which.
  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{center}
  \scriptsize
  \begin{tabular}{@{}p{2.4cm}p{4.6cm}p{5.2cm}@{}}
    & \textbf{V-JEPA 2-AC} (Meta) & \textbf{LeWorldModel} \\[2pt]
    \hline\rule{0pt}{2.6ex}
    Size & 1.2B & compact; \textbf{one GPU, a few hours} \\[4pt]
    Trained on & 1M h internet video + 62 h DROID & raw pixels and actions from one task \\[4pt]
    Runs on & real \textbf{Franka arms}, two labs & \textbf{PushT}, a 2-D pushing task \\[4pt]
    Shown here & the zero-shot result & the \textbf{inside} of the world model \\
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}
  \vskip 8pt
  \footnotesize
  We use the toy to \textbf{look inside} - because on PushT you can train a decoder and
  actually see what the model imagined. That is not possible at Meta's scale, and the
  pictures that follow are \textbf{not} what the Franka arm is running.
\end{frame}

\wlslide{borrowed/welchlabs/lewm_pusht_arch.jpg}

\begin{frame}{It learns the physics from pixels alone (LeWorldModel, PushT)}
  \small
  Train on nothing but sequences of \textbf{(image, action, next image)}. Then train a
  small decoder purely so \emph{we} can look at the predicted embeddings.
  \vskip 8pt
  Press \emph{up}, and the decoded prediction moves up. Left, left. Push into the T, and
  the T rotates about the contact point.
  \vskip 10pt
  \begin{center}
    \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.88\linewidth}{\small\centering
      Nobody told it the T is rigid, that it is movable, or how contact works. All of that
      is \textbf{inferred from watching pixels change under actions.}}}
  \end{center}
  \vskip 8pt
  \footnotesize\centering
  What you have is effectively \textbf{a learned video game}: a simulator you can press
  keys in, built from observation instead of written by a programmer.
\end{frame}

\wlslide{borrowed/welchlabs/pusht_decoded_actions.jpg}

\begin{frame}{And it drifts \small (toy system: LeWorldModel on PushT)}
  \small
  Run the model forward 18 steps and put it beside the real environment under the same
  actions.
  \vskip 8pt
  Early steps match closely. Then they separate - and once they separate they do not come
  back, because each prediction is fed its own previous output.
  \vskip 10pt
  \begin{center}
    \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!10}{\parbox{0.88\linewidth}{\small\centering
      In the authors' own words, the learned world model \emph{"goes off the rails
      sometimes"} - and that instability sets a hard limit on how far ahead you can plan.}}
  \end{center}
  \vskip 8pt
  \footnotesize
  \color{armred}Chapter 15's compounding error, third appearance: no teacher forcing at
  inference, so small errors feed themselves.
\end{frame}

\section{Planning as search in embedding space}

\begin{frame}[plain]
  \vfill
  \begin{center}
    {\Large\color{popblue}\textbf{Planning as search in embedding space}}\\[0.9em]
    \begin{minipage}{0.82\textwidth}\centering\small
      Now use it.
    \end{minipage}
  \end{center}
  \vfill
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The loop}
  \small
  You are given a \textbf{goal image}. Encode it once; call that $s_{\text{goal}}$. Then,
  at every step:
  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{enumerate}\small
    \item Sample many candidate action sequences.
    \item Roll each one forward \textbf{through the world model}, in latent space.
    \item Score each by $\|\,s_{\text{final}} - s_{\text{goal}}\,\|$.
    \item Keep the best few, resample around them, repeat. \hfill
      (\textbf{cross-entropy method}, \textbf{CEM})
    \item \textbf{Execute only the first action.} Then re-plan from what actually happened.
      \hfill (\textbf{model-predictive control}, \textbf{MPC})
  \end{enumerate}
  \vskip 8pt
  \footnotesize\color{gray}
  Both CEM and MPC are new here - chapter 11 planned with a \emph{given} model, and never
  needed either.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The cross-entropy method, in two pictures \small (toy system: PushT)}
  \small
  Start with 500 completely random trajectories. Roll them all out in the world model,
  colour each by its distance to the goal embedding, keep the best 30 as an
  \textbf{elite set}, and resample from that set's mean and variance. Repeat.
  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{center}
    \small\color{armred}No gradients. No trained planner. Just imagine, score, and narrow.
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\wlslide{borrowed/welchlabs/cem_random_trajectories.jpg}
\wlslide{borrowed/welchlabs/cem_elite_set.jpg}

\begin{frame}{What the score actually is}
  \small
  \begin{center}
    \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.84\linewidth}{\centering\small
      Euclidean distance between the \textbf{predicted final embedding} and the
      \textbf{goal embedding}.}}
  \end{center}
  \vskip 12pt
  Notice what is \emph{not} in that sentence:
  \begin{itemize}\small
    \item No reward function was specified.
    \item No reward model was trained.
    \item Nothing was ever rendered - the entire search happens in the representation,
      which is what makes it fast enough to run at all.
  \end{itemize}
  \vskip 10pt
  \begin{center}
    \small\color{armred}\textbf{You say what you want by showing it a picture.}
  \end{center}
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{Execute one step, then re-plan}
  \small
  The rollout drifts. So do not trust it for long.
  \vskip 8pt
  Model-predictive control plans a whole sequence, executes \textbf{one action}, throws the
  rest away, and re-plans from the state the world is actually in.
  \vskip 10pt
  \begin{center}
    \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.88\linewidth}{\small\centering
      Chapter 15 met the same disease and chose a different cure.\\[5pt]
      \textbf{Action chunking:} commit to a burst of actions, so there are fewer decisions
      to compound.\\[3pt]
      \textbf{MPC:} re-plan constantly, so errors never get a long horizon to compound
      over.}}
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The result, and the honest numbers \small (real arms: V-JEPA 2-AC)}
  \small
  \textbf{V-JEPA 2-AC} was deployed \textbf{zero-shot} on Franka arms in two laboratories
  Meta had collected no data from - no task-specific training, no reward - reaching
  \textbf{65-80\%} on pick-and-place with novel objects.
  \vskip 10pt
  \begin{center}
    \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!10}{\parbox{0.88\linewidth}{\small
      Now the other side of the ledger:
      \begin{itemize}\small
        \item Planning takes \textbf{on the order of a minute per task} - orders of
          magnitude too slow for a real controller.
        \item \textbf{LeWorldModel} reliably plans about \textbf{five steps} ahead on
          PushT before the rollout stops being trustworthy.
      \end{itemize}}}
  \end{center}
  \vskip 8pt
  \footnotesize
  This is not a robot controller. It is a demonstration that the \textbf{representation}
  contains enough physics to plan in.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The limitation hiding inside "show it a picture"}
  \small
  Goal-image planning is elegant. It is also the method's boundary.
  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{center}
    \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.88\linewidth}{\small\centering
      This works when \textbf{the goal is known and the path is unknown.}}}
  \end{center}
  \vskip 10pt
  \emph{"Put the cup on the shelf"} - fine, photograph the outcome.
  \vskip 6pt
  \emph{"Tidy the kitchen."} \emph{"Make this safe."} \emph{"Explore."} \textbf{There is no
  image to aim at.}
  \vskip 10pt
  Open-ended tasks need exactly what this approach was advertised as avoiding: reward
  design, goal proposal, or something that invents intermediate subgoals.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The proposed fix: hierarchy}
  \small
  Two levels of world model. The \textbf{high-level} predictor proposes a
  \textbf{subgoal}; the \textbf{low-level} predictor plans the actions to reach it.
  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{center}
    \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.86\linewidth}{\small\centering
      On PushT, two levels extend the reliable planning horizon from about
      \textbf{5 steps to 15}.}}
  \end{center}
  \vskip 10pt
  It also answers the previous slide: a hierarchy that \emph{generates} subgoals is a
  mechanism for turning \emph{"tidy the kitchen"} into a sequence of things you \emph{can}
  aim a goal-conditioned planner at.
  \vskip 8pt
  \footnotesize\color{gray}
  Zhang et al., \emph{Hierarchical Planning with Latent World Models}, 2026.
\end{frame}

\wlslide{borrowed/welchlabs/hierarchical_planning.jpg}

\begin{frame}{Why hierarchy is the right shape}
  \small
  LeCun's illustration, and it is a good one. Sitting in his office at NYU, wanting to be
  in Paris tomorrow:
  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{center}
    \emph{go to the airport} $\rightarrow$ \emph{catch a plane} $\rightarrow$
    \emph{be in Paris}
  \end{center}
  \vskip 8pt
  He \textbf{cannot} plan that trip as a sequence of millisecond muscle commands. Not
  because it would be slow, but because \textbf{he does not have the information} - he does
  not know yet where he will be standing when he hails the taxi.
  \vskip 10pt
  \begin{center}
    \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!10}{\parbox{0.88\linewidth}{\small\centering
      Detail buys accuracy at short range and \textbf{destroys} it at long range. So
      predict in detail over short horizons, and abstractly over long ones.}}
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\wlslide{borrowed/welchlabs/nyu_to_paris.jpg}

\begin{frame}{The open part}
  \small
  The hope is that the hierarchy is \textbf{learned rather than designed} - that if you
  train low levels to make short, detailed predictions and high levels to make long,
  abstract ones, a useful hierarchy of representations simply emerges, the way feature
  hierarchies emerged in convolutional networks.
  \vskip 10pt
  Two honest caveats:
  \begin{itemize}\small
    \item LeCun concedes it requires \textbf{semi-expert trajectories} - you cannot learn
      high-level structure from random flailing.
    \item Hierarchical JEPA is still largely a \textbf{proposal}. The systems that ship
      are single-level.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\section{The honest scoreboard, and the bet}

\begin{frame}[plain]
  \vfill
  \begin{center}
    {\Large\color{popblue}\textbf{The honest scoreboard}}\\[0.9em]
    \begin{minipage}{0.82\textwidth}\centering\small
      Meta shipped a benchmark that its own flagship model fails.
    \end{minipage}
  \end{center}
  \vfill
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Three benchmarks, released alongside V-JEPA 2}
  \small
  \begin{itemize}\small
    \item \textbf{IntPhys 2} - is this scene \textbf{physically possible}? Objects passing
      through each other, vanishing, floating.
    \item \textbf{MVPBench} - video-language understanding on \textbf{minimally different
      pairs}.
    \item \textbf{CausalVQA} - counterfactuals, anticipation, planning. \emph{What
      \textbf{could} happen}, not what happened.
  \end{itemize}
  \vskip 10pt
  MVPBench's construction is worth stealing. Take pairs of videos that differ as little as
  possible but have \textbf{different correct answers}. A model that relies on a shortcut -
  the caption prior, a single frame - now scores \textbf{at chance by construction}.
  \vskip 6pt
  \footnotesize\color{gray}That trick generalises far beyond video. If you build a
  benchmark, build it so shortcuts score zero.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The result}
  \small
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.70\textwidth]{physics_gap.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vskip -4pt
  \footnotesize
  A model that is \textbf{state of the art at anticipating human actions} cannot reliably
  tell you whether a scene is \textbf{physically possible}. Whatever V-JEPA 2 learned from
  a million hours of video, it is \textbf{not} the intuitive physics the world-model thesis
  promises.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{A second admission}
  \small
  V-JEPA 2 shipped in June 2025. In March 2026 its own successor described its
  representations as \textbf{"noisy"}, with \textbf{"fragmented local spatial structure"}:
  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{center}
    \begin{tabular}{@{}lrr@{}}
      & \textbf{V-JEPA 2} & \textbf{V-JEPA 2.1} \\ \hline\rule{0pt}{2.6ex}
      NYUv2 depth, RMSE (lower better) & 0.642 & \textbf{0.350} \\
      ADE20K segmentation, mIoU & 24.4 & \textbf{47.8} \\
      Something-Something v2 & 77.3 & 76.9 \\
    \end{tabular}
  \end{center}
  \vskip 8pt
  For nine months, the flagship world model was strong globally and close to
  \textbf{uninformative about \emph{where} things are} - in a system whose proposed use is
  robot manipulation.
  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{center}
    \small\color{armred}Note the last row: motion got slightly \emph{worse} while
    segmentation doubled. \textbf{"The representation is good" is not one number.}
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The critique of VLA, prong 1: cloning does not scale}
  \small
  Chapter 15's models are trained largely by \textbf{behavioural cloning} - a human
  teleoperates the robot, and the model learns to imitate.
  \vskip 8pt
  LeCun's objection: you cannot collect demonstrations for every variation of every task,
  and outside the demonstrations these systems are, in his words, \emph{"completely
  helpless \dots\ brittle"}.
  \vskip 10pt
  \begin{center}
    \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.88\linewidth}{\small\centering
      A world model needs no demonstration of the task at all. It needs to know
      \textbf{what happens if}, and then it can search for a solution nobody showed it.}}
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\wlslide{borrowed/welchlabs/teleoperation_rig.jpg}

\begin{frame}{Prong 2: no explicit planning}
  \small
  A VLA is trained and deployed \textbf{end to end}. Camera images and joint positions in,
  next joint positions out, repeat.
  \vskip 8pt
  It plainly does something plan-like - chapter 15's robots pass a key between grippers to
  orient it correctly. But that reasoning is \textbf{implicit and invisible}.
  \vskip 10pt
  \begin{center}
    \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.88\linewidth}{\small\centering
      \emph{"If you really want to build reliable agentic systems, they absolutely have to
      be able to predict the consequences of their actions \dots\ the inference process now
      becomes a \textbf{search}, as opposed to just an autoregressive prediction."}}}
  \end{center}
  \vskip 6pt
  \footnotesize\centering
  A search you can inspect, bound, and attach safety constraints to.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The counter-evidence, kept deliberately}
  \small
  The critique is strong. It is also not the whole picture, and leaving it unanswered
  would be unfair to chapter 15.
  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{itemize}\small
    \item \textbf{RT-2} moved an object to a picture of Taylor Swift. Nothing in its
      demonstration data involved Taylor Swift - it connected a concept from
      \textbf{web pretraining} to actions learned separately.
    \item Later systems perform tasks absent from their demonstrations: microwaves, paper
      towel rolls, air fryers.
  \end{itemize}
  \vskip 10pt
  \begin{center}
    \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!10}{\parbox{0.88\linewidth}{\small\centering
      \textbf{Generalisation is a sliding scale, not a yes/no.} These tasks were not in the
      demonstrations; \emph{similar} ones were. The open question is how far that stretches
      - not whether it exists.}}
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\wlslide{borrowed/welchlabs/rt2_generalisation.jpg}

\begin{frame}{The higher you climb, the thinner the evidence}
  \small
  Look back at the stack we climbed, and at what was actually behind each rung.
  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{center}
  \scriptsize
  \begin{tabular}{@{}p{1.2cm}p{5.0cm}p{6.2cm}@{}}
    & \textbf{The JEPA alternative} & \textbf{What the evidence actually is} \\[2pt]
    \hline\rule{0pt}{2.6ex}
    Level 1 & V-JEPA 2, 1.2B, shipped &
      Real benchmark wins - but the headline numbers come from \textbf{aligning it to an
      8B language model} \\[5pt]
    Level 2 & VL-JEPA, 1.6B, research &
      Beats larger models - on a \textbf{multiple-choice protocol}, with a text decoder
      bolted on to speak \\[5pt]
    Level 3 & LeWorldModel / V-JEPA 2-AC &
      A \textbf{2-D pushing toy} planning \textbf{5 steps}; a real arm at 65-80\%, about a
      minute per task \\
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}
  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{center}
    \small\color{armred}The idea gets more ambitious as you climb. \textbf{The evidence
    gets thinner.}
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Nobody actually has to choose}
  \small
  The framing all lecture has been \emph{replace}. That is the argument as its champion
  states it. It is not what the evidence shows.
  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{itemize}\small
    \item V-JEPA 2's best numbers come from \textbf{pairing it with a language model}.
    \item VL-JEPA ships a \textbf{text decoder} so it can answer at all.
    \item 2026 systems couple a chapter-15 VLA to a \textbf{latent world model} and use
      both.
  \end{itemize}
  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{center}
    \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.88\linewidth}{\small\centering
      Even the documentary this lecture borrows from concludes that the JEPA approach is
      \emph{"not incompatible with the current mainstream language-driven approach"} - and
      that on agentic and robotics problems it remains \textbf{"quite limited"}.}}
  \end{center}
  \vskip 6pt
  \footnotesize\centering
  The interesting question is not \emph{which wins}. It is \emph{which layer does each
  idea belong in}.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Score the thesis}
  \small
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}p{7.4cm}p{4.6cm}@{}}
    \textbf{Claim} & \textbf{Status, August 2026} \\[2pt]
    \hline\rule{0pt}{2.8ex}
    Predicting in latent space gives good, efficient encoders &
      \color{paramgreen}\textbf{Supported} \\[5pt]
    You can plan with a video world model &
      \color{paramgreen}\textbf{Supported}, \color{black}slowly, small scale \\[5pt]
    Watching video yields \textbf{intuitive physics} &
      \color{armred}\textbf{Not supported} \color{black}(IntPhys 2) \\[5pt]
    It replaces next-token prediction for language &
      \color{armred}\textbf{Not demonstrated} \\
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}
  \vskip 10pt
  \footnotesize\centering
  Two green, two red. Anyone who tells you this is settled - in \textbf{either} direction -
  is selling something.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The bet}
  \small
  LeCun left Meta at the end of 2025 after twelve years as its Chief AI Scientist, and
  co-founded \textbf{AMI Labs}: \textbf{\$1.03B} raised in March 2026 at a \$3.5B
  pre-money valuation, headquartered in Paris. \textbf{No revenue plans.}
  \vskip 8pt
  What he says they will actually build is more specific than the coverage suggests -
  \textbf{not robots}:
  \vskip 4pt
  \begin{center}
    \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.88\linewidth}{\small\centering
      Complex systems you \textbf{cannot reduce to equations}: a jet engine, a chemical
      plant, a power plant, a patient's blood sugar.\\[4pt]
      \emph{"If you can write down the equations, just write down the equations."}}}
  \end{center}
  \vskip 8pt
  \footnotesize
  That is a sharper thesis than "beat the LLMs": world models are for systems where
  \textbf{nobody has a simulator} and data is the only route to one. Meanwhile Meta shipped
  V-JEPA 2.1 without him.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Recap}
  \small
  \begin{itemize}\small
    \item Every layer of the mainstream stack has a \textbf{JEPA counterpart} - and the
      evidence for each gets thinner as you climb.
    \item A \textbf{world model} means two different things. JEPA's kind predicts
      \textbf{state}, not pixels: cheap and unwatchable.
    \item Planning is \textbf{search in embedding space} against a goal \emph{image}. No
      reward is ever specified - and that is both the elegance and the boundary.
    \item Model-based RL avoided collapse for free because it had \textbf{reward}. JEPA
      pays for dropping it.
    \item The honest state: \textbf{good encoders, real but slow planning, no intuitive
      physics yet.}
  \end{itemize}
  \vskip 4pt
  \begin{center}
    \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.88\linewidth}{\small\centering
      \textbf{Anyone who leaves this room certain either way was taught badly.}\\
      \small The measurements are in the lecture. The bet is still running.}}
  \end{center}
\end{frame}
