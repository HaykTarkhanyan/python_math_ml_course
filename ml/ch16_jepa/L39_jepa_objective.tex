\begin{frame}{Same model. Same masking. One thing changed.}
  \small
  Take one architecture, one masking scheme, one dataset. Train it twice. The only
  difference: what it is asked to predict.
  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Run A} predicts the \textbf{pixels} of the hidden region.
    \item \textbf{Run B} predicts a \textbf{representation} of the hidden region.
  \end{itemize}
  \vskip 8pt
  Run A is given \textbf{60\% more training} than run B.
  \vskip 10pt
  \pause
  \begin{center}
    \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.8\linewidth}{\centering
      Linear evaluation on 1\% of ImageNet:\\[3pt]
      \textbf{pixels 40.7} \quad vs \quad \textbf{representations 66.9}\\[3pt]
      \small A gap of \textbf{26.2 points}, with the losing run getting more compute.}}
  \end{center}
  \vskip 4pt
  \footnotesize\centering (Assran et al., 2023, Table 7.)
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The question this chapter asks}
  \large
  \begin{center}
    \vskip 10pt
    Fifteen chapters have quietly been answering one question\\ without ever asking it
    out loud:
    \vskip 16pt
    {\color{popblue}\textbf{What should a model be asked to predict?}}
    \vskip 16pt
    \normalsize
    Not \emph{which architecture}. Not \emph{which optimiser}. The \textbf{objective}.
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Four answers you have already seen}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{@{}p{3.6cm}p{4.2cm}p{4.6cm}@{}}

\section{Three ways to build a self-supervised task}

\begin{frame}[plain]
  \vfill
  \begin{center}
    {\Large\color{popblue}\textbf{Three ways to build a self-supervised task}}\\[0.9em]
    \begin{minipage}{0.82\textwidth}\centering\small
      You have already seen two of them.
    \end{minipage}
  \end{center}
  \vfill
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The joint-embedding architecture, via CLIP}
  \small
  Chapter 12, renamed. Two encoders, one shared space, agreement as the objective.
  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Take two views of the same thing: two crops, an image and its caption, two
      signatures.
    \item Encode both. Train so matching pairs land close together.
  \end{itemize}
  \vskip 8pt
  The idea is older than CLIP by three decades. LeCun and colleagues built it at Bell Labs
  in the early 1990s to detect \textbf{fraudulent signatures}: pass a reference and a
  candidate through two copies of the same network, and compare the vectors that come out.
  They called them \textbf{Siamese networks}.
  \vskip 8pt
  \footnotesize\color{gray}Nothing is generated. The network never draws a signature.
\end{frame}

\wlslide{borrowed/welchlabs/siamese_signatures.jpg}

\begin{frame}{What a joint-embedding architecture cannot do}
  \small
  It answers \emph{"do these two things match?"} and nothing else.
  \vskip 10pt
  \begin{center}
    \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.86\linewidth}{\small
      Give it the left half of a photograph and the right half. It can score them as
      compatible. It \textbf{cannot tell you what the right half looks like} - not in
      pixels, not in features, not at all. There is no mechanism in the architecture for
      expressing \emph{how} $y$ relates to $x$.}}
  \end{center}
  \vskip 10pt
  For a world model that is fatal. \emph{"Is this a plausible next second of video?"} is
  useful. \emph{"What is the next second of video?"} is what an agent actually needs.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The generative architecture}
  \small
  Hide part of the input. Reconstruct it. Score the reconstruction against the truth.
  \vskip 6pt
  This is the shape of the denoising autoencoder (ch8), of masked autoencoders, and -
  with the axis relabelled from space to time - of next-token prediction.
  \vskip 10pt
  It fixes the joint-embedding problem exactly: it says \emph{what} $y$ is. And it cannot
  collapse, because the target is fixed by the data and the model does not get a vote.
  \vskip 10pt
  \pause
  \begin{center}
    \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!10}{\parbox{0.86\linewidth}{\small
      So why is this not the end of the lecture? Because for video and images it produces
      \textbf{blur}, and the reason it produces blur is not a bug to be engineered away.}}
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\wlslide{borrowed/welchlabs/ball_video_frames.jpg}

\begin{frame}{Why: you cannot enumerate the futures}
  \small
  A language model has a \textbf{finite vocabulary}. GPT-2 has exactly
  \textbf{50{,}257} possible next tokens, one output per token, and it can raise or lower
  each independently.
  \vskip 8pt
  Video has no such luxury. Each subpixel of a full-HD frame takes one of 256 values, and
  there are $1920 \times 1080 \times 3$ of them:
  \vskip 4pt
  \begin{center}
    $256^{\,1920 \times 1080 \times 3} \;\approx\; 10^{15{,}000{,}000}$ possible next frames
  \end{center}
  \vskip 4pt
  against roughly $10^{80}$ atoms in the observable universe.
  \vskip 8pt
  \footnotesize
  There is no output layer for that. So the network must emit \textbf{pixel values
  directly} - and now uncertainty has nowhere to go.
\end{frame}

\wlslide{borrowed/welchlabs/counting_argument.jpg}

\begin{frame}{So it predicts the average, and the average is a blur}
  \small
  A ball rolls toward the edge of a table. In the training data it sometimes bounces left,
  sometimes right.
  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.47\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\small
      \textbf{Language model.} Separate outputs for \emph{"left"} and \emph{"right"}.
      It raises \textbf{both}. The distribution is the answer.}}
    \column{0.47\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\small
      \textbf{Video model.} One frame out. Squared error is minimised by the
      \textbf{conditional mean} - so it draws the ball in \textbf{both places, faintly}.}}
  \end{columns}
  \vskip 10pt
  \begin{center}
    \small\color{armred}\textbf{Third time this course has met this argument.}
  \end{center}
  \footnotesize
  Chapter 15: averaging two valid robot trajectories gives a third, invalid one. Chapter
  10: diffusion exists partly to avoid committing to a mean. Same disease every time -
  \textbf{a single-valued prediction of a multi-valued thing}.
\end{frame}

\wlslide{borrowed/welchlabs/bouncing_ball_average.jpg}

\begin{frame}{And most of those pixels never mattered}
  \small
  Blur is the visible symptom. The deeper cost is \textbf{where the capacity goes}.
  \vskip 10pt
  \begin{center}
    \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!10}{\parbox{0.9\linewidth}{\small
      A model trained to predict a dashcam feed spends most of its resources predicting
      \textbf{the motion of the leaves} on the trees beside the road. Those leaves are
      essentially unpredictable - and they are a very large number of pixels.}}
  \end{center}
  \vskip 10pt
  The loss does not know the difference between \emph{"a car is pulling out"} and
  \emph{"that leaf moved 3 pixels left"}. It charges the same price for both, and there
  are far more leaves than cars.
  \vskip 6pt
  \footnotesize\color{gray}This example is LeCun's. On the next slide, the right-hand panel
  is the \textbf{optical flow} of that road scene - colour shows which direction each pixel
  is moving. Almost all the motion in the frame is foliage.
\end{frame}

\wlslide{borrowed/welchlabs/dashcam_leaves.jpg}

\begin{frame}{JEPA: predict the representation, not the thing}
  \small
  Encode \emph{both} sides. Then predict one embedding from the other, through a
  \textbf{predictor} network.
  \vskip 4pt
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.93\textwidth]{three_architectures.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vskip -2pt
  \footnotesize
  The latent $z$ absorbs what is genuinely unpredictable - which way the ball bounced.
  Two things are new: the loss lives in \textbf{representation space}, and the target is
  produced by a network that is \textbf{itself being trained}.
\end{frame}

\wlslide{borrowed/welchlabs/jepa_core_arch.jpg}

\begin{frame}{The sentence to remember}
  \vfill
  \begin{center}
    \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.86\linewidth}{\centering\large
      \textbf{JEPA gives the model permission to\\ throw information away.}}}
    \vskip 14pt
    \normalsize
    If the target encoder may discard the leaves, the loss stops charging for them.
  \end{center}
  \vfill
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Careful: none of that was I-JEPA}
  \small
  Every example so far - the bouncing ball, the dashcam - has been \textbf{video}. That
  was to show why \emph{predicting pixels} is hopeless in general.
  \vskip 10pt
  \begin{center}
    \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.88\linewidth}{\small
      The first working JEPA, \textbf{I-JEPA}, has \textbf{no time axis at all}. It takes
      \textbf{one still photograph}, hides some regions, and predicts their
      representations from the rest of the same image. Nothing is a "next frame".}}
  \end{center}
  \vskip 10pt
  \textbf{Predictive} here means \emph{predicts one part from another part}, not
  \emph{predicts the future}. Time arrives in L40, and only then does any of this become
  a world model.
\end{frame}

\section{The catch: collapse}

\begin{frame}[plain]
  \vfill
  \begin{center}
    {\Large\color{popblue}\textbf{The catch: collapse}}\\[0.9em]
    \begin{minipage}{0.82\textwidth}\centering\small
      And the rest of the design exists to stop it throwing \emph{everything} away.
    \end{minipage}
  \end{center}
  \vfill
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Write down the degenerate solution}
  \small
  Let the target encoder map \textbf{every} input to the same constant vector $c$. Let the
  predictor ignore its input and output $c$.
  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{center}
    \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.72\linewidth}{\centering
      $\|\,\text{Pred}(s_x) - s_y\,\| \;=\; \|c - c\| \;=\; \mathbf{0}$}}
  \end{center}
  \vskip 8pt
  The loss is \textbf{exactly zero} - the best value it can possibly take. The
  representation is worthless: every image, every caption, every video maps to the same
  point.
  \vskip 8pt
  This is \textbf{representation collapse}, and nothing in the objective forbids it.
  \vskip 4pt
  \footnotesize\color{gray}The generative architecture cannot do this, because it is
  scored against pixels it does not control. That safety is exactly what we gave up.
\end{frame}

\wlslide{borrowed/welchlabs/representation_collapse.jpg}

\begin{frame}{This is not mode collapse}
  \small
  Same word, different failure. Chapter 8b used it for something else.
  \vskip 10pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.47\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!10}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\small
      \textbf{Mode collapse} (ch8b, GANs)\\
      A \emph{generator} covers one mode of the data. It produces real-looking output,
      but always the same kind.}}
    \column{0.47\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\small
      \textbf{Representation collapse} (here)\\
      An \emph{encoder} maps every input to one point. It produces nothing at all, and
      the loss is delighted.}}
  \end{columns}
  \vskip 12pt
  \centering\small One is a generator being lazy about \emph{variety}.\\
  The other is an encoder being lazy about \emph{information}.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The loss will not tell you it broke}
  \small
  A falling loss curve is the thing you normally trust. Here it proves nothing, for
  \textbf{three} separate reasons.
  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{enumerate}\small
    \item \textbf{Zero is reachable by cheating.} The degenerate solution attains the
      global minimum. A low loss is consistent with a useless model.
    \item \textbf{The target is moving.} The loss measures agreement with the target
      encoder, which is itself being updated. It is not comparable across runs, or even
      across time within one run.
    \item \textbf{Shrinking beats learning.} The loss is a distance between embeddings, so
      \textbf{scaling the target encoder's outputs toward zero reduces it} without
      changing anything the representation encodes.
  \end{enumerate}
  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{center}
    \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.88\linewidth}{\small\centering
      You cannot early-stop on this loss. Model selection needs a \textbf{downstream
      probe} - which is expensive, so far fewer configurations get tried than you are
      used to.}}
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The energy picture}
  \small
  Define an energy $F(x,y)$ that is low when $x$ and $y$ are compatible. Training pushes
  it down on observed pairs.
  \vskip 2pt
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.86\textwidth]{energy_landscape.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vskip -2pt
  \footnotesize
  The failure mode is \textbf{not} low energy on the data - that is the goal. It is low
  energy \textbf{everywhere}: a flat landscape that distinguishes nothing. So the real
  objective is always \emph{low on the data, and kept high off it}.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{One loss, three choices}
  \small
  Every JEPA objective in the literature has the same two-term shape:
  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{center}
    \fcolorbox{popblue}{popblue!7}{\parbox{0.88\linewidth}{\centering\vskip 3pt
      $\mathcal{L} \;=\;
      \underbrace{\mathbb{E}\big[\, d\big(\text{Pred}(s_x, z),\, s_y\big) \big]}
                 _{\text{latent predictive invariance}}
      \;+\;
      \lambda \cdot \underbrace{R(s_1, \ldots, s_B)}_{\text{anti-collapse}}$
      \vskip 3pt}}
  \end{center}
  \vskip 6pt
  \footnotesize
  Reading it: $s_x, s_y$ are the two embeddings; $d$ is a distance; $z$ is the optional
  latent that absorbs what is genuinely unpredictable (which way the ball bounced);
  $s_1, \ldots, s_B$ are the embeddings of a \textbf{batch} of $B$ examples; and $\lambda$
  is a single weight trading the two terms off.
  \vskip 6pt
  \small
  Everyone agrees on the first term. \textbf{The families differ only in what $R$ is} -
  and in one case, in whether it is a term in the loss at all rather than a property of
  the architecture.
  \vskip 4pt
  \footnotesize\color{gray}The energy $F(x,y)$ from the previous slide is the same
  quantity as that first term, viewed one pair at a time.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The three families}
  \scriptsize
  \begin{tabular}{@{}p{3.0cm}p{4.3cm}p{2.6cm}p{3.1cm}@{}}
    \textbf{Family} & \textbf{What $R$ is} & \textbf{Examples} & \textbf{Cost} \\[2pt]
    \hline\rule{0pt}{2.6ex}
    \textbf{Contrastive} &
      not a regulariser - a second data term: pull matching pairs together, and explicitly
      push \emph{non}-matching pairs apart &
      \textbf{CLIP} (ch12) &
      needs many negative pairs; scales badly with dimension \\[8pt]
    \textbf{Teacher--student} &
      \emph{absent from the loss.} Collapse is blocked \textbf{structurally}, by making the
      two branches asymmetric &
      \textbf{I-JEPA}, DINO &
      works, poorly understood; silent failure mode \\[8pt]
    \textbf{Moment-matching} &
      an explicit penalty on batch statistics: keep every dimension's variance up,
      decorrelate the dimensions &
      \textbf{Barlow Twins} &
      one more term and weight to tune \\
  \end{tabular}
  \vskip 8pt
  \normalsize
  I-JEPA is in the middle row - worth dwelling on, because it is the row with no theory
  behind it.
  \vskip 4pt
  \footnotesize\color{gray}
  One representative each; BYOL, SimCLR, MoCo, VICReg and SIGReg belong to the same three
  rows and are not separate ideas.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The teacher that resists being dragged}
  \small
  I-JEPA's answer is an \textbf{exponential moving average} (\textbf{EMA}) teacher. The
  target encoder is never touched by gradient descent. It is a slow copy of the context
  encoder:
  \vskip 4pt
  \begin{center}
    $\theta_{\text{target}} \;\leftarrow\; m \cdot \theta_{\text{target}} \;+\;
     (1-m)\cdot \theta_{\text{context}}$
    \qquad $m: 0.996 \rightarrow 1.0$
  \end{center}
  \vskip 8pt
  Two supporting details, both load-bearing:
  \begin{itemize}\small
    \item \textbf{Stop-gradient.} No gradient flows into the target branch. The student
      chases the teacher; the teacher never chases back.
    \item By the end of pretraining $m \rightarrow 1$, so the teacher \textbf{freezes}
      entirely.
  \end{itemize}
  \vskip 6pt
  \textbf{Why this blocks collapse:} to reach the constant solution the student would have
  to \emph{drag the teacher there too} - but the teacher only moves at rate $1-m \approx
  0.004$ per step, and it is the thing defining the target. The constant is no longer a
  fixed point you can reach in one step.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Say it plainly: a heuristic, not a theorem}
  \small
  Keep the intuition from the last slide - it is genuinely why this works in practice. But
  be precise about what it does \emph{not} establish.
  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{center}
    \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!10}{\parbox{0.88\linewidth}{\small\centering
      It argues the constant solution is \textbf{hard to reach quickly}. It does
      \textbf{not} prove the constant solution is unreachable, nor that some
      \emph{partial} collapse - a representation using only a few of its dimensions -
      is prevented at all.}}
  \end{center}
  \vskip 8pt
  There is \textbf{no general proof} that an EMA teacher prevents collapse. It is an
  empirical recipe inherited from BYOL and DINO, and it is contested.
  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{itemize}\small
    \item \textbf{C-JEPA} argues I-JEPA's EMA \emph{does not fully prevent collapse}, and
      bolts VICReg's variance and covariance terms on to the same backbone to fix it.
    \item \textbf{SALT} (2025) reports that a \textbf{frozen} teacher beats an EMA teacher
      at matched compute.
    \item \textbf{LeJEPA} (2025) removes the teacher, the stop-gradient and the predictor
      altogether.
  \end{itemize}
  \vskip 10pt
  \begin{center}
    \small\color{armred}The load-bearing mechanism of the most cited paper in this area is
    a trick nobody has justified.
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The other teacher-student method: DINO}
  \small
  DINO shares I-JEPA's row, and it is the one that wins the benchmarks at the end of this
  lecture - so it deserves its mechanism stated, not just its name.
  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{itemize}\small
    \item Same asymmetry: a \textbf{student} network and an \textbf{EMA teacher}, with a
      stop-gradient on the teacher.
    \item But the two branches see \textbf{different hand-crafted crops} of the image - a
      global view for the teacher, small local views for the student - and the student must
      match the teacher's output distribution across views.
    \item Anti-collapse comes from two extra tricks on the teacher's outputs:
      \textbf{centring} (subtract a running mean, stopping one class dominating) and
      \textbf{sharpening} (a low temperature, stopping the output going uniform).
  \end{itemize}
  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{center}
    \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.88\linewidth}{\small\centering
      The difference that matters here: \textbf{DINO needs you to hand-design the
      augmentations.} I-JEPA needs you to hand-design the \textbf{masking} instead.\\
      Neither is free. Remember that when we get to the scoreboard.}}
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The family that came back}
  \small
  The third row has the best origin story, and it is where the field may be heading.
  \vskip 6pt
  LeCun's own account: the joint-embedding methods were \emph{"kind of hacks"} until
  \textbf{Stephane Deny} brought in \textbf{Horace Barlow's 1961} hypothesis - that
  neurons in a visual system work by \textbf{reducing redundancy between each other}.
  \vskip 8pt
  \textbf{Barlow Twins} applies it literally:
  \begin{enumerate}\small
    \item Take the batch of activations from each of the two encoders.
    \item Compute the \textbf{cross-correlation matrix} between them.
    \item Drive it toward the \textbf{identity}: diagonal $\rightarrow 1$ (the two views
      agree), off-diagonal $\rightarrow 0$ (the dimensions stop duplicating each other).
  \end{enumerate}
  \vskip 6pt
  \footnotesize
  \textbf{VICReg} is the simplified successor; \textbf{SIGReg} (LeJEPA, 2025) the current
  one. Keep this row in mind - the last frame of this deck comes back to it.
\end{frame}

\section{I-JEPA, in detail}

\begin{frame}[plain]
  \vfill
  \begin{center}
    {\Large\color{popblue}\textbf{I-JEPA, in detail}}\\[0.9em]
    \begin{minipage}{0.82\textwidth}\centering\small
      The first one that worked.
    \end{minipage}
  \end{center}
  \vfill
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Three networks, and the one you keep}
  \small
  All three are Vision Transformers (ch12).
  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{tabular}{@{}p{3.3cm}p{9.4cm}@{}}
    \textbf{Context encoder} & standard ViT. Sees \textbf{only the visible patches}.
      Trained by gradient descent. \\[5pt]
    \textbf{Target encoder} & same architecture. \textbf{Not} trained by gradients -
      EMA of the context encoder. \\[5pt]
    \textbf{Predictor} & a deliberately \textbf{narrow} ViT: width fixed at
      \textbf{384} whatever the backbone, depth 6 / 12 / 16. \\
  \end{tabular}
  \vskip 10pt
  \begin{center}
    \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.88\linewidth}{\small\centering
      After pretraining you \textbf{keep the target encoder} and \textbf{throw the
      predictor away}. The predictor existed only to make the task solvable.}}
  \end{center}
  \vskip 4pt
  \footnotesize\color{gray}Remember that. Next lecture reverses it exactly.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Multi-block masking}
  \small
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.93\textwidth]{masking_strategy.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vskip -4pt
  \footnotesize
  Sample \textbf{4 target blocks} at scale $(0.15, 0.2)$ and aspect ratio $(0.75, 1.5)$.
  Sample \textbf{one context block} at scale $(0.85, 1.0)$, then \textbf{delete from it
  any region overlapping a target} - otherwise the task is trivial. About \textbf{25\%}
  of patches survive as context.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The subtle bit: targets are masked at the \emph{output}}
  \small
  The target blocks are selected \textbf{after} the target encoder has run, not before.
  \vskip 10pt
  \begin{center}
    \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.88\linewidth}{\small
      The target encoder sees the \textbf{entire, unmasked image}. Only then do we pick
      out the patch embeddings belonging to the target blocks.}}
  \end{center}
  \vskip 10pt
  Why it matters: a patch embedding computed \emph{in context} already reflects the whole
  scene, so it is a \textbf{semantic} target. Encode the block in isolation and you are
  back to predicting local texture.
  \vskip 8pt
  \footnotesize\color{gray}Be honest about the cost: the two branches now see different
  amounts of evidence. The JEPA loss is not the likelihood of anything.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{How we are going to read the numbers}
  \small
  Every result in this deck uses a \textbf{linear probe} on a \textbf{frozen} encoder.
  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{enumerate}\small
    \item Pretrain with no labels. Then \textbf{freeze the encoder completely}.
    \item Train \textbf{one linear layer} on top of it, with labels, to classify.
    \item Report that accuracy.
  \end{enumerate}
  \vskip 10pt
  \begin{center}
    \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.88\linewidth}{\small\centering
      A linear layer can only \emph{read off} what is already there. It cannot fix a bad
      representation. So this measures \textbf{how much usable structure the pretraining
      put in} - not how good the whole system can be made.}}
  \end{center}
  \vskip 6pt
  \footnotesize
  It is also the honest choice: fine-tuning the whole network would let a good optimiser
  paper over a weak representation.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Ablation 1: the masking strategy is not a detail}
  \small
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.74\textwidth]{ablation_masking.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vskip -4pt
  \footnotesize
  A \textbf{34 to 39 point} spread from how you cut up the image, at fixed architecture
  and fixed compute. Larger than most architectural choices this course has taught.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Read the rasterized row}
  \small
  \begin{center}
    \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.86\linewidth}{\small\centering
      \textbf{Rasterized} uses one image quadrant as context: \textbf{25\% of the
      patches}, exactly the same budget as the winning strategy.\\[4pt]
      It scores \textbf{15.5} against \textbf{54.2}.}}
  \end{center}
  \vskip 12pt
  Same amount of information by the patch count. \textbf{39 points worse} by where the
  information sits.
  \vskip 10pt
  Two rules come out of this:
  \begin{itemize}\small
    \item Targets must be \textbf{large enough to be semantic}. A small target is a
      texture patch, and predicting texture teaches you texture.
    \item Context must be \textbf{spatially distributed}. A single corner of an image
      tells you almost nothing about the rest of it.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Ablation 2: the target space}
  \small
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.62\textwidth]{ablation_target.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vskip -4pt
  \footnotesize
  The frame-one result. Same architecture, same masking; only the target changes. If this
  chapter has one number, it is this one: \textbf{what you predict matters more than what
  you predict it with.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What the predictor learns to represent}
  \small
  Train a separate decoder purely to \emph{look} at what the predictor outputs (it is not
  part of I-JEPA). Across samples of the same prediction:
  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.47\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\small
      \textbf{Consistent:} the pose and the object part. The back of a bird. The roof of
      a car. It knows \emph{what} is there.}}
    \column{0.47\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\small
      \textbf{Varies wildly:} exact texture, background, fine detail. It has
      \textbf{declined to represent} them.}}
  \end{columns}
  \vskip 12pt
  \centering
  That is the architecture working as designed: representing what is predictable, and
  staying \textbf{silent} about what is not.
  \vskip 6pt
  \footnotesize\color{gray}Caveat honestly: hand-picked visualisations in a paper, using a
  decoder the method does not otherwise have.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What I-JEPA still gets wrong: it assumes it knows \emph{where}}
  \small
  I-JEPA's predictor is told the \textbf{exact position} of the patch it must predict, via
  fixed positional embeddings.
  \vskip 10pt
  \begin{center}
    \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!10}{\parbox{0.88\linewidth}{\small\centering
      Given only part of a dog, \textbf{you cannot locate its tail precisely.}\\
      The position is genuinely uncertain - and the model is not allowed to say so.}}
  \end{center}
  \vskip 10pt
  \textbf{StoP-JEPA} models each masked position as a \textbf{Gaussian random variable}
  with learned covariance instead of a fixed point. A few lines of code, no extra compute,
  better probing.
  \vskip 8pt
  \footnotesize
  \color{armred}This is the bouncing-ball argument again - applied to \textbf{position}
  instead of content. Fourth appearance. It keeps being the same lesson: \emph{do not
  force a single-valued answer onto a multi-valued question.}
\end{frame}

\section{Does it work? Reading a scoreboard honestly}

\begin{frame}[plain]
  \vfill
  \begin{center}
    {\Large\color{popblue}\textbf{Does it work?}}\\[0.9em]
    \begin{minipage}{0.82\textwidth}\centering\small
      This is the part most summaries get wrong.
    \end{minipage}
  \end{center}
  \vfill
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{ImageNet-1k, linear evaluation}
  \small
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.52\textwidth}
    \scriptsize
    \textbf{No hand-crafted augmentations}\\[3pt]
    \begin{tabular}{@{}llr@{}}
      Method & Arch & Top-1 \\ \hline\rule{0pt}{2.4ex}
      MAE & ViT-H/14 (1600 ep) & 77.2 \\
      data2vec & ViT-L/16 (1600 ep) & 77.3 \\
      CAE & ViT-L/16 (1600 ep) & 78.1 \\
      \textbf{I-JEPA} & \textbf{ViT-H/14 (300 ep)} & \textbf{79.3} \\
      \textbf{I-JEPA} & \textbf{ViT-H/16$_{448}$ (300 ep)} & \textbf{81.1} \\
    \end{tabular}
    \vskip 8pt
    \textbf{With augmentations}\\[3pt]
    \begin{tabular}{@{}llr@{}}
      Method & Arch & Top-1 \\ \hline\rule{0pt}{2.4ex}
      SimCLR v2 & RN152 ($2\times$) & 79.1 \\
      DINO & ViT-B/8 (300 ep) & 80.1 \\
      iBOT & ViT-L/16 (250 ep) & 81.0 \\
    \end{tabular}
    \column{0.44\textwidth}
    \vskip 20pt
    \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.93\linewidth}{\small
      Read it carefully before you repeat it.\\[5pt]
      I-JEPA \textbf{clearly wins its own category}, at a quarter to a half the epochs.\\[5pt]
      It does \textbf{not} beat DINO or iBOT at $224$px. Only the $448$px variant matches
      them.\\[5pt]
      The paper's own words: it \emph{"decreases the gap"}.}}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What it does win: efficiency}
  \small
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.72\textwidth]{efficiency.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vskip -4pt
  \footnotesize
  A ViT-Huge/14 trained on \textbf{16 A100s in under 72 hours} - less compute than a
  ViT-\emph{Small}/16 trained with iBOT. The reason is structural: augmentation-based
  methods process \textbf{ten or more crops} of every image per step. I-JEPA processes
  \textbf{one view}, and its context encoder sees only a quarter of that.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{A slide about where numbers come from}
  \small
  A widely-circulated summary of this paper reports:
  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{center}
    \emph{"I-JEPA achieves \textbf{72.4\%} on 1\% ImageNet against MAE's
    \textbf{59.8\%}."}
  \end{center}
  \vskip 8pt
  The paper's own Table 2 says:
  \vskip 4pt
  \begin{center}
    \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.74\linewidth}{\centering\small
      I-JEPA ViT-H/14, 300 epochs: \textbf{73.3}\\
      MAE ViT-H/14, 1600 epochs: \textbf{71.5}}}
  \end{center}
  \vskip 8pt
  A claimed 12.6-point gap is really \textbf{1.8 points}. Inflated roughly sixfold.
  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{center}
    \small\color{armred}\textbf{And the true story is the better one:} I-JEPA got there
    in \textbf{300 epochs against 1600}. The real result is efficiency, not a blowout.
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Where the image line actually went}
  \small
  Uncomfortable epilogue. The default general-purpose image encoder in 2026 is
  \textbf{DINOv2/v3} - self-distillation \textbf{with} hand-crafted augmentations, the
  family I-JEPA was positioned against.
  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{center}
    \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.88\linewidth}{\small\centering
      DINOv3 (August 2025) reached \textbf{88.4} on ImageNet - against a supervised state
      of the art of 88.6. Its authors: \emph{"the first time that a self-supervised model
      has reached comparable results to weakly and supervised models on image
      classification."}}}
  \end{center}
  \vskip 8pt
  \footnotesize
  \textbf{Not comparable to the 79.3 above} - different protocol, different era, vastly
  more data. The point is not the number. The point is that Meta ships both, and the one
  people build on is not the JEPA.
  \vskip 4pt
  \color{armred}Being right about the objective did not automatically win the benchmark.
  \vskip 6pt
  \color{black}\footnotesize
  \textbf{Does that sink the thesis?} No - but be precise about what survives. The
  \emph{target-space} result (66.9 vs 40.7) is a controlled comparison and stands. What has
  \emph{not} been shown is that \textbf{dropping augmentations} is also a win: DINOv3 keeps
  them and leads. Two separate claims, and this chapter only established the first.
\end{frame}

\section{Where this leaves us}

\begin{frame}[plain]
  \vfill
  \begin{center}
    {\Large\color{popblue}\textbf{Where this leaves us}}\\[0.9em]
    \begin{minipage}{0.82\textwidth}\centering\small
      One idea that travels, one thing it cannot do, one question still open.
    \end{minipage}
  \end{center}
  \vfill
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The objective travels}
  \small
  Predicting in representation space is now standard far beyond images:
  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{itemize}\small
    \item \textbf{Audio and speech} - A-JEPA, Audio-JEPA, WavJEPA (ch13)
    \item \textbf{Biosignals} - EEG, ECG, brain dynamics, genomics
    \item \textbf{3D and point clouds}, \textbf{molecules and polymers}
    \item \textbf{Tabular and time series} - including LaT-PFN, which crosses JEPA with
      the prior-fitted networks of \textbf{ch14}
    \item \textbf{Remote sensing}, \textbf{recommendation}, \textbf{robotics}
  \end{itemize}
  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{center}
    \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.86\linewidth}{\small\centering
      Look at that list again. Almost every one is a domain with \textbf{few labels and
      abundant unlabelled data} - which is exactly the regime where "pretrain without
      labels or augmentations" pays.}}
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What it structurally cannot do}
  \small
  A question you should be asking after ch10: \emph{why not just use JEPA instead of
  diffusion?}
  \vskip 10pt
  \begin{center}
    \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.88\linewidth}{\small\centering
      JEPA is \textbf{not designed to reconstruct anything}. It deliberately discards the
      information a decoder would need. No pixels, no waveforms, no tokens.}}
  \end{center}
  \vskip 10pt
  They are not applying for the same job. Diffusion generates; JEPA understands.
  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{center}
    \small\color{armred}\textbf{The reason it is good at understanding is the reason it
    cannot generate.}
  \end{center}
  \vskip 6pt
  \footnotesize\color{gray}This will matter in L40: a world model you cannot look inside
  is harder to debug than one that draws you a picture.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What is still unresolved}
  \small
  The middle row of the taxonomy - the one I-JEPA depends on - is under active attack.
  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{itemize}\small
    \item \textbf{SALT} (Sept 2025) reports a \textbf{frozen} teacher beating EMA at
      matched compute, and that student quality is remarkably insensitive to teacher
      quality.
    \item \textbf{LeJEPA} (Nov 2025) proves the \textbf{isotropic Gaussian} is the optimal
      embedding distribution and enforces it with a single regulariser (SIGReg) - deleting
      the teacher, the stop-gradient and the predictor. Its authors report one
      hyperparameter and results across 50+ architectures.
  \end{itemize}
  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{center}
    \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!10}{\parbox{0.88\linewidth}{\small\centering
      The claim that would matter most in practice: \textbf{LeJEPA's training loss
      correlates with downstream accuracy.} If that holds, the expensive-probe problem
      from earlier in this lecture goes away.}}
  \end{center}
  \vskip 4pt
  \footnotesize\color{gray}Both are young, and neither has displaced the standard recipe.
  The moment-matching family may yet win.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Recap}
  \small
  \begin{itemize}\small
    \item The choice of \textbf{prediction target} dominates. Representation targets beat
      pixel targets by \textbf{26.2 points} with less training.
    \item Pixel targets fail because they force a \textbf{single-valued answer to a
      multi-valued question}, and because they charge full price for unpredictable detail.
    \item Learned targets buy that freedom at the cost of \textbf{collapse}. All the
      machinery - EMA teachers, stop-gradients, VICReg, SIGReg - exists to pay it.
    \item \textbf{The loss cannot tell you it worked.} Only a downstream probe can.
    \item Read scoreboards carefully. I-JEPA wins its category and \textbf{does not} beat
      DINO at equal resolution.
  \end{itemize}
  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{center}
    \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.88\linewidth}{\small
      \textbf{Next:} everything today happened inside \textbf{one still photograph}. Add a
      time axis and you get a video model. Add \textbf{actions} and you get something you
      can plan with - a \textbf{world model}, and a billion-dollar argument about whether
      it is the future of AI.}}
  \end{center}
\end{frame}
