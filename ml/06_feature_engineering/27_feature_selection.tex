\section{Why select, and when not to}

\begin{frame}[plain]
  \vfill
  \begin{center}
    {\Large\color{popblue}\textbf{Fewer features, on purpose}}\\[0.9em]
    \begin{minipage}{0.82\textwidth}\centering\small
      Five good reasons --- and one honest reason not to bother.
    \end{minipage}
  \end{center}
  \vfill
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Five reasons to drop features}
  \begin{enumerate}\small
    \item \textbf{The curse of dimensionality} (math module 30). Volume grows exponentially with
      $p$; with $n$ fixed, your data gets sparser with every column you add.
    \item \textbf{Multicollinearity} (lecture 22). Correlated features make coefficients unstable
      --- exactly the \texttt{temp}/\texttt{atemp} problem from slide 1.
    \item \textbf{Variance.} More features means more flexibility means more overfitting.
      Regularization helps; it does not eliminate.
    \item \textbf{Compute and latency.} Every feature must be computed, stored, and served ---
      forever, for every prediction, including the useless ones.
    \item \textbf{Interpretability and audit.} ``We use 8 features'' defends far better than
      ``we use 800''. In credit, healthcare and insurance this is not optional.
  \end{enumerate}
  \vfill
  \callout{armorange}{Reason 4 is the one students underrate. A feature is not free once it is
  chosen --- somebody maintains that pipeline at 3am when the upstream source changes.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Predict-first: does adding features always help?}
  You add candidates one at a time, best-first, refitting Ridge after each.
  \vfill
  \textbf{What happens to (a) the training error, (b) the test error?}
  \pause
  \vskip 2pt
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.66\linewidth]{fig/fs_add_features_ucurve.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vskip -2pt
  \callout{paramgreen}{\textbf{Training error falls forever} --- more columns can only fit the
  training set better. \textbf{Test error is U-shaped}: down while features carry signal, then up
  as they bring more variance than they remove bias. Selection is the search for that minimum.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{So how much does selection actually buy?}
  Two regimes, same data, same model, same split:
  \vskip 3pt
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.76\linewidth]{fig/fs_both_regimes.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vskip -2pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.49\textwidth}
    \footnotesize \textbf{Eleven honest features.} Recursive elimination with cross-validation
    kept \textbf{all eleven}. Selection bought \textbf{exactly nothing} --- not ``a little'',
    \emph{zero}.
    \column{0.49\textwidth}
    \footnotesize \textbf{Two hundred and thirty candidates.} It kept \textbf{45} and bought
    $\mathbf{+68.2}$ mean absolute error (MAE).
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{When \emph{not} to select}
  \callout{paramgreen}{\textbf{Selection pays in proportion to how much junk you generated.} If you
  did not mass-produce candidates, you probably do not need this lecture's machinery --- fit the
  model on everything and regularize.}
  \vfill
  Three cases where selection is not worth it:
  \begin{itemize}\small
    \item \textbf{$p$ is small and every feature was chosen deliberately.} The bike eleven.
    \item \textbf{You are already regularizing hard.} Ridge and Lasso select \emph{softly}
      (lecture 7); a hard cut can only lose the small-but-real signal they were keeping.
    \item \textbf{$n \gg p$.} With plenty of rows, the variance cost of a weak feature is small.
  \end{itemize}
  \vfill
  \callout{armred}{And note the orange line on the previous slide. Engineering 219 features and
  selecting the best 45 got Ridge to $505.5$. A \textbf{random forest on the raw eleven}, with no
  feature engineering and no selection at all, gets $\mathbf{455.1}$. Sometimes the answer is
  ``change the model'', not ``change the features''.}
\end{frame}

\section{You already own half the toolkit}

\begin{frame}[plain]
  \vfill
  \begin{center}
    {\Large\color{popblue}\textbf{You have done this before}}\\[0.9em]
    \begin{minipage}{0.82\textwidth}\centering\small
      without calling it selection.
    \end{minipage}
  \end{center}
  \vfill
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Refresher: what each chapter-5 tool measures}
  \begin{center}\small
    \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.25}
    \begin{tabular}{@{}l l l@{}}
      \textbf{Tool} & \textbf{The question it answers} & \textbf{From} \\ \hline
      \textbf{Permutation feature importance} (PFI) & how much does the model \emph{rely} on it? & L23 \\
      \textbf{Conditional feature importance} (CFI) & \ldots\ beyond what its correlated partners give? & L23 \\
      \textbf{Leave-One-Covariate-Out} (LOCO) & how much worse if I \emph{delete} it and refit? & L23 \\
      \textbf{Shapley Additive Global importance} (SAGE) & what is its fair share of the performance? & L23 \\
      \textbf{SHapley Additive exPlanations} (SHAP) & how much did it move \emph{this} prediction? & L24 \\
      \textbf{Lasso} coefficient path & is it worth a non-zero coefficient? & L7, L22 \\
    \end{tabular}
  \end{center}
  \vfill
  \callout{armblue}{Six tools, six different questions --- and \textbf{six ordered lists}. Slide 1
  is what happens when two of them disagree: they were never answering the same question.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Read them again as selection algorithms}
  \begin{itemize}\small
    \item \textbf{Leave-One-Covariate-Out is one step of backward elimination.} Drop a feature,
      refit, measure. You have already run it. Repeat it $p$ times greedily and you have
      \emph{recursive feature elimination} (section 4).
    \item \textbf{The Lasso path is embedded selection.} A coefficient hitting exactly zero
      \emph{is} the feature being dropped. You have watched this happen since lecture 7.
    \item \textbf{Impurity and permutation importance are the input} that wrapper methods consume
      --- they decide \emph{which} feature to drop next.
    \item \textbf{Boruta's shadow features} (section 4) are permutation importance with a null
      distribution bolted on: instead of ``how important?'', ask ``more important than a
      \emph{shuffled copy} of itself?''
  \end{itemize}
  \vfill
  \callout{paramgreen}{You are not starting from zero. You are missing exactly three things ---
  and the next slides are those three.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Two different goals, and students conflate them}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.48\textwidth}
    \textbf{Minimal-optimal}
    \vskip 3pt
    \footnotesize The \emph{smallest} subset that predicts as well as the full set.
    \begin{itemize}\scriptsize
      \item Lasso, recursive elimination, stability selection
      \item correlated duplicates get dropped \emph{arbitrarily}
      \item what you want when you \textbf{ship a model}
    \end{itemize}
    \column{0.48\textwidth}
    \textbf{All-relevant}
    \vskip 3pt
    \footnotesize \emph{Every} feature carrying signal about $y$, duplicates included.
    \begin{itemize}\scriptsize
      \item Boruta
      \item keeps both members of a correlated pair
      \item what you want when you are \textbf{doing science}
    \end{itemize}
  \end{columns}
  \vfill
  \callout{armorange}{\textbf{Bike makes this concrete, and it finally answers slide 1.}
  Minimal-optimal \emph{drops} \texttt{atemp} --- given \texttt{temp}, it adds nothing.
  All-relevant \emph{keeps} it --- it genuinely predicts bike rentals. \textbf{Same data, both
  answers correct, different question.} You cannot pick a method until you know which one you are
  asking.}
  \vskip 3pt
  \footnotesize \scriptsize The distinction is due to Nilsson et al.\ (2007).
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{So what is actually left to learn?}
  A ranking gives you an order. It does not give you:
  \vfill
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{A cutoff.} Where does the list stop? \emph{(sections 3 and 4)}
    \item \textbf{A way to score a \emph{subset} rather than a feature.} Features interact; the
      best three individually are rarely the best three together. \emph{(section 4)}
    \item \textbf{A guarantee the answer is stable.} Run it on a slightly different sample --- do
      you get the same list? \emph{(section 5)}
  \end{enumerate}
  \vfill
  \callout{armblue}{Three families of methods map onto these: \textbf{filter} (cheap, one feature
  at a time), \textbf{embedded} (free with the model --- that is chapter 5), and \textbf{wrapper}
  (expensive, scores subsets). We spend the rest of the lecture on filter and wrapper, because you
  already have embedded.}
\end{frame}

\section{Filter methods}

\begin{frame}[plain]
  \vfill
  \begin{center}
    {\Large\color{popblue}\textbf{Score every feature once}}\\[0.9em]
    \begin{minipage}{0.82\textwidth}\centering\small
      Before you fit anything at all.
    \end{minipage}
  \end{center}
  \vfill
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The three families}
  \begin{center}\small
    \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.2}
    \begin{tabular}{@{}p{2.4cm} p{5.0cm} p{5.4cm}@{}}
      \textbf{Family} & \textbf{How it works} & \textbf{Trade-off} \\ \hline
      \textbf{Filter} & score each feature \emph{independently} against $y$, keep the top $k$
        & fast, model-agnostic. \textbf{Blind to interactions.} \\
      \textbf{Embedded} & the model selects \emph{while} fitting (Lasso, tree importances)
        & cheap, model-aware. Tied to that one model. \\
      \textbf{Wrapper} & search over \emph{subsets}, score each by cross-validation
        & most accurate. Refits the model $\mathcal{O}(p)$ times. \\
    \end{tabular}
  \end{center}
  \vfill
  \textbf{The standard funnel} --- cost rises left to right, so spend it late:
  \begin{center}\small
    $10{,}000 \;\xrightarrow[\text{filter}]{}\; 500
     \;\xrightarrow[\text{embedded}]{}\; 50
     \;\xrightarrow[\text{wrapper}]{}\; 15$
  \end{center}
  \vfill
  \footnotesize The canonical survey is Guyon \& Elisseeff (2003), still worth reading.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[fragile]{Variance thresholds and univariate filters}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.52\textwidth}
    \footnotesize
    \textbf{Step 1: drop the near-constants.} A column that never varies cannot predict anything.
    Free, and always worth doing.
    \vskip 5pt
    \textbf{Step 2: score each feature against $y$.}
    \begin{itemize}\scriptsize
      \item \textbf{Regression:} \texttt{f\_regression} (linear association only),
        \texttt{mutual\_info\_regression} (\emph{any} dependence)
      \item \textbf{Classification:} \texttt{f\_classif}, \texttt{chi2},
        \texttt{mutual\_info\_classif}
    \end{itemize}
    \column{0.46\textwidth}
{\lstset{basicstyle=\ttfamily\scriptsize}
\begin{lstlisting}
X = VarianceThreshold(0.01).fit_transform(X)

# top 20 by linear association
SelectKBest(f_regression, k=20).fit(X, y)

# top 20 by ANY dependence
SelectKBest(mutual_info_regression,
            k=20).fit(X, y)
\end{lstlisting}}
  \end{columns}
  \vfill
  \callout{armorange}{The choice between those two scores is not cosmetic. \texttt{f\_regression}
  measures \emph{linear} association only --- it is blind to exactly the effects lecture 26 spent a
  slide binning.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Where the two scores disagree}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.46\textwidth}
    \begin{center}
      \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig/fs_filter_scores.pdf}
    \end{center}
    \column{0.52\textwidth}
    \vskip 6pt
    Ten of the eleven features rank almost identically under both scores. \textbf{One moves three
    places: \texttt{mnth}.}
    \vskip 6pt
    \footnotesize
    F-test rank \textbf{6} $\rightarrow$ mutual-information rank \textbf{3}.
    \vskip 6pt
    Why: month is \textbf{cyclic}. Rentals are low in January, high in July, low again in December,
    so the \emph{linear} association is weak while the actual dependence is strong. The F-test
    cannot see a shape it cannot draw with a straight line.
    \vskip 6pt
    This is exactly what the cyclic $\sin/\cos$ encoding from lecture 3 exists to fix.
  \end{columns}
  \vfill
  \callout{armblue}{If you had filtered on \texttt{f\_regression} alone at $k=5$, you would have
  thrown away \texttt{mnth} --- the third most informative column you had.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The independence trap}
  Filters score each feature \emph{alone}. Consider two binary features with $y = x_1 \oplus x_2$:
  \vskip 3pt
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.86\linewidth]{fig/fs_xor_trap.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vskip -2pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.49\textwidth}
    \footnotesize Each feature alone: \textbf{no signal}. Mean $y$ is the same whether $x_1$ is 0
    or 1. Correlation $\approx 0$, mutual information $\approx 0$.
    \column{0.49\textwidth}
    \footnotesize Together: \textbf{a perfect predictor.} Any filter, univariate or not, deletes
    both features before the model ever sees them.
  \end{columns}
  \vskip 3pt
  \callout{armred}{\textbf{A filter is a screen, not a decision.} Use it to go from 10{,}000 to
  500, never from 500 to 15. A feature that fails on its own may be indispensable in company.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Multivariate filters}
  Filters that look at features \emph{together}, without the cost of a wrapper:
  \vfill
  \begin{itemize}\small
    \item \textbf{Minimum Redundancy Maximum Relevance} (mRMR). Greedily pick the feature
      maximising (relevance to $y$) $-$ (mean correlation with what you already picked). Directly
      attacks the \texttt{temp}/\texttt{atemp} problem: the second one adds relevance but also
      redundancy.
    \item \textbf{Cluster-and-represent.} Hierarchically cluster the features by correlation, keep
      one representative per cluster. Crude, cheap, effective when $p$ is huge.
    \item \textbf{Hilbert-Schmidt Independence Criterion Lasso} (HSIC-Lasso). Kernel-based, catches
      non-linear dependence, scales to many thousands of features.
  \end{itemize}
  \vfill
  \footnotesize Libraries: \texttt{mrmr-selection}, \texttt{scikit-feature}. These are not the main
  event in most projects, but they earn their keep in genomics and sensor data, where $p$ is in the
  tens of thousands and half the columns are near-duplicates.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{How many features pass by pure chance?}
  Score \textbf{10{,}000 columns of random numbers} against the bike target:
  \vskip 3pt
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.84\linewidth]{fig/fs_multiple_testing.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vskip -2pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.53\textwidth}
    \footnotesize \textbf{528} clear $\alpha = 0.05$. The best random column reaches
    $p = 1.17 \times 10^{-4}$ --- a number most people would call significant without blinking.
    \column{0.45\textwidth}
    \footnotesize The $p$-values are \emph{uniform}, exactly as theory says. 5\% of them land below
    $0.05$ because that is what $0.05$ \emph{means}.
  \end{columns}
  \vskip 3pt
  \callout{armred}{Control the family-wise error rate with \textbf{Bonferroni} ($\alpha/m$) or the
  false discovery rate with \textbf{Benjamini-Hochberg} (1995) --- \texttt{SelectFwe},
  \texttt{SelectFdr}. Both remove all 528. \textbf{Almost nobody applies them to a feature pool},
  and last lecture's 230 mechanical candidates are exactly this problem at smaller scale.}
\end{frame}

\section{Wrapper methods}

\begin{frame}[plain]
  \vfill
  \begin{center}
    {\Large\color{popblue}\textbf{Stop scoring features}}\\[0.9em]
    \begin{minipage}{0.82\textwidth}\centering\small
      Start scoring subsets.
    \end{minipage}
  \end{center}
  \vfill
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[fragile]{Recursive Feature Elimination}
  \textbf{Recursive Feature Elimination (RFE).} Fit the model. Rank the features. Drop the weakest.
  \emph{Refit.} Repeat.
  \vfill
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.50\textwidth}
    \footnotesize
    \textbf{Why the refit matters.} Importances are not fixed --- they change as features leave.
    Drop \texttt{atemp} and \texttt{temp}'s importance \emph{jumps}, because it is no longer
    splitting the credit with a near-duplicate (lecture 22).
    \vskip 4pt
    A one-shot ranking cannot see that. Recursive elimination re-measures after every removal,
    which is exactly why it beats ``keep the top $k$ of a single ranking''.
    \column{0.48\textwidth}
{\lstset{basicstyle=\ttfamily\scriptsize}
\begin{lstlisting}
from sklearn.feature_selection import RFE

rfe = RFE(Ridge(),
          n_features_to_select=15,
          step=1).fit(X, y)
X_sel = X[:, rfe.support_]
\end{lstlisting}}
    \vskip 2pt
    \footnotesize \textbf{Cost:} $\mathcal{O}(p)$ refits. Use a fast model, or pre-filter first.
  \end{columns}
  \vfill
  \footnotesize Introduced for gene selection with support vector machines by Guyon et al.\ (2002),
  where $p$ was in the thousands and $n$ in the dozens.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Where the cutoff finally comes from}
  \texttt{RFECV} cross-validates \emph{at every subset size} and keeps the best one. The cutoff is
  no longer your judgement call --- it is a measurement.
  \vskip 3pt
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.84\linewidth]{fig/fs_rfecv_curve.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vskip -2pt
  \callout{paramgreen}{On the 230 candidates it chose $\mathbf{k = 45}$. \textbf{This slide answers
  slide 2:} you do not read the cutoff off a bar chart, you find it with cross-validation.
  \emph{Hand-designed} features survived at the highest rate of any kind ($2$ of $3$, versus $13\%$
  of the ratios) --- but there were only three of them to begin with.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[fragile]{Sequential selection: forward and backward}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.52\textwidth}
    \footnotesize
    \textbf{Forward} --- start empty, repeatedly add the feature that most improves
    cross-validation. Fast when you want \emph{few} features.
    \vskip 4pt
    \textbf{Backward} --- start full, repeatedly drop the one whose removal hurts least. Fast when
    you want to drop only a few. \emph{This is Leave-One-Covariate-Out, run greedily to
    exhaustion.}
    \column{0.46\textwidth}
{\lstset{basicstyle=\ttfamily\scriptsize}
\begin{lstlisting}
SequentialFeatureSelector(
    Ridge(),
    n_features_to_select=15,
    direction="forward",   # or "backward"
    cv=5).fit(X, y)
\end{lstlisting}}
  \end{columns}
  \vfill
  \callout{armred}{\textbf{Both are greedy, and greedy fails on the exclusive-or (XOR) trap.}
  Forward selection
  evaluates $x_1$ alone (useless), then $x_2$ alone (useless), and never adds either --- so it
  never discovers that together they are perfect. Backward selection has the mirror problem. When
  features only pay off jointly, \emph{no} greedy method finds them.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Boruta: asking a different question}
  Every method so far asks ``which features are \emph{best}?'' Boruta asks
  \textbf{``which features are better than \emph{nothing}?''}
  \vfill
  \textbf{The algorithm} (Kursa \& Rudnicki, 2010):
  \begin{enumerate}\small
    \item For every real feature, add a \textbf{shadow} --- a shuffled copy of that same column.
      It has the same distribution and, by construction, \emph{no relationship to $y$}.
    \item Fit a random forest on real $+$ shadow features together.
    \item Any real feature scoring above the \textbf{maximum} shadow importance gets a hit.
      (The maximum, not the mean --- this is the multiple-testing correction from slide 20, built in.)
    \item Repeat with fresh shuffles. Over many iterations, a feature that consistently beats the
      best shadow is \textbf{confirmed}; one that consistently loses is \textbf{rejected}.
  \end{enumerate}
  \vfill
  \callout{armblue}{This is the \textbf{all-relevant} method from slide 12. It is not trying to
  find a small set --- it is trying to find \emph{every} column that carries signal.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Boruta on bike --- and a flat contradiction}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.55\textwidth}
    \begin{center}
      \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig/fs_boruta_shadow.pdf}
    \end{center}
    \column{0.43\textwidth}
    \vskip 4pt
    \footnotesize
    \textbf{Both \texttt{temp} and \texttt{atemp} confirmed} --- as slide 12 predicted, all-relevant
    keeps the correlated pair that recursive elimination throws half of away.
    \vskip 6pt
    \textbf{But look at the bottom three.} Boruta \emph{rejects} \texttt{holiday},
    \texttt{weekday} and \texttt{workingday}: no better than a shuffled column.
  \end{columns}
  \vskip 2pt
  \callout{armred}{\textbf{And the next slide will show Lasso keeping \texttt{weekday} in 100\% of
  bootstrap samples.} Same eleven features, same data, opposite verdicts. Not a bug: Boruta asks
  ``does this beat noise \emph{inside a forest}?'' while Lasso asks ``does this earn a coefficient
  in a \emph{linear} model?'' A weekday effect a tree already absorbs through \texttt{season} and
  \texttt{temp} can still be worth its own coefficient.}
\end{frame}

\section{Stability: is the answer real?}

\begin{frame}[plain]
  \vfill
  \begin{center}
    {\Large\color{popblue}\textbf{Run it twice}}\\[0.9em]
    \begin{minipage}{0.82\textwidth}\centering\small
      Do you get the same features?
    \end{minipage}
  \end{center}
  \vfill
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Stability selection}
  One Lasso fit gives one answer. Fit it on \textbf{100 bootstrap resamples} and count how often
  each feature survives (Meinshausen \& B\"uhlmann, 2010):
  \vskip 3pt
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.72\linewidth]{fig/fs_stability.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vskip -2pt
  \callout{armorange}{\textbf{\texttt{atemp} 99\%, \texttt{temp} 68\% --- but one of the two is
  selected in 100\% of resamples.} They are not splitting the credit evenly; \texttt{atemp} is
  marginally the better predictor, so Lasso reaches for it first and \texttt{temp} only gets in
  when the resample happens to favour it. \textbf{Correlated features split credit in proportion to
  a small edge}, and the weaker one then looks dispensable when it is not.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What stability tells you that a single fit cannot}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.48\textwidth}
    \textbf{The pair}
    \vskip 3pt
    \footnotesize Report the \emph{union}, not the members. ``One temperature column, always'' is
    the true finding; ``\texttt{temp} is borderline at 68\%'' is an artefact of having two.
    \column{0.50\textwidth}
    \textbf{The genuinely unstable one}
    \vskip 3pt
    \footnotesize \texttt{mnth} survives in \textbf{9\%} of resamples --- it flickers in and out.
    And it is the same column that the F-test undersold on slide 17. A feature a \emph{linear}
    model can barely use is exactly the one a \emph{linear} selector cannot make its mind up about.
  \end{columns}
  \vfill
  \callout{paramgreen}{Stability selection also answers \emph{how many} for free: $k$ is however
  many features clear your threshold. No elbow to eyeball, no $k$ to guess.}
  \vfill
  \footnotesize \textbf{Four ways to pick $k$, in practice:} the \texttt{RFECV} curve, a stability
  threshold, Boruta's shadow cutoff --- and \textbf{a hard business constraint}. ``The credit model
  ships with twelve features because a regulator reads all of them'' is a real reason, and it
  overrules all three of the others.
\end{frame}

\section{Pitfalls}

\begin{frame}[plain]
  \vfill
  \begin{center}
    {\Large\color{popblue}\textbf{The mistake that makes everything look great}}\\[0.9em]
    \begin{minipage}{0.82\textwidth}\centering\small
      And it is the same mistake as last lecture's, one level up.
    \end{minipage}
  \end{center}
  \vfill
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Selection bias: selecting outside the loop}
  Rank all 230 features on the \textbf{whole dataset}, keep the top 20, \emph{then} cross-validate
  them. The selection already saw the test rows.
  \vskip 3pt
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.74\linewidth]{fig/fs_selection_bias.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vskip -2pt
  \callout{armred}{On real candidates the lie is worth $+5.8$ MAE --- unpleasant but survivable.
  On \textbf{500 columns of pure noise} it is worth $\mathbf{+201.1}$, and the model looks
  respectable \emph{on data containing no signal whatsoever}. \textbf{The bias is worst exactly
  when you have the most junk} --- which is to say, right after last lecture.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[fragile]{The fix is the same rule as lecture 6}
  Cross-validation estimates the performance of a \textbf{procedure}, not of a fitted model. If
  selection is part of your procedure, it goes \emph{inside} the loop.
  \vfill
{\lstset{basicstyle=\ttfamily\scriptsize}
\begin{lstlisting}
# WRONG - selection saw every row before the split
X_sel = SelectKBest(f_regression, k=20).fit_transform(X, y)
cross_val_score(Ridge(), X_sel, y, cv=5)

# RIGHT - selection is refitted inside every fold
pipe = Pipeline([("sel", SelectKBest(f_regression, k=20)),
                 ("sc",  StandardScaler()),
                 ("m",   Ridge())])
cross_val_score(pipe, X, y, cv=5)
\end{lstlisting}}
  \vfill
  \callout{paramgreen}{Identical in shape to lecture 26's rule for engineered features: \textbf{if
  it learns anything from the data, it belongs inside the \texttt{Pipeline}.} A selector learns
  \emph{which columns to keep} --- that is learning, and it leaks.}
  \vskip 3pt
  \footnotesize And if you tune hyperparameters \emph{and} select features, you need
  \textbf{nested} cross-validation --- an outer loop for the estimate, an inner one for the choices.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Four more pitfalls}
  \begin{enumerate}\small
    \item \textbf{Leakage through the encoder.} If a feature was target-encoded without
      out-of-fold discipline (lecture 26), selection just ranks the leak highest. Fix the encoder
      before you trust the ranking.
    \item \textbf{Model-dependence.} Lasso's picks are not the forest's picks --- slide 26 is a
      whole slide about that. \textbf{Selection results do not transfer across model classes.}
      Change the model, redo the selection.
    \item \textbf{Arbitrary thresholds.} ``Keep importance $> 0.01$'' is a number somebody made up.
      Use a cross-validated curve, a stability frequency, or a shadow comparison --- all three are
      defensible, and $0.01$ is not.
    \item \textbf{Forgetting the baseline.} Always compare against keeping everything. Slide 8 did
      exactly this and found selection was worth \emph{zero} on the raw eleven. \textbf{Selection
      that does not beat keep-all was not worth doing.}
  \end{enumerate}
\end{frame}

  cross-validation loop, and compare against keeping everything.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Recap}
  \begin{itemize}\small
    \item \textbf{A ranking is not a decision.} Chapter 5's tools order features; none of them
      picks a cutoff. That is what filter and wrapper methods are for.
    \item \textbf{Selection pays in proportion to the junk you generated:} $+0.0$ MAE on the
      honest eleven, $+68.2$ on 230 mechanical candidates.
    \item \textbf{Filter is a screen, not a decision} --- fast, but blind to interactions (XOR)
      and to non-linear effects (\texttt{mnth}, rank 6 by F-test and rank 3 by mutual information).
    \item \textbf{Wrappers score subsets.} \texttt{RFECV} finds the cutoff by cross-validation;
      it chose $k=45$. Greedy sequential methods still miss jointly-useful pairs.
    \item \textbf{Minimal-optimal $\neq$ all-relevant.} Recursive elimination drops \texttt{atemp};
      Boruta keeps it. \textbf{Both are right} --- so answer slide 1 by naming your question.
    \item \textbf{Check stability.} \texttt{atemp} 99\%, \texttt{temp} 68\%, but one of them is in
      100\% of resamples. Report the union.
    \item \textbf{Select inside the cross-validation loop.} Outside it, 500 pure-noise columns
      produced a respectable-looking model.
  \end{itemize}
  \vfill
  \callout{paramgreen}{\textbf{Next: classic methods} --- k-nearest neighbours, naive Bayes,
  discriminant analysis, support vector machines and Gaussian processes. Models where, unlike
  trees, the features you feed in are the features it gets.}
\end{frame}
