% ============================================================ SECTION 1
\section{Assembling the context}
\sectiontransition{Assembling the context}{You have a ranked list. The model needs a
prompt. That is not the same object.}

\begin{frame}{Three decisions between the list and the prompt}
  \vspace{0.6em}
  The retriever hands you 100 chunks with scores. You cannot paste 100 chunks. Someone has
  to decide:

  \vspace{0.9em}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.31\textwidth}
      \textbf{\color{popblue}How many?}\\[0.3em]
      {\small A token budget you are paying for on every question.}
    \column{0.31\textwidth}
      \textbf{\color{popblue}In what order?}\\[0.3em]
      {\small Position inside the prompt is not neutral.}
    \column{0.31\textwidth}
      \textbf{\color{popblue}Which are the same?}\\[0.3em]
      {\small Overlapping chunks and old revisions repeat the same sentence.}
  \end{columns}

  \vspace{1.2em}
  \keybox{This step has no model in it and no maths. It is plumbing. It is also where a
  surprising share of real RAG bugs live, because nobody thinks it is worth attention.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{How much actually fits}
  \vspace{0.1em}
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.86\linewidth]{fig/l43_02_token_budget.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vspace{-0.4em}
  {\small Real token counts from one technical document of 24{,}519 tokens, cut into
  800-character chunks. Equal-sized chunks are \emph{not} equal-cost: 150 to 340 tokens each,
  because tables and formulas tokenize badly.}

  \vspace{0.3em}
  \watchbox{A 2{,}000-token context holds \textbf{11 of the 128 chunks} - 8.6\% of one
  document. Your budget is far smaller than it feels.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Where in the prompt matters}
  \vspace{0.7em}
  Models do not read a prompt uniformly. A widely reproduced result (Liu et al., 2023) is
  that accuracy is highest when the needed passage sits near the \textbf{start} or the
  \textbf{end} of a long context, and lowest when it is buried in the middle.

  \vspace{1.0em}
  \begin{center}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{ll}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Do} & \textbf{Why} \\
    \midrule
    Put the highest-scoring chunk first   & it is the one you are betting on \\
    Keep the context short                & fewer places for the answer to hide \\
    Label each chunk with an id           & so the model can cite it \\
    Include the metadata line             & date and source change the reading \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.9em}
  \watchbox{Do not sort your chunks alphabetically, by document name, or by upload date
  because it was easier to implement. Sort by score, and know that you did.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The same sentence, three times}
  \vspace{0.2em}
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.72\linewidth]{fig/l43_03_dedup_similarity.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vspace{-0.4em}
  {\small Measured on our corpus. The three chunks about press pressure - two revisions and
  the original paragraph - all sit above 0.89. Two chunks with nothing in common still sit
  at \textbf{0.767}.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The dedup trap}
  \vspace{0.5em}
  The obvious rule is: drop any chunk that is more than 0.90 similar to one you already
  kept. Look at what that rule does here.

  \vspace{0.9em}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.47\textwidth}
      \textbf{\color{armred}What it deletes}\\[0.4em]
      {\small Revision 2 and revision 4 score 0.952 against each other. One of them gets
      dropped. Nothing in a cosine tells you which one is current.}
    \column{0.47\textwidth}
      \textbf{\color{armred}Why the threshold is a guess}\\[0.4em]
      {\small Unrelated text already scores 0.767 on this model. The usable range is
      0.77 to 1.00, not 0 to 1, and it moves when you change model.}
  \end{columns}

  \vspace{1.1em}
  \takebox{Deduplicate on \textbf{metadata first} - same document, same section, newer
  revision wins - and only then on similarity. A date is a fact. A cosine is an opinion.}
\end{frame}

% ============================================================ SECTION 2
\section{Prompting so the answer stays grounded}
\sectiontransition{Prompting so the answer stays grounded}{The model will happily answer
from memory. You have to make that the harder option.}

\begin{frame}{What all of that actually produces}
  \vspace{0.3em}
  {\footnotesize Three decisions, one filter, three chunks. This is the literal string the
  model receives - nothing is hidden behind an abstraction:}

  \vspace{0.3em}
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=lightbg, colframe=gray, boxrule=0.5pt, arc=2pt,
                    width=0.96\textwidth, left=4pt, right=4pt, top=3pt, bottom=3pt]
    \ttfamily\scriptsize
    Answer the question using ONLY the context below.\\
    Cite the block number you used, like [1].\\
    If the context does not contain the answer, reply exactly: I don't know.\\[0.4em]
    CONTEXT\\
    \lbrack1\rbrack\ (press\_manual, rev 4, 2024)\\
    Press manual revision 4, issued 2024: the Lori press starts at 2.5 bar and is\\
    raised to 3.2 bar after twenty minutes.\\[0.3em]
    \lbrack2\rbrack\ (equipment\_log, rev 1, 2022)\\
    Model PRS-400 replaces the older PRS-220 press on line two.\\[0.4em]
    QUESTION\\
    What pressure should the Lori press run at?\\[0.4em]
    ANSWER
  \end{tcolorbox}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.2em}
  {\footnotesize\color{gray}Produced by \texttt{ml/ch17\_rag/rag\_demo.py}, which you can run
  and change. Retrieval, filtering and this prompt are about 140 lines in total.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The prompt is a contract}
  \vspace{0.4em}
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=lightbg, colframe=gray, boxrule=0.5pt, arc=2pt, width=0.92\textwidth]
    \small\ttfamily
    Answer using ONLY the passages below.\\
    After every sentence, cite the passage id, like [3].\\
    If the passages do not contain the answer, reply exactly:\\
    "I do not know from the documents I have."\\[0.6em]
    {[1]} (rev.4, 2024) The Lori press starts at 2.5 bar, raised to\\
    \phantom{[1]} 3.2 bar after twenty minutes.\\
    {[2]} (rev.2, 2019) The Lori press is held at 2.5 bar throughout.\\[0.6em]
    Question: What pressure should the Lori press run at?
  \end{tcolorbox}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.4em}
  \keybox{Three separate instructions: \textbf{restrict the source}, \textbf{demand a
  citation}, \textbf{license a refusal}. Drop any one of them and you lose a different
  guarantee.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Licensing ``I do not know''}
  \vspace{0.2em}
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.68\linewidth]{fig/l43_04_grounding_outcomes.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vspace{-0.5em}
  \keybox{A model that never refuses will invent. A model that refuses too easily is
  useless. This is a \textbf{trade-off you choose}, and where you set it depends on what a
  wrong answer costs: a wrong pressure setting breaks a press, a wrong film recommendation
  does not.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Making the refusal reachable}
  \vspace{0.7em}
  ``Say you do not know if you are unsure'' does not work well, because the model has no
  calibrated sense of unsure. Give it a mechanical test instead:

  \vspace{0.9em}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Name the exact refusal string.} A fixed sentence you can detect in code
      and count. ``I do not know'' phrased twelve ways is not measurable.
    \item \textbf{Make refusal a valid answer, not a failure.} If the prompt only shows
      examples of successful answers, refusing looks off-distribution.
    \item \textbf{Refuse per claim, not per question.} ``The pressure is 2.5 bar [1]; the
      humidity is not in these documents'' beats refusing the whole thing.
    \item \textbf{Add a retrieval-score floor.} If the best chunk scores far below what a
      real match scores, do not call the model at all.
  \end{itemize}

  \vspace{0.9em}
  \watchbox{The score floor needs a number, and that number is corpus-specific. Ours would
  have to sit above 0.767, because that is what two unrelated chunks already score.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What a citation does and does not prove}
  \vspace{0.8em}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.46\textwidth}
      \textbf{\color{paramgreen}A citation gives you}\\[0.5em]
      {\small
      A place to look.\\[0.4em]
      A reader who can check in ten seconds.\\[0.4em]
      A trail when the answer is later found to be wrong.}
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
      \textbf{\color{armred}A citation does not give you}\\[0.5em]
      {\small
      Any guarantee that the cited passage says it.\\[0.4em]
      Any guarantee that the passage is current.\\[0.4em]
      Any guarantee the model used it rather than attached it afterwards.}
  \end{columns}

  \vspace{1.2em}
  \trapbox{Citations are a \textbf{verification aid}, not a correctness mechanism. The
  system that produces the citation is the same system you do not trust. That is the whole
  problem with checking a model's work using the model.}
\end{frame}

% ============================================================ SECTION 3
\section{How this breaks}
\sectiontransition{How this breaks}{Four places a pipeline leaks, and the uncomfortable
fact that your metrics only see two of them.}

\begin{frame}{Where a pipeline leaks}
  \vspace{0.3em}
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.80\linewidth]{fig/l43_05_failure_ladder.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vspace{-0.5em}
  {\small The green boxes are the ones a standard evaluation catches. The two hardest
  failures - a destroyed chunk and a self-contradicting corpus - are in red.}

  \vspace{0.25em}
  \keybox{\footnotesize The first two leaks you have already met: \textbf{chunking} was
  lecture 41's recall ceiling, \textbf{retrieval} was most of lecture 42. The next three
  slides cover what is left - the failures that appear \emph{after} retrieval succeeded.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Failure 1: retrieved, and then ignored}
  \vspace{0.7em}
  The correct chunk is in the prompt. The model answers from its training data anyway.

  \vspace{0.9em}
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=lightbg, colframe=gray, boxrule=0.5pt, arc=2pt, width=0.9\textwidth]
    \small
    \textbf{Context:} ``The Lori press starts at 2.5 bar\ldots''\\
    \textbf{Answer:} ``Cheese presses typically operate between 4 and 6 bar.''
  \end{tcolorbox}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.9em}
  It happens most when the retrieved fact \textbf{contradicts what the model already
  believes}, and when the context is long enough that the relevant line is easy to skip.

  \vspace{0.8em}
  \takebox{Cheap defences: a shorter context, the relevant chunk placed first, and an
  explicit ``only from the passages'' instruction. All three make the training-data answer
  the harder path.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Failure 2: right answer, wrong citation}
  \vspace{0.7em}
  The answer is correct. The bracket after it points at the wrong passage.

  \vspace{0.9em}
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=lightbg, colframe=gray, boxrule=0.5pt, arc=2pt, width=0.9\textwidth]
    \small
    \textit{``The ripening cellars are held at 10 degrees [2].''}\\[0.3em]
    \ldots where passage [2] is the brine tank, and passage [4] is the cellar.
  \end{tcolorbox}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.9em}
  This looks harmless and is not. It is the failure that \textbf{destroys trust fastest},
  because it is the one a careful reader finds first: they click the citation, it does not
  say that, and now they doubt everything else on the page.

  \vspace{0.8em}
  \watchbox{It is also easy to check mechanically: for every cited id, test whether that
  passage actually supports that sentence. You do not need a human for this, and almost
  nobody does it.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Failure 3: the sources disagree}
  \vspace{0.6em}
  This is the one from the cold open, and it is different in kind from the other two.

  \vspace{0.9em}
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=lightbg, colframe=gray, boxrule=0.5pt, arc=2pt, width=0.92\textwidth]
    \small
    \textbf{[1] rev.4, 2024:} starts at 2.5 bar, raised to 3.2 bar after twenty minutes.\\
    \textbf{[2] rev.2, 2019:} held at 2.5 bar for the whole pressing stage.
  \end{tcolorbox}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.9em}
  Retrieval did its job: both relevant chunks came back. Generation did its job: the answer
  is grounded in a retrieved passage, with a citation. \textbf{The corpus is the thing that
  is broken.}

  \vspace{0.9em}
  \trapbox{No metric in this lecture catches this, because every metric asks ``is the answer
  supported by the context?'' and the answer is \textbf{yes}. Only \textbf{metadata} fixes
  it: index the revision date, filter to current, and prefer the newest at retrieval time.}
\end{frame}

% ============================================================ SECTION 4
\section{Measuring retrieval}
\sectiontransition{Measuring retrieval}{Three numbers, each answering a different question.
All three are computed by hand on this slide.}

\begin{frame}{The cure, actually applied}
  \vspace{0.4em}
  We diagnosed the opening failure and prescribed a fix. Here it is running, on the same
  question, with one line changed:

  \vspace{0.5em}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.47\textwidth}
      {\footnotesize\textbf{\color{armred}Before} - no filter}
      \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armred!8, colframe=armred, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt,
                        left=3pt, right=3pt, top=3pt, bottom=3pt]
        \ttfamily\scriptsize
        \lbrack1\rbrack\ rev 2, \textbf{2019}\\
        \lbrack2\rbrack\ rev 4, 2024\\
        \lbrack3\rbrack\ PRS-400 log
      \end{tcolorbox}
      {\footnotesize The superseded revision is \textbf{first}. The model quotes it, cites it
      correctly, and is wrong.}
    \column{0.47\textwidth}
      {\footnotesize\textbf{\color{paramgreen}After} - \texttt{current == True}}
      \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=paramgreen!8, colframe=paramgreen, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt,
                        left=3pt, right=3pt, top=3pt, bottom=3pt]
        \ttfamily\scriptsize
        \lbrack1\rbrack\ rev 4, \textbf{2024}\\
        \lbrack2\rbrack\ PRS-400 log\\
        \lbrack3\rbrack\ pasteurisation
      \end{tcolorbox}
      {\footnotesize The 2019 revision never reaches the prompt. The answer is now the
      current procedure.}
  \end{columns}

  \vspace{0.7em}
  \takebox{Nothing about the model, the embedder or the metrics changed. \textbf{One
  metadata field and one filter} fixed a failure that every retrieval number called perfect.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What you need before any number exists}
  \vspace{0.7em}
  Every metric here needs the same thing: a list of questions where somebody has written
  down \textbf{which chunk is the right one}.

  \vspace{0.9em}
  \begin{center}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{ll}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Question} & \textbf{Correct chunk} \\
    \midrule
    ``How small do we chop it up?''              & curd cutting \\
    ``How is the raw milk made safe\ldots?''      & pasteurisation \\
    ``How hard does the machine squeeze the cheese?'' & press, 2.5 bar \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{1.0em}
  Run the retriever on each question, find the \textbf{position} of the correct chunk in
  the results, and every metric below is a function of those positions.

  \vspace{0.8em}
  \keybox{Where this list comes from when you have no users yet is a whole section of this
  lecture. For now, assume somebody wrote it.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{recall@k: did it get into the prompt at all?}
  \vspace{0.6em}
  \begin{center}
    $\displaystyle \text{recall@}k \;=\;
    \frac{\text{questions whose correct chunk is in the top } k}{\text{all questions}}$
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.8em}
  By hand, on the three questions above, with the positions our retriever actually
  produced:

  \vspace{0.5em}
  \begin{center}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{lccc}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Question} & \textbf{Position} & \textbf{in top 1?} & \textbf{in top 5?} \\
    \midrule
    chop it up          & 1  & \color{paramgreen}yes & \color{paramgreen}yes \\
    raw milk made safe  & 2  & \color{armred}no      & \color{paramgreen}yes \\
    squeeze the cheese  & 10 & \color{armred}no      & \color{armred}no \\
    \midrule
    & & \textbf{1/3 = 0.33} & \textbf{2/3 = 0.67} \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.7em}
  \keybox{This is \textbf{the} metric for RAG. The generator reads all $k$ chunks, so it
  does not care whether the answer was 1st or 5th - only whether it was there.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{recall@k over the whole set}
  \vspace{-0.2em}
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.575\linewidth]{fig/l43_06_recall_at_k.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vspace{-0.5em}
  {\small Measured. Ignore the flat orange line for now - it comes back in two sections.}

  \vspace{0.2em}
  \watchbox{recall@$k$ only ever goes up with $k$. That is not a free win: bigger $k$ means
  more tokens, more cost, and more places for the answer to hide.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Mean Reciprocal Rank: how high did it land?}
  \vspace{0.4em}
  \begin{center}
    $\displaystyle \text{MRR} \;=\; \frac{1}{|Q|}\sum_{q \in Q} \frac{1}{\text{rank}_q}$
  \end{center}
  \vspace{0.1em}
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.66\linewidth]{fig/l43_07_mrr.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vspace{-0.5em}
  {\small \textbf{Mean Reciprocal Rank (MRR)} punishes depth harshly: rank 1 is worth ten
  times rank 10, and rank 10 and rank 50 are almost the same number. It only looks at the
  \emph{first} correct chunk.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{When one hit is not the whole story}
  \vspace{0.7em}
  recall@$k$ and MRR both treat relevance as \textbf{yes or no}. Real questions are not
  like that:

  \vspace{0.8em}
  \begin{center}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{lc}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Chunk} & \textbf{How useful} \\
    \midrule
    Press manual rev.4, 2024 - the current, complete procedure & \textbf{3} \\
    ``The Lori press operates at 2.5 bar\ldots'' - part of the answer & \textbf{2} \\
    Press manual rev.2, 2019 - right topic, superseded & \textbf{1} \\
    Brining, PRS-380 - unrelated & \textbf{0} \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.4em}
  {\footnotesize\color{gray}These grades are a \textbf{judgement call}, not a formula.
  Someone else could defend giving the superseded revision a 0. Everything below is exact
  arithmetic on a subjective input, which is the honest description of \emph{every} graded
  relevance metric.}

  \vspace{1.0em}
  \keybox{We want a metric that (a) uses these \textbf{grades}, and (b) rewards putting the
  3 above the 1. That is \textbf{normalized Discounted Cumulative Gain (nDCG)}.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{nDCG, computed}
  \vspace{0.2em}
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.88\linewidth]{fig/l43_08_ndcg.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vspace{-0.5em}
  {\small \textbf{Discounted Cumulative Gain (DCG)} adds up the grades, each divided by
  $\log_2(\text{rank}+1)$. \textbf{Ideal DCG (IDCG)} is the same sum for the best possible
  ordering, here $3, 2, 1, 0, 0$. Dividing one by the other puts the score on $[0,1]$ so
  queries with different numbers of relevant chunks are comparable.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Reading nDCG honestly}
  \vspace{0.6em}
  Our retriever put a superseded 2019 document above a chunk that was more useful, and
  still scored \textbf{0.97}.

  \vspace{0.9em}
  \begin{center}
    $\displaystyle \text{nDCG@5} = \frac{4.631}{4.762} = 0.973$
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.9em}
  Two things are true at once:

  \vspace{0.5em}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Given the grades, 0.97 is \textbf{correct}. Swapping ranks 2 and 3 is a small
      mistake and the metric says so.
    \item The consequence of that small mistake was a \textbf{wrong answer}, because the
      generator read the 2019 sentence and believed it.
  \end{itemize}

  \vspace{0.9em}
  \trapbox{A retrieval metric scores the \emph{ordering}. It has no idea what the text
  inside the chunks says, so it cannot know that rank 2 and rank 1 disagree with each
  other.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Which one to actually report}
  \vspace{0.8em}
  \begin{center}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{p{0.20\textwidth}p{0.34\textwidth}p{0.34\textwidth}}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Metric} & \textbf{Answers} & \textbf{Use it when} \\
    \midrule
    recall@$k$ & Did the answer make it into the prompt? & Always. This is the one that
    caps your whole system. \\[0.4em]
    MRR & How near the top was the first good chunk? & You show a ranked list to a person,
    or $k$ is very small. \\[0.4em]
    nDCG & Was the whole ordering sensible, with grades? & You have graded relevance and
    a reranker to compare. \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{1.0em}
  \takebox{Report \textbf{recall@$k$ at the $k$ you actually use in production}. A recall@100
  of 0.99 is meaningless if you paste 5 chunks into the prompt.}
\end{frame}

% ============================================================ SECTION 5
\section{Measuring generation}
\sectiontransition{Measuring generation}{Now the harder half: scoring an answer when nobody
wrote down what the right answer was.}

\begin{frame}{Three questions retrieval cannot answer}
  \vspace{0.9em}
  \begin{center}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.30\textwidth}
      \begin{center}
      \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armblue!8, colframe=armblue, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
        \textbf{Faithfulness}\\[0.4em]
        {\small Is the answer supported by the context we gave it?}
      \end{tcolorbox}
      \end{center}
    \column{0.30\textwidth}
      \begin{center}
      \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armblue!8, colframe=armblue, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
        \textbf{Answer relevance}\\[0.4em]
        {\small Does the answer address the question that was asked?}
      \end{tcolorbox}
      \end{center}
    \column{0.30\textwidth}
      \begin{center}
      \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armblue!8, colframe=armblue, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
        \textbf{Context precision}\\[0.4em]
        {\small Was the context worth the tokens it cost?}
      \end{tcolorbox}
      \end{center}
  \end{columns}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{1.2em}
  \keybox{These three come from \textbf{Ragas} (Retrieval Augmented Generation Assessment),
  2023. Their point is that all three can be computed \textbf{without a reference answer} -
  which matters, because you do not have one.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Faithfulness: break the answer into claims}
  \vspace{0.1em}
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.84\linewidth]{fig/l43_09_faithfulness.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vspace{-0.6em}
  \begin{center}
    $\displaystyle F \;=\; \frac{|V|}{|S|}
    \quad\text{= supported statements / all statements}$
  \end{center}
  \vspace{0.2em}
  {\small Split the answer into standalone statements, then check each one against the
  context on its own.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What faithfulness measured, and what it missed}
  \vspace{0.7em}
  It caught the over-generalisation: nothing in the context says the setting applies to
  every press. Good.

  \vspace{0.9em}
  It marked \textit{``the pressure stays at 2.5 bar throughout the pressing stage''} as
  \textbf{supported} - and it is, by the 2019 revision that was sitting in the context.

  \vspace{1.0em}
  \trapbox{\textbf{Faithfulness checks each claim against the context. It never checks the
  context against itself.} A perfectly faithful answer to an inconsistent corpus is still a
  wrong answer.}

  \vspace{0.9em}
  \begin{center}
  {\small\color{gray}This is not a flaw in the metric. It is the metric doing exactly what
  it says, and you assuming it said more.}
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Answer relevance: run the question backwards}
  \vspace{0.7em}
  You have no reference answer, so you cannot compare to one. The trick:

  \vspace{0.8em}
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item[\textbf{1.}] Hide the question. Show a model only the \textbf{answer}, and ask it
      to write the question this answers.
    \item[\textbf{2.}] Do that a few times, getting several reconstructed questions.
    \item[\textbf{3.}] Measure how close those land to the \textbf{real} question, with the
      embedding cosine from the retrieval lecture.
  \end{enumerate}

  \vspace{1.0em}
  \keybox{If the answer really addressed the question, you can recover the question from
  it. A vague or padded answer could have been written for many questions, so the
  reconstructions drift.}

  \vspace{0.7em}
  {\small Note what this does \emph{not} check: nothing about truth. An answer can be
  confidently wrong and perfectly relevant.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Answer relevance, measured}
  \vspace{-0.3em}
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.60\linewidth]{fig/l43_10_answer_relevance.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vspace{-0.5em}
  {\small Real cosines. A precise answer scores \textbf{0.948}; ``pressing is important and
  the settings depend on the equipment'' scores \textbf{0.790}.}

  \vspace{0.2em}
  \watchbox{The vague answer still scores 0.790, and two unrelated chunks scored 0.767
  earlier. \textbf{The scale does not start at zero.} Only the comparison between two
  systems on the same data means anything.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Was the context worth its tokens?}
  \vspace{0.5em}
  \textbf{Context relevance}, as originally defined: of the sentences you pasted, how many
  were needed?

  \vspace{0.5em}
  \begin{center}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{lc}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Sentence in the cold-open context} & \textbf{Needed?} \\
    \midrule
    rev.4: starts at 2.5 bar, raised to 3.2 bar & \color{paramgreen}yes \\
    rev.2: held at 2.5 bar throughout            & no \\
    ``The Lori press operates at 2.5 bar\ldots''  & \color{paramgreen}yes \\
    ``Cheese brining follows the pressing stage.'' & no \\
    ``The brine tank is kept at 12 degrees\ldots'' & no \\
    ``The PRS-380 is rated for harder cheeses\ldots'' & no \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.6em}
  \begin{center}
    $\displaystyle \text{context relevance} \;=\; \frac{2}{6} \;=\; 0.33$
  \end{center}
  \vspace{0.3em}
  {\small Two thirds of what you paid for did nothing.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Context precision: reward putting them first}
  \vspace{0.5em}
  The version in current tooling works on \textbf{chunks} and cares about \textbf{order}:

  \vspace{0.4em}
  \begin{center}
    $\displaystyle \text{context precision@}K \;=\;
    \frac{\sum_{k=1}^{K} \text{precision@}k \cdot v_k}{\text{number of relevant chunks in the top }K}$
  \end{center}
  \vspace{0.2em}
  {\small where $v_k = 1$ if the chunk at position $k$ is relevant, and precision@$k$ is the
  fraction of the first $k$ chunks that are relevant.}

  \vspace{0.6em}
  Two retrievals, same two relevant chunks out of five:

  \vspace{0.3em}
  \begin{center}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{lll}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Relevant at} & \textbf{Terms} & \textbf{Score} \\
    \midrule
    ranks 1, 2 & $\tfrac{1}{1}=1.00$, \; $\tfrac{2}{2}=1.00$ & $(1.00+1.00)/2 = \mathbf{1.00}$ \\
    ranks 2, 4 & $\tfrac{1}{2}=0.50$, \; $\tfrac{2}{4}=0.50$ & $(0.50+0.50)/2 = \mathbf{0.50}$ \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.5em}
  \takebox{Same chunks retrieved, half the score, because the junk was in front. This is the
  metric that tells you a \textbf{reranker} would help.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Every one of these needs a judge}
  \vspace{0.7em}
  Look at what the three metrics actually required:

  \vspace{0.7em}
  \begin{center}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{ll}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Step} & \textbf{Who does it} \\
    \midrule
    Split an answer into standalone statements & a model \\
    Decide whether a statement follows from the context & a model \\
    Write the question that an answer answers & a model \\
    Decide which sentences were needed & a model \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{1.0em}
  \keybox{This is \textbf{LLM-as-judge}, and it is not a shortcut anyone took out of
  laziness. There is no formula that decides whether a sentence follows from a paragraph.
  A human can do it; a human cannot do it 10{,}000 times a night, on every commit.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{And every one of these judges is suspect}
  \vspace{0.2em}
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.62\linewidth]{fig/l43_11_judge_agreement.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vspace{-0.4em}
  {\small Reported by the Ragas authors on 50 Wikipedia questions, against two human
  annotators. The purpose-built pipeline agrees 0.95 of the time on faithfulness. Simply
  asking a model ``which answer is better'' agrees \textbf{0.40} of the time - worse than
  flipping a coin.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{How to use a judge without lying to yourself}
  \vspace{0.7em}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Decompose the task.} ``Is this statement supported, yes or no'' is a
      question a model answers well. ``Score this answer out of 10'' is not.
    \item \textbf{Calibrate once against humans.} Label 50 examples yourself. If the judge
      disagrees with you 30\% of the time, its scores are decoration.
    \item \textbf{Use it for comparison, never as an absolute.} ``0.81'' means nothing.
      ``0.81 versus 0.74 for last week's version, same judge, same questions'' means
      something.
    \item \textbf{Freeze the judge.} Change the judge model or its prompt and every previous
      number becomes uncomparable. Version it like code.
    \item \textbf{Watch the known biases.} Judges prefer longer answers, prefer whichever
      candidate is shown first, and prefer text written by themselves.
  \end{itemize}

  \vspace{0.7em}
  \trapbox{Never let the judge be the same model, with the same prompt, as the generator.
  You are asking it to mark its own homework.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Which generation number to reach for}
  \vspace{0.3em}
  {\small Six numbers is too many to compute every time you touch something. Reach for them
  in this order:}

  \vspace{0.4em}
  \begin{center}
  \footnotesize
  \begin{tabular}{p{0.23\textwidth}p{0.24\textwidth}p{0.38\textwidth}}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Symptom} & \textbf{Measure first} & \textbf{Because} \\
    \midrule
    Plausible but wrong &
    \textbf{recall@k} &
    if the chunk never arrived, nothing after it matters \\[0.25em]
    Right chunk there, answer still wrong &
    \textbf{faithfulness} &
    separates ``ignored the context'' from ``context was bad'' \\[0.25em]
    Faithful, still wrong &
    \textbf{look at the corpus} &
    no metric catches a corpus that contradicts itself \\[0.25em]
    Answer drifts off the question &
    \textbf{answer relevance} &
    catches waffle that is technically supported \\[0.25em]
    Costs too much, quality fine &
    \textbf{context precision} &
    how much of the prompt earned its tokens \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.25em}
  \takebox{\scriptsize Always upstream first. \textbf{recall@k is the cheapest and most
  upstream}, so it is almost always the right first measurement - and the one most often
  skipped in favour of staring at the generated text.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The cold open, scored}
  \vspace{0.7em}
  \begin{center}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{lcl}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Metric} & \textbf{Score} & \textbf{What it was looking at} \\
    \midrule
    recall@1              & 1.00 & the right chunk came back first \\
    MRR                   & 1.00 & it came back first \\
    nDCG@5                & 0.97 & the ordering was nearly ideal \\
    faithfulness          & 0.75 & 3 of 4 claims supported by the context \\
    answer relevance      & high & it is clearly an answer about press pressure \\
    context relevance     & 0.33 & most of the context was wasted \\
    \midrule
    \textbf{correct?}     & \color{armred}\textbf{no} & \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.9em}
  \takebox{The only number that looked bad was context relevance, and it was bad for an
  unrelated reason. \textbf{Metrics are necessary and not sufficient.} Read some of your
  system's answers, by hand, every week. There is no substitute and there never has been.}
\end{frame}

% ============================================================ SECTION 6
\section{Building an eval set from nothing}
\sectiontransition{Building an eval set from nothing}{You have a corpus, no users, and no
labelled questions. Start anyway.}

\begin{frame}{The obvious idea, and it is a good one}
  \vspace{0.7em}
  Take a chunk. Ask a model to write a question that this chunk answers. Now you have a
  labelled pair, because \textbf{you know which chunk it came from}.

  \vspace{0.9em}
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=lightbg, colframe=gray, boxrule=0.5pt, arc=2pt, width=0.9\textwidth]
    \small
    \textbf{Chunk:} ``Ripening cellars are held at 10 degrees and 85 percent humidity.''\\[0.3em]
    $\rightarrow$ \textbf{Question:} ``At what temperature and humidity are ripening cellars
    held?''\\[0.3em]
    $\rightarrow$ \textbf{Correct chunk:} this one, by construction.
  \end{tcolorbox}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.9em}
  Two hundred chunks, two hundred questions, one afternoon, no annotators.

  \vspace{0.7em}
  \keybox{This is genuinely the right first move. It gets you from ``no idea'' to
  ``measurable'' in a day. But it has one specific defect, and it is worth a slide.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The defect, measured}
  \vspace{0.2em}
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.82\linewidth]{fig/l43_12_eval_set_trap.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vspace{-0.4em}
  {\small Same eight facts, same corpus, same retriever. The generated questions reuse the
  chunk's own words, so both retrievers find them \textbf{every time}. Phrase the same eight
  questions the way a person would, and BM25 drops to \textbf{0.00} and embeddings to
  \textbf{0.38}.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Your eval set was measuring the wrong thing}
  \vspace{0.6em}
  A question copied from a chunk shares that chunk's vocabulary. Retrieval by shared words
  is then trivial. \textbf{This is nearly a tautology} - which is exactly why it is
  dangerous. It looks like a measurement.

  \vspace{0.9em}
  \trapbox{Your dashboard reports recall@1 = 1.00. Your users experience something much
  worse. Nothing in the pipeline is broken and nothing in the dashboard is lying.}

  \vspace{0.9em}
  \begin{center}
  {\small\color{gray}Honest caveat: our eight ``as asked'' questions were deliberately
  written to avoid the chunk's words, so 0.008 word overlap is an extreme. Real user
  questions land in between. The gap is smaller than this - it is never zero.}
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Fixing it}
  \vspace{0.8em}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Instruct the generator to paraphrase.} ``Ask as a factory worker who has
      never read this document would ask, using none of its technical terms.''
    \item \textbf{Generate several styles per chunk.} Terse, rambling, misspelled, in
      Armenian, with the part number, without it.
    \item \textbf{Add multi-chunk questions on purpose,} so single-shot retrieval cannot
      pass by accident.
    \item \textbf{Read a sample yourself and throw out the bad ones.} Twenty minutes of
      this is worth more than another thousand generated questions.
    \item \textbf{Replace them with real ones as soon as you have real ones.} Log user
      questions from day one. Fifty real questions beat five hundred synthetic ones.
    \end{itemize}

  \vspace{0.8em}
  \takebox{Synthetic questions are \textbf{scaffolding}, not a benchmark. They tell you
  whether you broke something between Tuesday and Wednesday. They do not tell you how good
  your system is.}
\end{frame}

% ============================================================ SECTION 7
\section{Beyond the naive pipeline}
\sectiontransition{Beyond the naive pipeline}{Everything so far assumed one question, one
search, one answer. Drop that assumption.}

\begin{frame}{The question is not always the query}
  \vspace{0.7em}
  Users do not type search queries. They type this:

  \vspace{0.8em}
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=lightbg, colframe=gray, boxrule=0.5pt, arc=2pt, width=0.9\textwidth]
    \small
    \textbf{User:} What pressure does the Lori press run at?\\
    \textbf{System:} 2.5 bar, raised to 3.2 bar after twenty minutes [1].\\
    \textbf{User:} \textit{And the other one?}
  \end{tcolorbox}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.9em}
  Embedding ``And the other one?'' retrieves nothing useful, because on its own it means
  nothing.

  \vspace{0.9em}
  \keybox{\textbf{Query rewriting}: before retrieving, use the conversation so far to write
  a standalone query - ``What pressure does the PRS-380 press run at?'' - and search with
  that. Cheap, mechanical, and the single highest-value addition to a chat-shaped RAG
  system.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Search with the answer you do not have}
  \vspace{0.6em}
  A question and its answer often share no vocabulary - the whole problem from lecture one.
  So do not search with the question.

  \vspace{0.8em}
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item[\textbf{1.}] Ask a model to \emph{invent} a plausible answer. It will be wrong.
      That is fine.
    \item[\textbf{2.}] Search with that fake answer instead of the question.
    \item[\textbf{3.}] A fake answer looks like a passage, so it lands near real passages.
  \end{enumerate}

  \vspace{0.9em}
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=lightbg, colframe=gray, boxrule=0.5pt, arc=2pt, width=0.92\textwidth]
    \small
    \textbf{Question:} ``How warm is the room where the cheese matures?''\\[0.3em]
    \textbf{Invented answer:} ``The maturing room is kept cool and damp. Temperature is held
    at around \textbf{11 degrees} with high relative humidity so the rind does not dry out.''
  \end{tcolorbox}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.5em}
  {\small The real chunk says \textbf{10} degrees. The invented answer is factually wrong.
  \textbf{Hypothetical Document Embeddings (HyDE)} does not care - it only needs the fake
  answer to be shaped like the real one.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{HyDE, measured on our corpus}
  \vspace{0.2em}
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.74\linewidth]{fig/l43_13_hyde.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vspace{-0.4em}
  {\small Real measurement. Embeddings: rank 4 $\rightarrow$ rank 1. BM25 is the striking
  one: the question shares no content word with the chunk, so it scores \textbf{exactly
  0.000} and lands last. The invented answer scores \textbf{3.857} and ranks first.}

  \vspace{0.2em}
  \watchbox{The price is an extra model call before every search: more latency, more cost,
  and a hallucinated passage in your pipeline that must never reach the user.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Some questions need two searches}
  \vspace{0.7em}
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=lightbg, colframe=gray, boxrule=0.5pt, arc=2pt, width=0.86\textwidth]
    \small ``What pressure does the press that replaced the PRS-220 run at?''
  \end{tcolorbox}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.7em}
  {\scriptsize\color{gray}Three presses here: \textbf{PRS-220} (recalled, 2019),
  \textbf{PRS-400} (its replacement), \textbf{PRS-380} (harder cheeses).}

  No single chunk answers this. You need \textbf{``PRS-400 replaces the older PRS-220''},
  and then, knowing the model number, the chunk about the press pressure. One search cannot
  use a fact it has not read yet.

  \vspace{0.8em}
  \begin{center}
  {\small On our 20-chunk corpus, one embedding search put the two needed chunks at ranks
  \textbf{1} and \textbf{8} - so a top-5 prompt is missing half the answer.}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.6em}
  \watchbox{\small Honest caveat: BM25 happened to get both into its top 5 here, because the
  question contains the word ``pressure''. With 20 chunks that is luck, not evidence. The
  structural point stands: the second hop depends on a fact the first hop supplies.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Letting the system decide how many searches}
  \vspace{0.2em}
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.80\linewidth]{fig/l43_14_agentic_loop.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vspace{-0.4em}
  {\small \textbf{Agentic retrieval}: the model writes its own search query, reads the
  result, and decides whether it needs another one. Multi-hop stops being a special case.}

  \vspace{0.2em}
  \watchbox{You trade a fixed cost for an unbounded one. Cap the number of loops, log every
  query it writes, and expect a question that never terminates.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{When no chunk can hold the answer}
  \vspace{0.7em}
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=lightbg, colframe=gray, boxrule=0.5pt, arc=2pt, width=0.88\textwidth]
    \small ``Which suppliers are involved in the incidents that led to a recall?''
  \end{tcolorbox}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.5em}
  The answer is spread across forty documents and exists only as a \textbf{pattern between}
  them. Top-$k$ retrieval returns five paragraphs, and five paragraphs cannot contain a
  pattern over forty.

  \vspace{0.5em}
  \keybox{\textbf{Graph RAG}: while indexing, extract entities and relationships from each
  chunk - supplier, press, incident, recall - and build a graph. At query time, traverse the
  graph, then retrieve the text behind the relevant nodes.}

  \vspace{0.4em}
  \watchbox{The indexing cost is large: a model call per chunk, redone whenever the corpus
  changes. Use it when your questions are genuinely about connections.}
\end{frame}

% ============================================================ SECTION 8
\section{When not to use RAG}
\sectiontransition{When not to use RAG}{The most useful thing this chapter can teach you is
when to close the tab.}

\begin{frame}{Four questions to ask first}
  \vspace{0.3em}
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.88\linewidth]{fig/l43_15_when_not_rag.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vspace{-0.3em}
  {\small Retrieval is for \textbf{unstructured text that does not fit}. If your data is
  structured, small, or the problem is not about knowledge at all, you are building
  machinery you do not need.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The four alternatives, concretely}
  \vspace{0.7em}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{It is a database question.} ``How many wheels did line two produce in
      March?'' is a \texttt{SELECT}. Embedding your sales table is a way of getting a slower,
      less reliable \texttt{SUM}.
    \item \textbf{The whole corpus fits.} Ten policy documents, 40{,}000 tokens, a handful
      of questions a day: paste them and skip the pipeline. Retrieval is a response to
      \emph{scale}.
    \item \textbf{The problem is behaviour, not knowledge.} If the answers are factually
      right but the tone, format or language is wrong, retrieval cannot fix it. That is
      fine-tuning, or a better prompt.
    \item \textbf{The question is about everything.} Counting, averaging, ranking across
      the whole corpus. Next slide.
  \end{itemize}

  \vspace{0.8em}
  \takebox{Reach for RAG when the corpus is large, textual, changing, and the questions are
  answerable from a \textbf{few} passages. That is a narrow target, and it is a very common
  one.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why RAG cannot count}
  \vspace{0.2em}
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.64\linewidth]{fig/l43_16_aggregation.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vspace{-0.4em}
  {\small Illustrative archive: 400 incident reports, of which 37 mention the press. A
  top-$k$ retriever hands the model at most $k$ of them, so the model counts $k$ and reports
  it with total confidence.}

  \vspace{0.2em}
  \trapbox{The failure is \textbf{structural}, not a tuning problem. A better embedder, a
  reranker or a bigger model changes nothing. Count in a database, then hand the number to
  the model.}
\end{frame}

% ============================================================ RECAP
\begin{frame}{Recap}
  \vspace{0.3em}
  \small
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Assembly is plumbing that matters}: token budget, order, and dedup on
      metadata, not on a cosine whose floor here was 0.77.
    \item \textbf{The prompt is a contract}: restrict the source, demand citations, license
      a refusal. A citation is a place to look, not a proof.
    \item \textbf{recall@$k$}, at the $k$ you really use, is the metric that matters. MRR
      and nDCG describe the ordering, not the outcome.
    \item \textbf{Faithfulness, answer relevance, context precision} score generation with
      no reference answer - all three need a model as the judge, and \textbf{the judge is
      suspect}: purpose-built prompts agree with humans 0.95 of the time, ``which is
      better?'' agrees 0.40.
    \item \textbf{Synthetic eval sets flatter you}: recall@1 of 1.00 on copied questions,
      0.38 on the same facts in a user's words.
    \item \textbf{Sometimes the answer is not RAG}: SQL, a longer prompt, or a fine-tune.
  \end{itemize}

  \vspace{0.4em}
  \takebox{\small \textbf{Measure the generation, not just the search - and read the
  answers yourself.}}
\end{frame}
