\begin{frame}{A question about your own factory}
  \vspace{0.4em}
  The maintenance engineer asks a strong language model:

  \vspace{0.8em}
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=lightbg, colframe=gray, boxrule=0.5pt, arc=2pt, width=0.86\textwidth]
    \textit{``What pressure should the Lori cheese press run at?''}
  \end{tcolorbox}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.4em}
  It answers immediately, fluently, and in full sentences:

  \vspace{0.6em}
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armred!8, colframe=armred, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt, width=0.86\textwidth]
    \textit{``Typically around 4 to 6 bar, depending on the mould size.''}
  \end{tcolorbox}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.5em}
  \begin{center}
  {\large\color{armred}\textbf{It has never seen your factory.}}\\[0.2em]
  {\small The number is invented. Nothing in the answer tells you that.}
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The same question, with one paragraph attached}
  \vspace{0.3em}
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armblue!8, colframe=armblue, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt, width=0.9\textwidth]
    \small
    \textbf{Context:} ``The Lori press operates at 2.5 bar during the first pressing stage.
    After twenty minutes the pressure is raised to 3.2 bar for the second stage.''

    \vspace{0.5em}
    \textbf{Question:} What pressure should the Lori cheese press run at?
  \end{tcolorbox}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.3em}
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=paramgreen!8, colframe=paramgreen, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt, width=0.9\textwidth]
    \small
    \textit{``2.5 bar for the first stage, raised to 3.2 bar after twenty minutes.''}
    \hfill {\scriptsize [source: press manual, rev.~4]}
  \end{tcolorbox}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.6em}
  Same model. Same question. The only thing that changed is what was in front of it.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{That is the whole idea}
  \vspace{0.6em}
  \keybox{\textbf{Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)} means: before answering, go and
  fetch the relevant text, and put it in the prompt.}

  \vspace{1.0em}
  The idea takes one slide. The engineering takes the rest of the chapter, because of one
  question:

  \vspace{0.8em}
  \begin{center}
    {\large How do you find \textbf{that one paragraph}, out of \textbf{4{,}000 documents},}\\[0.3em]
    {\large in \textbf{200 milliseconds}, for a question phrased in words}\\[0.3em]
    {\large that may appear \textbf{nowhere in the document}?}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.9em}
  \begin{center}
  {\small\color{gray}Everything in this lecture is about that search. The generation half
  is the easy half.}
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

% ============================================================ SECTION 1
\section{Why retrieve at all}
\sectiontransition{Why retrieve at all}{The model already knows a lot. It just does not know
anything about you.}

\begin{frame}{What a trained model actually knows}
  \vspace{0.5em}
  Everything the model knows is frozen into its weights at training time. That gives you
  four problems at once:

  \vspace{0.8em}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.48\textwidth}
      \textbf{\color{armred}It is out of date.}\\
      {\small Training stopped on some date. Yesterday's price change is not in there.}

      \vspace{0.9em}
      \textbf{\color{armred}It never saw your data.}\\
      {\small Your manuals, contracts and incident reports were never public.}
    \column{0.48\textwidth}
      \textbf{\color{armred}It cannot cite.}\\
      {\small A fact recalled from weights has no source attached to it.}

      \vspace{0.9em}
      \textbf{\color{armred}It has no permissions.}\\
      {\small A model cannot show one answer to a manager and another to an intern.}
  \end{columns}

  \vspace{1.0em}
  \takebox{Retrieval fixes all four at once, because the answer arrives as \textbf{text you
  control}, not as a memory you hope is right.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The obvious objection}
  \vspace{0.5em}
  \begin{center}
  \textit{``Modern models have huge context windows. Why not paste all 4{,}000 documents?''}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{1.0em}
  Three reasons, in increasing order of how much they hurt:

  \vspace{0.6em}
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item[\textbf{1.}] \textbf{It does not fit.} A few thousand documents is tens of millions
      of tokens. No window is that large.
    \item[\textbf{2.}] \textbf{It is expensive.} You pay per token, on \emph{every single
      question}, for 4{,}000 documents of which one is relevant.
    \item[\textbf{3.}] \textbf{It gets worse answers.} Accuracy drops when the relevant
      sentence is buried in a long context, rather than sitting at the top of a short one.
  \end{enumerate}

  \vspace{1.0em}
  \keybox{Retrieval is not a workaround for small context windows. Giving the model
  \textbf{less, but the right less}, is what makes the answer good.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{RAG is two pipelines, not one}
  \vspace{0.2em}
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.80\linewidth]{fig/01_rag_loop.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vspace{0.2em}
  \keybox{The expensive work happens \textbf{once, offline}. When a user asks something,
  only the bottom row runs. Hold on to this split: it is the reason half the design
  decisions later in this lecture make sense.}
\end{frame}

% ============================================================ SECTION 2
\section{Chunking}
\sectiontransition{Chunking}{The least glamorous step in the pipeline, and the one that
quietly decides your ceiling.}

\begin{frame}{Why not just index whole documents?}
  \vspace{0.6em}
  Because retrieval returns whatever unit you indexed.

  \vspace{0.9em}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.47\textwidth}
      \textbf{Index whole manuals}\\[0.4em]
      {\small You get back a 90-page manual. You have moved the problem, not solved it:
      the model still has to find the paragraph.}
    \column{0.47\textwidth}
      \textbf{Index single sentences}\\[0.4em]
      {\small You get back ``It must then be raised.'' Raised from what? To what? The
      sentence lost its context.}
  \end{columns}

  \vspace{1.2em}
  \keybox{A \textbf{chunk} is the unit you retrieve. It should be small enough to be
  specific, and large enough to stand alone when it lands in the prompt.}

  \vspace{0.6em}
  {\small There is a second reason, which returns in Section 4: a chunk becomes
  \emph{one vector}, and one vector can only represent one meaning.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The five strategies, on the same text}
  \vspace{-0.3em}
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.86\linewidth]{fig/02_chunking_strategies.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vspace{-0.6em}
  {\small Boundaries computed by running each strategy, not drawn by hand. Note that the two
  \emph{structural} strategies, sentence and paragraph, both keep the answer intact, while
  the two \emph{arithmetic} ones destroy it.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Cut where the author already cut}
  \vspace{0.6em}
  Fixed-size chunking treats text as a character array. But your documents already contain
  the author's own decisions about where one idea ends:

  \vspace{0.8em}
  \begin{center}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{ll}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Boundary} & \textbf{What it usually means} \\
    \midrule
    sentence break     & one statement \\
    \textbf{paragraph break} & one idea, which is usually what you want \\
    heading            & one topic \\
    list item          & one step of a procedure \\
    table row          & one record, and never split one of these \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{1.0em}
  \keybox{\textbf{Paragraph chunking} is the cheapest good default. It costs one
  \texttt{split} call, needs no model, and respects a boundary a human already placed
  deliberately. Start here, and only get cleverer if it measurably fails.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The overlap trap}
  \vspace{0.4em}
  The standard fix for ``fixed-size chunking cut my sentence in half'' is to make chunks
  overlap. Look at what that actually measured:

  \vspace{0.7em}
  \begin{center}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{lcc}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Strategy} & \textbf{Chunks} & \textbf{Answer sentence} \\
    \midrule
    Fixed size, 60 characters              & 5 & \color{armred}\textbf{split} \\
    Fixed size, 60 characters + 15 overlap & 7 & \color{armred}\textbf{still split} \\
    By sentence                            & 4 & \color{paramgreen}\textbf{intact} \\
    By paragraph                           & 2 & \color{paramgreen}\textbf{intact} \\
    Recursive (merge up to 150)            & 2 & \color{paramgreen}\textbf{intact} \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.8em}
  \trapbox{\textbf{Overlap did not save it.} The answer sentence is 67 characters and the
  chunks are 60. No overlap puts a 67-character sentence inside a 60-character box. Overlap
  protects boundaries; it cannot protect a unit of meaning bigger than the chunk.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{So which one should you use?}
  \vspace{0.6em}
  \begin{center}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{lll}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Your documents} & \textbf{Start with} & \textbf{Why} \\
    \midrule
    Clean prose, real paragraphs & \textbf{paragraph} & the author already did the work \\
    Manuals, contracts, headings & \textbf{recursive}  & respects structure, caps the size \\
    Transcripts, chat logs       & \textbf{fixed + overlap} & there is no structure to respect \\
    Tables and records           & \textbf{one row per chunk} & a split row is a wrong answer \\
    Long undivided walls of text & \textbf{semantic}   & nothing else has a boundary to use \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.5em}
  {\footnotesize\color{gray}That last row needs a tool we do not have yet. We come back to it
  at the end of the lecture, once the machinery exists.}

  \vspace{0.5em}
  \takebox{There is no best chunking strategy, only a best strategy \textbf{for the shape of
  your documents}. Pick by looking at the documents, then measure, then change your mind.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The recall ceiling}
  \vspace{-0.2em}
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.48\linewidth]{fig/03_recall_ceiling.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vspace{-0.5em}
  \trapbox{If chunking destroyed the answer, \textbf{nothing downstream recovers it}. Not a
  better embedding model, not a reranker, not a bigger language model. When a RAG system is
  bad, people tune the model. The problem is usually here.

  \vspace{0.3em}
  {\footnotesize(\emph{Reranking}, on the axis above, means re-scoring a shortlist with a
  slower, more accurate model. It gets its own lecture next time.)}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Store more than the text}
  \vspace{0.6em}
  Each chunk should carry the facts you will need \emph{about} it:

  \vspace{0.8em}
  \begin{center}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{ll}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Field} & \textbf{Why you will want it} \\
    \midrule
    source document & so the answer can cite something \\
    section heading & so a chunk that says ``it'' still has a subject \\
    revision date   & so a 2019 procedure loses to the 2024 one \\
    access level    & so an intern does not retrieve the salary table \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{1.0em}
  \takebox{Two of these pay off later: \textbf{revision date} decides a contradiction in
  the next lecture's reranking section, and \textbf{access level} is how the permission
  problem from Section 1 actually gets solved.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The problem that sets up the rest of the lecture}
  \vspace{0.8em}
  Your chunk says:
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=lightbg, colframe=gray, boxrule=0.5pt, arc=2pt, width=0.82\textwidth]
    \small ``Ripening cellars are held at 10 degrees and 85 percent humidity.''
  \end{tcolorbox}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.3em}
  Your user asks:

  \end{tcolorbox}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.9em}
  \begin{center}
    {\large The chunk \textbf{is} the answer.}\\[0.3em]
    {\large They share \textbf{no content word at all}.}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.7em}
  \begin{center}
  {\small\color{gray}Remember this pair. We will run it through both retrieval methods.}
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

% ============================================================ SECTION 3
\section{Finding chunks by words}
\sectiontransition{Finding chunks by words}{The method from 1990s search engines that is
still, in 2026, half of every serious system.}

\begin{frame}{Start with the dumbest possible thing}
  \vspace{0.6em}
  Query: \texttt{lori press bar}. Count how many query words appear in each chunk.

  \vspace{0.9em}
  \begin{center}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{clc}
    \toprule
    & \textbf{Chunk} & \textbf{Query-word occurrences} \\
    \midrule
    D1 & ``The Lori press operates at 2.5 bar\ldots''            & 3 \\
    D2 & ``Press maintenance\ldots press\ldots press\ldots bar'' & 4 \\
    D3 & ``Cheese brining\ldots for Lori cheese.''               & 1 \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{1.0em}
  D2 wins. But D2 is a maintenance schedule that never mentions Lori cheese, and D1 is the
  chunk that actually answers the question.

  \vspace{0.7em}
  \watchbox{Counting fails for three separate reasons. We will fix them one at a time, and
  the result will be BM25.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Fix 1: rare words carry more information}
  \vspace{0.7em}
  ``The'' appears in nearly every chunk. ``Lori'' appears in a handful. Matching on
  ``Lori'' tells you far more.

  \vspace{0.9em}
  \begin{center}
    $\displaystyle \text{idf}(t) \;=\; \log\frac{N - n_t + 0.5}{n_t + 0.5}$
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.6em}
  {\small where $N$ is the number of chunks in the corpus and $n_t$ how many of them
  contain term $t$. This is the \textbf{inverse document frequency}: rare term, big weight.}

  \vspace{0.9em}
  For our factory archive ($N = 4{,}000$):

  \vspace{0.4em}
  \begin{center}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{lcc}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Term} & \textbf{Appears in} & \textbf{idf} \\
    \midrule
    \texttt{lori}  & 120 chunks & \textbf{3.47} \\
    \texttt{bar}   & 210 chunks & 2.89 \\
    \texttt{press} & 380 chunks & 2.25 \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{This already has a name: tf-idf}
  \vspace{0.6em}
  Multiply how often a term appears here by how rare it is overall, and sum over the query:

  \vspace{0.7em}
  \begin{center}
    $\displaystyle \text{tf-idf}(q, d) \;=\; \sum_{t \in q}
    \underbrace{tf_{t,d}}_{\substack{\text{term frequency:}\\ \text{how often, here}}}
    \;\cdot\;
    \underbrace{\text{idf}(t)}_{\substack{\text{inverse document frequency:}\\ \text{how rare, overall}}}$
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.9em}
  This is the classic weighting behind every search engine built before the 2000s, and it is
  still the first thing to try. It fixes the ``the'' problem completely.

  \vspace{0.9em}
  \keybox{\textbf{A term is worth something when it is frequent \emph{here} and rare
  \emph{everywhere else}.} That single sentence is the whole idea, and it survives into
  BM25 unchanged.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{But tf-idf still gets our example wrong}
  \vspace{0.6em}
  Query \texttt{lori press bar}, scored with tf-idf:

  \vspace{0.7em}
  \begin{center}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{clcc}
    \toprule
    & \textbf{Chunk} & \textbf{tf-idf} & \\
    \midrule
    D1 & the one that answers the question & 8.62 & \\
    D2 & the maintenance schedule          & \textbf{9.65} & \color{armred}\textbf{wins} \\
    D3 & brining                           & 3.47 & \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.9em}
  D2 wins because it says \texttt{press} \textbf{three times}, and tf-idf takes that
  literally: three times the term, three times the weight. It also never notices that D2 is
  more than twice as long as D1.

  \vspace{0.8em}
  \watchbox{So tf-idf fixed \emph{rarity} and nothing else. Two problems remain: repetition
  counts too much, and long chunks get a free advantage. \textbf{BM25 is tf-idf with exactly
  those two repairs.}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Fix 2: predict first}
  \vspace{1.2em}
  \begin{center}
    {\large A chunk mentions \texttt{pressure} \textbf{50 times}.}\\[0.7em]
    {\large Another mentions it \textbf{once}.}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{1.4em}
  \begin{center}
    {\Large\color{popblue}\textbf{Is the first one 50 times more relevant?}}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{1.6em}
  \begin{center}
  {\small\color{gray}Decide before the next slide.}
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Fix 2: term frequency saturates}
  \vspace{0.1em}
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.52\linewidth]{fig/04_bm25_saturation.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vspace{-0.3em}
  \begin{center}
    $\displaystyle \text{saturation}(tf) \;=\; \frac{tf}{k_1 + tf}$
  \end{center}
  \vspace{0.3em}
  \keybox{The 2nd mention is strong evidence. The 50th is almost none. $k_1$ sets how fast
  the curve flattens.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Fix 3: long chunks cheat}
  \vspace{0.1em}
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.48\linewidth]{fig/05_bm25_length_norm.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vspace{-0.4em}
  \begin{center}
    $\displaystyle B \;=\; (1 - b) + b\,\frac{dl}{avdl}$
  \end{center}
  \vspace{0.3em}
  {\small A long chunk contains more of every word, so it wins by being long rather than by
  being relevant. $B$ divides that advantage out: $dl$ is this chunk's length, $avdl$ the
  corpus average.}

  \vspace{0.3em}
  \watchbox{$b = 0$ turns the correction off, $b = 1$ applies it fully. Usually 0.5 to 0.8.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Putting the three fixes together}
  \vspace{0.9em}
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armblue!8, colframe=armblue, boxrule=0.8pt, arc=3pt, width=0.9\textwidth]
    \vspace{0.2em}
    \begin{center}
    $\displaystyle
      \text{BM25}(q, d) \;=\; \sum_{t \in q}
      \underbrace{\frac{tf_{t,d}}{k_1\left((1-b) + b\frac{dl}{avdl}\right) + tf_{t,d}}}_{\text{saturates, and discounts long chunks}}
      \;\cdot\;
      \underbrace{\text{idf}(t)}_{\text{rare terms count more}}
    $
    \end{center}
    \vspace{0.2em}
  \end{tcolorbox}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.9em}
  One sum over the query terms. Each term contributes ``how often it appears here, damped''
  times ``how rare it is overall''.

  \vspace{0.9em}
  {\small Usual settings: $k_1 \approx 1.5$ (range 1.2 to 2), $b \approx 0.75$ (range 0.5
  to 0.8). There is no theory that fixes them; they are tuned.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{By hand, on three real chunks}
  \vspace{0.1em}
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.62\linewidth]{fig/06_bm25_worked.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vspace{-0.3em}
  {\small\
  \textbf{D1} is short (12 tokens, $B = 0.70$) and hits all three terms, including the rare
  \texttt{lori}.\quad
  \textbf{D2} says \texttt{press} three times but is 30 tokens long ($B = 1.38$) and never
  says \texttt{lori}.\quad
  \textbf{D3} has only \texttt{lori}.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Three scorers, one right answer}
  \vspace{-0.2em}
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.80\linewidth]{fig/07_count_vs_bm25.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vspace{-0.5em}
  \takebox{Counting is wrong. \textbf{tf-idf is also wrong.} Only once repetition saturates
  and length is discounted does D1 come first - and then it wins by nearly double. No
  larger model was involved at any point.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{A formula can have decorative parts}
  \vspace{0.7em}
  You will often see BM25 written with an extra factor in the numerator:

  \vspace{0.6em}
  \begin{center}
    $\displaystyle \frac{tf \cdot (k_1 + 1)}{k_1 B + tf} \cdot \text{idf}(t)
    \qquad\text{instead of}\qquad
    \frac{tf}{k_1 B + tf} \cdot \text{idf}(t)$
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.9em}
  $(k_1 + 1)$ does not depend on the chunk or the term. It multiplies \emph{every} score by
  the same constant.

  \vspace{0.9em}
  \keybox{It changes every number and \textbf{cannot change the ranking}. It exists for
  historical compatibility. Worth internalising in general: when you meet an unfamiliar
  formula, ask which parts can actually reorder the output.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Where this method is unbeatable}
  \vspace{0.7em}
  BM25 matches \textbf{literal strings}. That sounds primitive until you notice what
  people actually type:

  \vspace{0.9em}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.46\textwidth}
      \small
      \texttt{PRS-400}\\
      \texttt{Article 47.2}\\
      \texttt{ISO 22000}\\
      \texttt{invoice \#88214}\\
      \texttt{Paniryan}
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
      {\small Part numbers, clause references, standards, invoice IDs, surnames. A model
      that ``understands meaning'' has no advantage here. There is no meaning. There is a
      string, and either you find it or you do not.}
  \end{columns}

  \vspace{1.1em}
  \keybox{BM25 gives you a \textbf{guarantee}: if the term is in the chunk, that chunk
  gets a non-zero score. Keep the word guarantee in mind for two slides' time.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{And where it stops working}
  \vspace{0.8em}
  Back to the pair from Section 2:

  \vspace{0.5em}
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=lightbg, colframe=gray, boxrule=0.5pt, arc=2pt, width=0.86\textwidth]
    \small
    \textbf{Chunk:} ``Ripening cellars are held at 10 degrees and 85 percent humidity.''\\
    \textbf{Query:} ``How warm is the room where the cheese matures?''
  \end{tcolorbox}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.8em}
  Shared content words: \textbf{none}. \texttt{warm} $\neq$ \texttt{degrees},
  \texttt{room} $\neq$ \texttt{cellars}, \texttt{matures} $\neq$ \texttt{ripening}.

  \vspace{0.9em}
  \trapbox{Every term contributes $tf = 0$, so the sum is \textbf{exactly zero}. Not a low
  score. No score. BM25 does not think this chunk is a weak match, it does not see it at all.

  \vspace{0.4em}
  The same literalism means \texttt{press}, \texttt{pressing} and \texttt{pressure} are three
  unrelated terms unless you bolt on a stemmer, and a stemmer for Armenian is its own
  project.}

  \vspace{0.4em}
  \begin{center}
  {\small\color{gray}This is called \textbf{vocabulary mismatch}, and it is why the next
  section exists.}
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

% ============================================================ SECTION 4
\section{Finding chunks by meaning}
\sectiontransition{Finding chunks by meaning}{If matching words fails, match what the words
mean.}

\begin{frame}{You already have most of this}
  \vspace{0.7em}
  In the RNN chapter you saw \textbf{word vectors}: train on enough text and words used in
  similar contexts end up close together, with cosine similarity as the measure of close.

  \vspace{1.0em}
  Two things change for retrieval:

  \vspace{0.6em}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.47\textwidth}
      \textbf{The unit is a chunk}\\[0.3em]
      {\small Not one word but a whole passage, compressed into a single vector.}
    \column{0.47\textwidth}
      \textbf{The goal is retrieval}\\[0.3em]
      {\small Not ``similar in general'' but specifically: \emph{does this chunk answer
      this question?}}
  \end{columns}

  \vspace{1.1em}
  \keybox{Those are not the same objective. A question and its answer are often not similar
  sentences at all. That difference is what the training has to fix.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Reminder: what cosine similarity measures}
  \vspace{0.4em}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.52\textwidth}
      \vspace{-0.3em}
      \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig/16_cosine_geometry.pdf}
    \column{0.44\textwidth}
      \vspace{0.6em}
      $\displaystyle \cos(\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b}) =
        \frac{\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b}}{\|\mathbf{a}\|\,\|\mathbf{b}\|}$

      \vspace{0.9em}
      {\small It measures the \textbf{angle} between two vectors, and deliberately ignores
      how long they are.

      \vspace{0.6em}
      Here the long document is exactly 3 times the short one. Same direction, triple the
      magnitude.}
  \end{columns}

  \vspace{0.5em}
  \keybox{Both score \textbf{0.992}. Straight-line distance would have called them very
  different: \textbf{0.14} versus \textbf{1.97}. For text that is the wrong answer, because
  a document should not become less relevant simply by being longer.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Which is why vectors get normalised}
  \vspace{0.3em}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.42\textwidth}
      \vspace{-0.6em}
      \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig/17_cosine_normalised.pdf}
    \column{0.54\textwidth}
      \vspace{1.2em}
      {\small Divide every vector by its own length and they all land on the unit circle.
      The short and long documents collapse onto the \textbf{same point}, because they only
      ever differed in magnitude.

      \vspace{0.8em}
      Once everything is normalised, the dot product \emph{is} the cosine. So similarity
      search becomes one matrix multiplication, which is why it is fast.}
  \end{columns}

  \vspace{0.2em}
  \watchbox{This is why libraries have a \texttt{normalize\_embeddings=True} flag, and why
  forgetting it quietly changes your rankings. Cosine of 1 means same direction, 0 means
  unrelated, and for text embeddings you will rarely see anything below about 0.6.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The corpus as points in space}
  \vspace{0.1em}
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.60\linewidth]{fig/08_embedding_space.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vspace{-0.6em}
  {\small Real vectors from a real multilingual model. Retrieval is now just: embed the
  question, take the nearest chunks.}

  \vspace{0.1em}
  {\footnotesize\color{gray}Careful: the vectors really have \textbf{384} dimensions, so this
  2-D shadow is an impression, not the retrieval signal. All \textbf{18} chunks are plotted,
  only a few labelled.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{How the vectors are trained to be useful}
  \vspace{0.6em}
  Collect pairs that belong together: a question and the passage that answers it.

  \vspace{0.9em}
  \begin{center}
    \textbf{Pull each pair together. Push everything else apart.}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.9em}
  \begin{center}
    $\displaystyle
      \mathcal{L} \;=\; -\log
      \frac{\exp\!\big(\text{sim}(q, d^{+})/\tau\big)}
           {\exp\!\big(\text{sim}(q, d^{+})/\tau\big) \;+\; \sum_{j}\exp\!\big(\text{sim}(q, d_j^{-})/\tau\big)}
    $
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.9em}
  {\small Read it as a classification problem: among all candidate passages, the correct
  one $d^{+}$ should get the highest similarity. $\tau$ is a \textbf{temperature}: small
  $\tau$ sharpens the competition and punishes near-misses harder.}

  \vspace{0.6em}
  \watchbox{This objective is called \textbf{InfoNCE}. The name is not important; the shape
  is: one positive on top, everything else pushed down.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The same loss, by hand}
  \vspace{0.4em}
  Query: \texttt{What pressure should the press run at for Lori cheese?}\\
  Three candidate passages, real similarities from a real model:

  \vspace{0.5em}
  \begin{center}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{llc}
    \toprule
    & \textbf{Passage} & \textbf{sim} \\
    \midrule
    $d^{+}$   & the press runs at 2.5 bar   & \textbf{0.834} \\
    $d_1^{-}$ & pasteurisation, 72 degrees  & 0.779 \\
    $d_2^{-}$ & ripening cellar, 10 degrees & 0.745 \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.6em}
  Divide each by $\tau$, exponentiate, normalise so they sum to 1, then the loss is
  $-\log$ of the probability given to $d^{+}$:

  \vspace{0.4em}
  \begin{center}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{lccc|c}
    \toprule
    & $p(d^{+})$ & $p(d_1^{-})$ & $p(d_2^{-})$ & \textbf{loss} \\
    \midrule
    $\tau = 1.0$  & 0.350 & 0.331 & 0.320 & 1.051 \\
    $\tau = 0.1$  & \textbf{0.504} & 0.290 & 0.207 & \textbf{0.686} \\
    $\tau = 0.05$ & \textbf{0.667} & 0.221 & 0.113 & \textbf{0.405} \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What that table just told you about temperature}
  \vspace{0.7em}
  At $\tau = 1$ the three probabilities are \textbf{0.350, 0.331, 0.320}. The model has
  effectively said ``any of these three, I do not mind''.

  \vspace{0.8em}
  And yet the similarities were 0.834 against 0.745 - the right answer really was ahead. The
  problem is that \textbf{all cosine similarities live in a narrow band}, so the gaps between
  them are small in absolute terms.

  \vspace{0.9em}
  \keybox{$\tau$ divides before the exponential, so a small $\tau$ \textbf{stretches small
  gaps into large ones}. It is the knob that turns ``0.834 versus 0.745'' into a confident
  decision, and it is why the loss drops from 1.05 to 0.41 without a single weight changing.}

  \vspace{0.6em}
  {\small\color{gray}Keep this in mind: the narrow-band problem comes back twice more, once
  for chunking and once for Armenian.}
\end{frame}

  \vspace{0.2em}

  \end{center}
  \vspace{-0.4em}
  {\small A real training run on toy data. Each line joins a query to its own passage.
  Before training the pairs are scattered; afterwards each pair has collapsed onto a single
  point and the gap between ``correct pair'' and ``any other pair'' has roughly doubled.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Where the negatives come from}
  \vspace{0.1em}
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.40\linewidth]{fig/10_in_batch_negatives.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vspace{-0.6em}
  {\small For query $i$, the answer is passage $i$. Every other passage in the batch is a
  free negative.}

  \vspace{0.1em}
  \keybox{64 pairs give 64 positives and 4{,}032 negatives, with no extra labelling. That is
  why contrastive training is cheap.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why this can possibly be fast enough}
  \vspace{0.2em}
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.62\linewidth]{fig/12_offline_vs_online.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vspace{-0.3em}
  The query and the chunk are encoded \textbf{separately}. So every chunk vector can be
  computed once, offline, and stored.

  \vspace{0.6em}
  \takebox{At query time you run the encoder \textbf{once}, on the question, then compare
  vectors. This is the offline/online split from Section 1, and it is the entire reason
  this approach is usable.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Back to the row we skipped}
  \vspace{0.7em}
  Remember the chunking table, and the one row we could not fill in:

  \vspace{0.6em}
  \begin{center}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{lll}
    \toprule
    Long undivided walls of text & \textbf{semantic} & nothing else has a boundary to use \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.9em}
  You now have everything that row needs. Embed each sentence, measure how similar each one
  is to the next, and \textbf{cut where the similarity drops} - because a drop means the
  topic changed.

  \vspace{0.9em}
  \keybox{This is the same offline pass we just described, used for a different purpose: not
  to search the chunks, but to \textbf{decide where the chunks should begin}.}
\end{frame}

  \vspace{0.2em}

\begin{frame}{Semantic chunking is not free}
  \vspace{0.7em}
  Look at why it over-split. Every similarity between neighbouring sentences sits between
  \textbf{0.819} and \textbf{0.876}:

  \vspace{0.6em}
  \begin{center}
    {\large 0.876 \quad \color{armred}0.819\color{black} \quad \color{armred}0.827\color{black}
     \quad \color{armred}0.827\color{black} \quad 0.833 \quad \color{armred}0.823\color{black}
     \quad 0.861}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.9em}
  The entire signal lives in a band \textbf{0.06 wide}. A threshold anywhere in there is
  making very fine distinctions on very little evidence.

  \vspace{0.9em}
  \watchbox{Semantic chunking is a real technique, not a bad one. But it costs an embedding
  pass over your whole corpus and hands you a \textbf{threshold to tune}. Paragraph chunking
  costs nothing and has no knobs. Reach for this when you have measured that structure is
  failing you, not by default.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Two shapes: encode apart, or encode together}

\begin{frame}{Practical: prefixes are not decoration}
  \vspace{0.7em}
  Many embedding models are trained \textbf{asymmetrically}: questions and passages are fed
  in with different prefixes.

  \vspace{0.8em}
  \begin{center}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{ll}
    \toprule
    \textbf{At index time} & \texttt{passage: Ripening cellars are held at 10 degrees\ldots} \\
    \textbf{At query time} & \texttt{query: How warm is the ripening room?} \\

  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \vspace{1.0em}
  \trapbox{Forget the prefixes and nothing crashes. You simply get quietly worse retrieval,

\begin{frame}{The honest comparison}
  \vspace{0.6em}
  It is tempting to say ``each method is better at different things''. Our own measurements
  do not support that cleanly: on exact identifiers, the embedding model did \emph{just as
  well} as BM25.

  \vspace{0.9em}
  The real difference is not who scores higher. It is what each one \textbf{guarantees}:

  \vspace{0.7em}
  \begin{center}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{p{0.42\textwidth}p{0.42\textwidth}}
    \toprule
    \textbf{BM25} & \textbf{Embeddings} \\
    \midrule
    Score is non-zero \emph{if and only if} a query term literally appears.
    &
    Score is never exactly zero, and never guaranteed either. \\[0.5em]

    &

  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

\section{When your language is not English}
\sectiontransition{When your language is not English}{Everything so far assumed a good
embedding model exists for your language.}

\begin{frame}{The assumption hiding in Section 4}
  \vspace{0.7em}
  Every claim about embeddings assumed the model represents your language well. For English
  that is safe. For Armenian it is not.

  \vspace{0.9em}
  Two compounding problems:

  \vspace{0.6em}
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item[\textbf{1.}] \textbf{Tokenization.} English-centric vocabularies fragment Armenian
      into many more pieces per word. You met this in the RNN chapter as the
      \emph{Armenian tax}.
    \item[\textbf{2.}] \textbf{Training data.} Multilingual models see far less Armenian than
      English, so Armenian occupies a smaller, blurrier region of the space.
  \end{enumerate}

  \vspace{1.0em}
  \watchbox{Blurry means everything looks moderately similar to everything. A generic
  multilingual model scored our correct Armenian passage \textbf{0.854} and a completely
  unrelated one \textbf{0.797}. A gap of 0.057 is not much to build a threshold on.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Two lessons worth more than the numbers}
  \vspace{0.9em}
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item[\textbf{1.}] \textbf{Bigger and more general is not automatically better for
      \emph{your} language.}\\[0.3em]
      {\small A 560M open model beat a large commercial one, because it was pointed at one
      language instead of a hundred.}

    \vspace{0.9em}
    \item[\textbf{2.}] \textbf{Targeting beats volume.}\\[0.3em]
      {\small Ten thousand noisy, automatically generated pairs were enough to match what
      far larger training sets buy. You do not need a million examples to make a real
      difference in a low-resource language.}
  \end{enumerate}

  \vspace{1.1em}
  \takebox{If you build Armenian retrieval, start from a model adapted to Armenian, and
  remember that producing your own domain pairs is a realistic project, not a research
  programme.}
\end{frame}

% ============================================================ RECAP
\begin{frame}{Recap}
  \vspace{0.3em}
  \small
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{RAG} is: retrieve the right text, then let the model read it. The
      retrieval is the hard part.
    \item It is \textbf{two pipelines}. Indexing runs once, offline; only the query path
      runs per question.
    \item \textbf{Chunking sets the ceiling} - nothing downstream recovers what it loses.
      Paragraph chunking is the cheapest good default.
    \item \textbf{tf-idf} = frequent here, rare elsewhere. Most of the way there, and it
      still ranked our example wrong.
    \item \textbf{BM25} = tf-idf plus two repairs: repetition saturates, long chunks get
      discounted. Unbeatable on exact strings, \emph{exactly zero} on rephrasing.
    \item \textbf{Cosine} measures angle, not length, so a long document is not penalised
      for being long.
    \item \textbf{Embeddings} are trained contrastively to pull questions and answers
      together, and degrade gracefully where BM25 stops dead.
    \item For \textbf{Armenian}, a targeted fine-tune on 10{,}000 pairs beat a commercial
      model.
  \end{itemize}

\end{frame}
