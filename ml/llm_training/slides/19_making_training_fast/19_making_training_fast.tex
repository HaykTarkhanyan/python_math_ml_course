\section{Know your hardware}

\begin{frame}[plain]
  \vfill
  \begin{center}
    {\color{popblue}\Large\bfseries Know your hardware}\\[4pt]
    \small Before you optimize anything, ask what the machine can actually do.
  \end{center}
  \vfill
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Three questions, always in this order}
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{What hardware do I have?} \quad(\texttt{nvidia-smi})
    \item \textbf{What does it offer?} \quad(the datasheet)
    \item \textbf{Am I actually getting it?} \quad(measure, do not assume)
  \end{enumerate}
  \vskip 8pt
  For this lecture: one \textbf{NVIDIA A100 80GB SXM}, rented by the hour.
  \vskip 6pt
  Every optimization below comes from taking question 3 seriously. The datasheet will
  promise us 8x, and we will not get it. \textbf{Understanding that gap is the lecture.}
  \keybox{\footnotesize Optimization without measurement is decoration. Every number in
    this deck was measured on the same box, one change at a time.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What the machine offers, by numeric format}
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.86\textwidth,height=0.52\textheight,keepaspectratio]{fig/a100_tflops.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vskip -4pt
  {\footnotesize The same silicon delivers wildly different throughput depending on how
  many bits you spend per number. TFLOPS = trillions of \textbf{floating-point
  operations} (\textbf{FLOPs}) per second. Note the shape: \textbf{fewer bits, more
  throughput}.}
\end{frame}

\vidslide{fig/borrowed/karpathy_a100_datasheet.jpg}%
  {The actual A100 datasheet. Karpathy, ``Let's reproduce GPT-2 (124M)'' (2024), 1:25:20}

\begin{frame}{What a tensor core actually is}
  Not a mysterious accelerator. A single \textbf{instruction} that multiplies two small
  matrices and adds a third, in one shot.
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.88\textwidth,height=0.30\textheight,keepaspectratio]{fig/borrowed/nvidia_tensor_core_mma.jpg}
    \\[3pt]
    {\tiny Source: NVIDIA developer blog, shown in Karpathy (2024), 1:28:45}
  \end{center}
  \vskip 2pt
  {\footnotesize Every large matrix multiply in your model gets chopped into thousands of
  these fixed-size operations. Those rows on the previous chart are simply \textbf{which
  precisions this one instruction will accept} - which is why lower precision buys
  throughput at all. (\texttt{HMMA} and \texttt{IMMA} are just the float and integer
  names for the instruction.)}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why matrix multiplication is the whole game}
  A Transformer is, computationally, \textbf{a stack of matrix multiplies} with cheap
  things sandwiched between them: residual adds, layer norms, GELU.
  \vskip 4pt
  And at the 124M scale, one matmul dwarfs all the others:
  \vskip 2pt
  \begin{center}
    \begin{tabular}{lrr}
      \toprule
      \textbf{Matrix multiply} & \textbf{Shape} & \textbf{Arithmetic} \\
      \midrule
      one MLP (feed-forward) projection & $768 \rightarrow 3072$ & 0.077 TFLOP \\
      \textbf{the classifier} & $768 \rightarrow 50257$ & \textbf{1.26 TFLOP} \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
  \end{center}
  \vskip 4pt
  {\footnotesize Projecting to the vocabulary is about \textbf{16x} the arithmetic of a
  single MLP projection. The vocabulary is large and the embedding is small, so that one
  layer dominates the whole network.}
  \keybox{\footnotesize Hold on to this table. In rung 5 we speed the model up by making
    \textbf{this exact matmul bigger}.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why INT8 sits out this lecture}
  INT8 is the fastest row on that chart, and we will not use it for \textbf{training}.
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.72\textwidth,height=0.45\textheight,keepaspectratio]{fig/int8_vs_float.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vskip -2pt
  {\footnotesize Integers space their levels \textbf{evenly}, so they spend most of their
  precision where almost no values live. Floats put fine steps near zero, which is where
  the values actually are.}
  \keybox{\footnotesize INT8 is a standard \textbf{inference} format. For training we
    stay with floating point and only argue about \emph{how many} bits.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The bottleneck is not the arithmetic}
  Here is the fact that explains four of our five speedups.
  \vskip 6pt
  Tensor cores can multiply far faster than memory can \textbf{deliver numbers to
  them}. Most of the time they are idle, waiting.
  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{center}
    {\color{armred}\bfseries If you hit 60\% hardware utilization, you are doing
    extremely well.}
  \end{center}
  \vskip 6pt
  Half the time, in a well-tuned job, your tensor cores are doing nothing because the
  data has not arrived.
  \keybox{\footnotesize This is why lower precision pays \textbf{twice}: the multiply
    gets faster, \emph{and} every number becomes smaller to move and store. The second
    effect is usually the bigger one.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Measure it properly, or you measure nothing}
  Your CPU does not run the network. It \textbf{queues work} for the GPU and moves on.
  \vskip 5pt
  So a naive timer stops the clock when the \emph{queueing} finishes, not when the
  \emph{computing} does.
  \codebox{t0 = time.time()\\
    \# ... forward, backward, optimizer step ...\\
    {\color{armred}torch.cuda.synchronize()} \ \ \# wait for the GPU to actually finish\\
    dt = time.time() - t0}
  \warnbox{\footnotesize Without \texttt{synchronize()} you will happily report a
    speedup that does not exist. Also: \textbf{ignore the first iteration} - it pays for
    memory allocation and other one-time setup, so it is never representative.}
  \vskip 2pt
  \begin{center}
    Baseline, batch 16 x 1024 tokens: \textbf{1000 ms/step}, about \textbf{16k tokens/s}.
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Batch size, and the metric that survives it}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Max out the batch that fits.} If you hit an out-of-memory error, halve
      it: 16, then 8, then 4. If you have headroom, go up.
    \item \textbf{Keep it rich in factors of two.} 8, 16, 32 and 48 all divide by 16.
      17 divides by nothing, and rung 5 explains why that hurts.
    \item \textbf{Report tokens per second, not milliseconds per step.} The moment the
      batch size changes, milliseconds stop being comparable between runs. Tokens per
      second is the number you actually care about.
  \end{itemize}
  \vskip 4pt
  \begin{center}
    $16 \times 1024 = 16{,}384$ tokens per step, at 1000 ms $\rightarrow$
    \textbf{16,384 tokens/s}.
  \end{center}
  \watchbox{\footnotesize Careful: batch size is a \textbf{throughput} knob \emph{and} an
    optimization knob. Changing it changes your training run, not just its speed.
    Decoupling the two is what gradient accumulation is for - the next deck.}
\end{frame}

\section{Rungs 1 and 2: spend fewer bits}

\begin{frame}[plain]
  \vfill
  \begin{center}
    {\color{popblue}\Large\bfseries Spend fewer bits}\\[4pt]
    \small The cheapest speedup available is to stop moving 32 bits when 16 will do.
  \end{center}
  \vfill
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Anatomy of a float: range and precision are separate budgets}
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.93\textwidth,height=0.50\textheight,keepaspectratio]{fig/float_formats.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vskip -2pt
  {\footnotesize The \textbf{exponent} decides how big and how small a number you can
  represent (the \emph{range}). The \textbf{mantissa} decides how finely you can tell
  nearby numbers apart (the \emph{precision}). Every format below is a different
  opinion about how to split the budget.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Rung 1: TensorFloat-32 (TF32)}
  TF32 crops the mantissa from 23 bits to 10 - but only \textbf{inside the tensor-core
  instruction}.
  \vskip 5pt
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Your inputs are FP32 (32-bit floating point). Your outputs are FP32.
    \item The internal accumulation is FP32.
    \item Nothing in your code changes dtype. \textbf{You cannot see it from Python.}
  \end{itemize}
  \codebox{torch.set\_float32\_matmul\_precision('high')}
  \keybox{\footnotesize This is why TF32 is such a sweet spot: one line, no code
    changes anywhere else, slightly more approximate results that you will not notice
    in training. It only exists on Ampere and newer.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Predict first: the datasheet promised 8x}
  FP32 gives 19.5 TFLOPS. TF32 gives 156 TFLOPS. That is exactly \textbf{8x}.
  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{center}
    \textbf{We flip the switch and re-time the step. What do we get?}
  \end{center}
  \pause
  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{center}
    {\color{armred}\Large 1000 ms $\rightarrow$ \textbf{333 ms}. Three times, not eight.}
  \end{center}
  \vskip 8pt
  We made the \emph{multiply} eight times faster and left the \emph{traffic} completely
  unchanged. Every number in and out of that instruction is still a full 32 bits moving
  across the memory bus.
  \keybox{\footnotesize First encounter with the memory wall: \textbf{we are not
    compute-bound, so making compute faster buys less than advertised.} 3x for one line
    is still an excellent trade.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Rung 2: BF16, and why FP16 needs a babysitter}
  Now we shrink the numbers themselves - 16 bits instead of 32.
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.80\textwidth,height=0.44\textheight,keepaspectratio]{fig/bf16_vs_fp16_range.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vskip -4pt
  {\footnotesize \textbf{BF16} (brain floating point) keeps FP32's 8 exponent bits, so
  the \emph{range} is identical and only precision is lost. \textbf{FP16} spends 5 bits
  on the exponent, so small gradients fall off the bottom of its range - which is the
  entire reason \textbf{gradient scalers} exist.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{A short history, because it explains the confusion}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{FP16 came first} (Volta). Everyone trained in it, and everyone had to
      wrap their training loop in gradient scaling to stop small gradients underflowing.
      Extra state, extra complexity, extra bugs.
    \item \textbf{BF16 arrived with Ampere} and deleted the problem. Same range as FP32,
      so \textbf{no gradient scaler at all}.
  \end{itemize}
  \vskip 6pt
  Most tutorials you will find online were written during the FP16 era and are full of
  scaler machinery you do not need.
  \watchbox{\footnotesize If your GPU supports BF16, use BF16 and ignore every gradient
    scaler tutorial. If it does not (a Colab T4, for instance), you are back in the FP16
    world and you \emph{do} need the scaler.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Mixed precision: what actually changes}
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.86\textwidth]{fig/autocast_what_changes.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \codebox{with torch.autocast(device\_type='cuda', dtype=torch.bfloat16):\\
    \ \ \ \ logits, loss = model(x, y)}
  \vskip -4pt
  {\footnotesize Unlike TF32, this \textbf{is} visible: your logits come back as BF16
  while your parameters stay FP32. That is what ``mixed'' means.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Two rules and an honest result}
  \warnbox{\footnotesize \textbf{Wrap the forward pass and the loss only.} Never the
    backward pass, never the optimizer step. This is the guidance from the PyTorch team,
    and it is easy to get wrong because wrapping more \emph{looks} more thorough.}
  \vskip 2pt
  \watchbox{\footnotesize \textbf{Do not call \texttt{.bfloat16()} on your tensors by
    hand.} Let autocast decide. Which operations it casts is not documented as clearly as
    you would like - matmul-like ops get cast, reduction-heavy ops such as layer norm and
    softmax mostly do not.}
  \vskip 4pt
  \begin{center}
    \textbf{333 ms $\rightarrow$ 300 ms.} Real, but small.
  \end{center}
  \vskip 4pt
  {\footnotesize We have now spent everything precision alone can buy. The bottleneck
  has moved somewhere else - and to find it we have to look at how data physically
  travels inside the GPU.}
\end{frame}

\section{Rung 3: stop making round trips}

\begin{frame}[plain]
  \vfill
  \begin{center}
    {\color{popblue}\Large\bfseries Stop making round trips}\\[4pt]
    \small Arithmetic is cheap. Moving bytes is expensive.
  \end{center}
  \vfill
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The memory wall, drawn to scale}
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.92\textwidth]{fig/memory_hierarchy.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vskip -4pt
  {\footnotesize On-chip \textbf{static random-access memory} (\textbf{SRAM}) is roughly
  ten times the bandwidth of \textbf{high-bandwidth memory} (\textbf{HBM}) and about four
  thousand times smaller. The CPU's \textbf{dynamic random-access memory}
  (\textbf{DRAM}) is enormous and hopelessly slow by comparison. Both axes are
  logarithmic.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Worked example: is that actually true?}
  One elementwise operation on the MLP hidden activations, in FP32. Everything here is
  arithmetic you can do on paper.
  \vskip 2pt
  \begin{center}
    \begin{tabular}{ll}
      \toprule
      Tensor shape & $16 \times 1024 \times 3072 = 50.3$M numbers \\
      Bytes moved  & read 201 MB $+$ write 201 MB $=$ \textbf{403 MB} \\
      Arithmetic   & 50.3M multiplies $=$ \textbf{50.3 MFLOP} \\
      \midrule
      Time to move it, at 2.0 TB/s       & \textbf{201 $\mu$s} \\
      Time to compute it, at 19.5 TFLOPS & \textbf{2.6 $\mu$s} \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
  \end{center}
  \vskip 5pt
  \begin{center}
    {\color{armred}\Large 78x more time moving the data than using it.}
  \end{center}
  \keybox{\footnotesize So ``memory-bound'' is not a figure of speech. For everything that
    is not a matrix multiply, the arithmetic is nearly free and the transfer is the whole
    cost - and the fix becomes obvious: \textbf{move it once, do everything, move it back}.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{One plot behind the entire lecture}
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.88\textwidth,height=0.56\textheight,keepaspectratio]{fig/roofline.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vskip -4pt
  {\footnotesize \textbf{Arithmetic intensity} = FLOPs performed per byte moved. Left of
  the bend you are memory-bound and the datasheet's peak is unreachable whatever precision
  you choose. Only matrix multiplies live on the right.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Reading the roofline, rung by rung}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{TF32 raised the ceiling} from 19.5 to 156 TFLOPS, and moved the bend
      from 9.8 to 78 FLOPs per byte. Our elementwise work sits at 0.125, far to the left
      of both. The ceiling was never the constraint - that is the 8x that became 3x.
    \item \textbf{BF16 moves us sideways as well as up.} Halving the bytes doubles the
      intensity, 0.125 to 0.25. That sideways step is why it helps at all.
    \item \textbf{Fusion is the move that changes intensity a lot.} Three round trips
      become one, so the same arithmetic is paid for with a third of the traffic.
    \item \textbf{FlashAttention is that same move}, applied to an operation the compiler
      is not able to restructure on its own.
  \end{itemize}
  \keybox{\footnotesize Four of our five rungs are attempts to move right on this plot or
    to stop caring about the ceiling. Only vocab padding is about something else entirely.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Where that memory physically sits}
  The GPU die is covered in \textbf{streaming multiprocessors} (SMs) - 108 active on an
  A100, out of 128 on the full chip. Each has tensor cores, ordinary arithmetic units,
  registers and L1 cache.
  \vskip 5pt
  The large memory (HBM) is a \emph{separate chip}. Every byte your kernel reads from it
  has to cross that gap.
  \vskip 5pt
  \begin{center}
    {\color{popblue}\bfseries On the chip: lightning fast, almost no room.\\
     Off the chip: all the room you want, and you wait.}
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\vidslide{fig/borrowed/karpathy_ga100_die.jpg}%
  {GA100 die: 128 SMs, L2 in the middle, HBM links on the sides. Karpathy (2024), 1:56:40}

\begin{frame}{One activation function, three pointless round trips}
  Take the \textbf{Gaussian Error Linear Unit} (\textbf{GELU}) and write it out by hand:
  \texttt{x**3}, then \texttt{* 0.044715}, then \texttt{+ x}. In eager PyTorch each of
  those is its own kernel.
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.90\textwidth,height=0.50\textheight,keepaspectratio]{fig/kernel_fusion.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vskip -6pt
  {\footnotesize Each kernel streams the whole tensor out of HBM, does one cheap
  elementwise operation, and writes the whole tensor back. The arithmetic is trivial.
  \textbf{The travel is the cost.}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Rung 3: \texttt{torch.compile}}
  \codebox{model = torch.compile(model)}
  \vskip 4pt
  Two separate wins, from one line:
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{The Python interpreter leaves the forward pass.} Eager mode walks your
      code operation by operation with no idea what comes next. The compiler sees the
      whole graph at once and emits it as a single object.
    \item \textbf{Kernel fusion.} Knowing the whole chain, it loads a chunk of the tensor
      into on-chip memory, performs \emph{every} elementwise operation there, and writes
      back \textbf{once}.
  \end{enumerate}
  \vskip 4pt
  \begin{center}
    {\color{paramgreen}\Large 300 ms $\rightarrow$ \textbf{130 ms}. A 2.3x speedup.}
  \end{center}
  \keybox{\footnotesize PyTorch's own summary: ``speedup mainly comes from reducing
    Python overhead and GPU read/writes''. Those are exactly the two wins above.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What it costs you}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Compilation time up front.} The first iteration is slow, sometimes
      dramatically. This is a second reason to throw the first step out of your timing.
    \item \textbf{Debugging gets harder.} The graph you are stepping through is no longer
      the Python you wrote.
  \end{itemize}
  \vskip 6pt
  Karpathy's position, and it is a reasonable default: \textbf{use it unless you are
  actively debugging.}
  \vskip 6pt
  {\footnotesize Note where this sits in the ranking. TF32 is still the biggest single
  rung (3.0x against compile's 2.3x), and the two precision rungs together beat it as
  well. But TF32 and BF16 bought their speed by \textbf{throwing away mantissa bits};
  \texttt{torch.compile} is the largest win that costs no accuracy at all. It only stopped
  us walking to HBM and back for no reason.}
\end{frame}

\section{Rungs 4 and 5: what the compiler cannot find}

\begin{frame}[plain]
  \vfill
  \begin{center}
    {\color{popblue}\Large\bfseries What the compiler cannot find}\\[4pt]
    \small Two more wins, both of which you have to know about yourself.
  \end{center}
  \vfill
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Rung 4: FlashAttention}
  FlashAttention (Dao et al., 2022) is \textbf{also} a kernel fusion. So why did
  \texttt{torch.compile} not find it?
  \vskip 6pt
  Because it is not a graph optimization. It requires an \textbf{algorithmic rewrite}:
  computing softmax incrementally so the $T \times T$ attention matrix is
  \textbf{never materialized} in HBM at all.
  \vskip 6pt
  That trick - the \textbf{online softmax} - was published by Milakov and Gimelshein at
  NVIDIA in \textbf{2018}, four years before FlashAttention used it. They even predicted
  it would help. Nobody connected it to attention until Stanford did.
  \nextbox{\footnotesize The tiling and the online-softmax recursion are derived properly
    in \textbf{deck 15 (FlashAttention)}. Today it is one rung on a ladder.}
\end{frame}

\vidslide{fig/borrowed/karpathy_flash_fig1.jpg}%
  {FlashAttention (Dao et al., 2022), Figure 1. Shown in Karpathy (2024), 2:01:10}

\begin{frame}{Predict first: it does more arithmetic}
  FlashAttention performs \textbf{more} floating-point operations than the standard
  implementation. Count them and it loses.
  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{center}
    \textbf{So is it slower?}
  \end{center}
  \pause
  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{center}
    {\color{paramgreen}\Large 130 ms $\rightarrow$ \textbf{96 ms}. About 27\% faster.}
  \end{center}
  \vskip 8pt
  It never writes the big attention matrix to slow memory, and that saving dwarfs the
  extra arithmetic.
  \keybox{\footnotesize The lesson to carry out of this room:
    \textbf{FLOPs are not the cost model. Memory access is.} A FLOP count can rank an
    algorithm as the worse one when it is in fact the faster one.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The change itself}
  Four lines of hand-written attention:
  \codebox{att = (q @ k.transpose(-2, -1)) * (1.0 / math.sqrt(k.size(-1)))\\
    att = att.masked\_fill(bias[:,:,:T,:T] == 0, float('-inf'))\\
    att = F.softmax(att, dim=-1)\\
    y = att @ v}
  become one:
  \codebox{y = F.scaled\_dot\_product\_attention(q, k, v, is\_causal=True)}
  \vskip 2pt
  \watchbox{\footnotesize You have to ask for it explicitly. PyTorch will not rewrite
    your four lines into this call, even though it recognizes the pattern well enough to
    provide the fused version.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Rung 5: nice numbers and ugly numbers}
  CUDA kernels chop work into \textbf{block tiles} that are powers of two - 16, 32, 64,
  128. When your shapes divide evenly, every tile is full. When they do not, a second
  clean-up kernel runs for the remainder, and it is far less efficient.
  \vskip 6pt
  So scan the model config for numbers that are not friendly:
  \vskip 4pt
  \begin{center}
    \begin{tabular}{lll}
      \toprule
      \textbf{Value} & \textbf{Verdict} & \textbf{Why} \\
      \midrule
      1024 \ (block size)   & nice  & $2^{10}$ \\
      768 \ (embedding dim) & nice  & $256 \times 3$ \\
      12 \ (layers, heads)  & fine  & divisible by 4 \\
      \textbf{50257} \ (vocab)   & {\color{armred}\textbf{ugly}} & $29 \times 1733$, odd, no factor of two \\
      \textbf{25} \ (heads, GPT-2 XL) & {\color{armred}\textbf{ugly}} & odd \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Predict first: make the model bigger to make it faster}
  We pad the vocabulary from \textbf{50257} up to \textbf{50304}, which is
  $2^7 \times 393$ and divides by 128.
  \vskip 4pt
  That is 47 extra tokens, a bigger embedding table, a bigger classifier, and
  \textbf{strictly more arithmetic}.
  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{center}
    \textbf{Faster or slower?}
  \end{center}
  \pause
  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{center}
    {\color{paramgreen}\Large 96 ms $\rightarrow$ \textbf{93 ms}. About 3\% faster.}
  \end{center}
  \vskip 6pt
  {\footnotesize That is \textbf{0.09\% more arithmetic} traded for \textbf{3\% less
  time}. And on PyTorch 2.3.1 or earlier the same change was worth roughly \textbf{30\%};
  the kernels have since got better at ragged shapes, which is itself worth knowing:
  \emph{these numbers rot}.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why padding the vocabulary is safe}
  A fair objection: we just invented 47 tokens that do not exist. Does that break
  anything?
  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{At the embedding table:} the tokenizer only ever emits ids up to
      50256, so the added rows are \textbf{never indexed}. They cost memory and nothing
      else.
    \item \textbf{At the classifier:} we now predict 47 extra logits. The optimizer
      drives them toward negative infinity, exactly as it already does for the thousands
      of real tokens that never appear in our data.
  \end{itemize}
  \vskip 6pt
  {\footnotesize For context, 50257 is itself an awkward artifact: 50,000 byte-pair
  merges, plus 256 byte tokens, plus one \texttt{<|endoftext|>}. Nobody chose it for
  the hardware.}
  \keybox{\footnotesize Functionally identical, measurably faster. This is the one
    optimization that feels like cheating and is not.}
\end{frame}

\section{The ladder, and what it means for you}

\begin{frame}[plain]
  \vfill
  \begin{center}
    {\color{popblue}\Large\bfseries The whole ladder}\\[4pt]
    \small Eleven times faster, and the model never changed.
  \end{center}
  \vfill
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Five rungs}
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.90\textwidth,height=0.52\textheight,keepaspectratio]{fig/speedup_ladder.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vskip -4pt
  {\footnotesize Notice where the money was. The two precision rungs together bought
  3.3x; \texttt{torch.compile} alone more than doubled that again. The last two rungs
  are small in percentage terms and are the ones that require you to actually understand
  the hardware.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Which rungs cost you accuracy?}
  \begin{center}
    \begin{tabular}{lll}
      \toprule
      \textbf{Rung} & \textbf{Result} & \textbf{What you trade} \\
      \midrule
      TF32              & approximate & 13 mantissa bits, invisibly \\
      BF16 autocast     & approximate & 16 mantissa bits on some ops \\
      \texttt{torch.compile} & same computation & compile time, debuggability \\
      FlashAttention    & same computation & nothing \\
      vocab padding     & same computation & a little memory \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
  \end{center}
  \vskip 8pt
  Only the first two are genuine trades, and both are trades the field has decided are
  worth making. You lose a little numerical precision and you can \textbf{train for
  longer with the time you saved}, which more than pays it back.
  \watchbox{\footnotesize ``Same computation'' means algebraically equivalent, not
    bit-identical - floating-point addition is not associative, so reordering work
    changes the last digits. Expect losses to match to about four decimal places, not
    exactly.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{How do you know you did not break it?}
  Every rung here is a performance change, and performance changes are exactly where
  silent bugs hide. The discipline that catches them is dull and non-negotiable:
  \vskip 4pt
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item Fix the seed. Record the loss at a \textbf{fixed iteration} before the change.
    \item Make \textbf{one} change.
    \item Re-run and compare that same loss.
  \end{enumerate}
  \vskip 4pt
  When FlashAttention went in, the loss at iteration 49 matched the previous run to about
  four decimal places - not bit-identical, because reordering floating-point work changes
  the last digits, but far closer than any genuine bug would leave it. When BF16 went in,
  it moved a little more, and that was expected.
  \warnbox{\footnotesize A speedup that also moves your loss is a \textbf{bug} until you
    can name the approximation responsible. ``It got faster'' is not a test.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Reality check: you are probably not on an A100}
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.78\textwidth]{fig/hardware_reality.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vskip -4pt
  {\footnotesize TF32 and BF16 both arrived with \textbf{Ampere}. On a Colab T4 (Turing)
  neither exists, so the precision rung becomes \textbf{FP16 plus a gradient scaler} -
  back to the era the tutorials were written in. \texttt{torch.compile} and power-of-two
  shapes still work everywhere.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What actually transfers}
  The specific numbers in this deck belong to one GPU in 2024. The method does not.
  \vskip 5pt
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Measure first, and synchronize before you measure.} Most reported
      speedups are timing bugs.
    \item \textbf{Find out what your hardware promises}, then find out what fraction you
      are getting. The gap is where the work is.
    \item \textbf{Assume you are memory-bound until proven otherwise.} It explained four
      of our five rungs.
    \item \textbf{FLOPs are not the cost model.} FlashAttention does more of them and
      wins.
    \item \textbf{Give the kernels powers of two.} Free, and occasionally worth 30\%.
  \end{enumerate}
  \keybox{\footnotesize All five changes together are about six lines of code. The hard
    part was never the typing - it was knowing which six.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Recap}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{1000 ms to 93 ms}, roughly \textbf{11x}, on an unchanged GPT-2 124M.
    \item \textbf{Precision} (TF32, then BF16) is the cheapest rung, and disappointed us
      relative to the datasheet because we are \textbf{memory-bound}.
    \item \textbf{\texttt{torch.compile}} was the biggest win that costs no accuracy
      (2.3x): it removes the Python interpreter from the forward pass and \textbf{fuses
      elementwise chains} so data stays on-chip.
    \item \textbf{FlashAttention} is the fusion a compiler cannot discover, because it
      needs an algorithmic rewrite (online softmax).
    \item \textbf{Nice numbers} matter, because CUDA tiles in powers of two.
  \end{itemize}
  \vfill
  \nextbox{\textbf{Next:} the other half of ``let's reproduce GPT-2'' - \textbf{gradient
    accumulation}, AdamW with gradient clipping, the \textbf{warmup plus cosine} schedule,
    and \textbf{distributed data parallel} across eight GPUs. For the algorithm behind
    rung 4 see \emph{deck 15 (FlashAttention)}; for these ideas at frontier scale, including
    \textbf{FP8} training, see \emph{deck 17 (DeepSeek-V3)}.}
\end{frame}
