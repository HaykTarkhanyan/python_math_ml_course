\begin{frame}{It answered out loud}
  \small
  You asked a question. About \textbf{200 milliseconds} later a voice started answering, with
  the right emphasis in the right places. You interrupted halfway through, and it stopped.
  \vskip 8pt
  It did not call a speech-synthesis service. The same network that chose the words produced the
  sound.
  \vskip 10pt
  \centering
  \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.9\linewidth}{\small\centering
    Last lecture got sound \emph{into} a language model. This one is the harder direction, and
    it needs an idea we have been avoiding since ch9.}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The contradiction}
  \small
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.48\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{popblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\small
      \textbf{What a language model does}\\[4pt]
      Picks the next item from a \textbf{finite vocabulary}, by a softmax over maybe 100,000
      choices. Discrete, one at a time.}}
    \column{0.52\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\small
      \textbf{What sound is}\\[4pt]
      A \textbf{continuous} pressure curve, arriving 16,000 numbers a second, where the exact
      values matter and there is no alphabet.}}
  \end{columns}
  \vskip 10pt
  This is the same collision as L34, where a continuous diffusion model met a discrete language
  model over images. The resolution is the same too, and it is audacious:
  \vskip 8pt
  \centering
  \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.88\linewidth}{\small\centering
    \textbf{Invent a vocabulary for sound.} Then talking is just next-token prediction.}}
\end{frame}

\section{A vocabulary for sound}

\begin{frame}[plain]
  \vfill
  \begin{center}
    {\Large\color{popblue}\textbf{A vocabulary for sound}}\\[0.9em]
    \begin{minipage}{0.82\textwidth}\centering\small
      What if a sound were made of words? The first three attempts all fail, and each one
      fails for a reason worth knowing.
    \end{minipage}
  \end{center}
  \vfill
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Attempt 1: just round the samples}
  \small
  Each sample is a number. Round it to one of 256 levels and you have a vocabulary of 256. This
  is literally what WaveNet did.
  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      \textbf{The vocabulary is fine.} 256 is tiny. With a logarithmic spacing that matches
      loudness perception, 8 bits per sample sounds acceptable.}}
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      \textbf{The sequence length is fatal.} You are back to 16,000 tokens per second - exactly
      the problem L35 opened with. Nothing was gained.}}
  \end{columns}
  \vskip 10pt
  \centering
  We do not need a smaller \emph{vocabulary}. We need fewer \emph{tokens}.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Attempt 2: quantize frames instead of samples}
  \small
  Push the audio through an encoder that reduces it to a few frames per second, then snap each
  frame's vector to the nearest entry of a learned codebook. This is \textbf{vector
  quantization}, exactly as in L34, and the audio becomes a grid of integers.
  \vskip 8pt
  \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.97\linewidth}{\small
    \textbf{And now the vocabulary is fatal.} To match a real codec's quality with a single
    codebook you would need roughly
    \[ 2048^{8} = 2^{88} \approx 3 \times 10^{26} \text{ entries.} \]
    That is not a large softmax. That is more entries than there are stars in the observable
    universe - by at least a factor of a hundred, on any published estimate.}}
  \vskip 8pt
  \centering
  Attempt 1 had too many tokens. Attempt 2 has too many \emph{words}. The fix is to stop
  choosing.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Attempt 3: residual vector quantization}
  \small
  \textbf{Residual Vector Quantization} (\textbf{RVQ}) is the idea that made audio tokenizable,
  and it is three lines long.
  \vskip 6pt
  \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.97\linewidth}{\small
    \begin{enumerate}\itemsep2pt
      \item Quantize the frame with codebook 1. Keep the index.
      \item Look at what is \textbf{left over} - the error you just made.
      \item Quantize \emph{that} with codebook 2. Repeat for 8 codebooks.
    \end{enumerate}}}
  \vskip 8pt
  Formally, with $r_0 = x$ and $e_k(\cdot)$ the nearest entry in codebook $k$:
  \[ r_k = r_{k-1} - e_k(r_{k-1}), \qquad \hat{x} = \sum_{k=1}^{8} e_k(r_{k-1}) \]
  \vskip 4pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      Eight codebooks of 2,048 give $8 \times 11 = 88$ bits per frame - the same expressive
      power as $2^{88}$ entries, from 16,384 vectors you can actually store.}}
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!12}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      The cost: a frame is now \textbf{8 tokens, not 1}. Section 7 is entirely about what to do
      about that.}}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The same idea, watched level by level}
  \vskip -2pt
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.66\textwidth]{rvq_mechanism.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vskip -6pt
  \footnotesize
  One real frame, quantized four times. \textbf{Left:} what the decoder would receive so far.
  \textbf{Right:} the error still outstanding, which is the next level's entire job.
  \vskip 2pt
  \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.97\linewidth}{\footnotesize
    Notice the residual is not just \emph{smaller} - it is \textbf{less structured}. Level 1
    takes the big obvious shape; by level 4 what remains looks close to noise. That is exactly
    why the returns diminish so fast on the next slide.}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What does each level actually buy?}
  \vskip -4pt
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.76\textwidth]{vq_residual_stages.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vskip -4pt
  \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!12}{\parbox{0.97\linewidth}{\footnotesize
    \textbf{Sharply front-loaded: level 1 buys about $\mathbf{2.5\times}$ what all seven
    remaining levels add together}, for one eighth of the bits. \emph{That ratio} is the
    finding; the dB values belong to this stand-in, not to a real codec. Same shape as L34's
    image codebooks - which is why level 1 is the one everybody fights over.}}
\end{frame}

  Encoder, residual quantizer, decoder, trained end to end.
  \vskip 4pt
  \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.97\linewidth}{\small
    \textbf{On raw waveforms - not on the mel spectrograms we spent six slides building.} A
    spectrogram cannot be played back (L35, the phase slide), so a system that must \emph{emit
    sound} cannot live there. Mel is for models that only listen.}}
  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{center}
    \footnotesize
    \begin{tabular}{llr}
      \toprule
      \textbf{Codec} & \textbf{Who, when} & \textbf{Note} \\
      \midrule
      SoundStream & Zeghidour et al., 2021 & introduced residual quantization here \\
      EnCodec & D\'efossez et al., 2022 & good enough for music, not just speech \\
      \rowcolor{armblue!12}
      Mimi & D\'efossez et al., 2024 & the one Moshi runs on \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \centering
  \footnotesize
  Mimi: 24 kHz in, \textbf{12.5 frames per second} out - a \textbf{1,920$\times$} cut in
  sequence length - with \textbf{8} codebooks of 2,048, which is \textbf{1.1 kbps}.
  \vskip 3pt
  (The released checkpoint ships 32 quantizers; Moshi uses 8 of them.)
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Two things that make the training work}
  \small
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.49\textwidth}
    \textbf{Rounding has no gradient}
    \vskip 3pt
    \footnotesize
    "Snap to the nearest entry" is a step function: its derivative is zero everywhere it is
    defined. The encoder would never learn.
    \vskip 4pt
    \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      \textbf{Straight-through estimator.} On the backward pass, pretend the quantizer was the
      identity and pass the gradient through unchanged. Exactly the L34 trick, same
      justification, and it still feels like cheating.}}
    \column{0.51\textwidth}
    \textbf{The loss is not squared error}
    \vskip 3pt
    \footnotesize
    Minimising squared error on a waveform gives muffled audio: two signals can be
    perceptually identical and numerically far apart, because the ear does not hear phase.
    \vskip 4pt
    \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      Instead: \textbf{multi-scale spectral loss} (compare log-spectrograms at several window
      sizes) plus \textbf{adversarial discriminators} - the ch8b idea, doing exactly what it is
      good at, judging whether something sounds real.}}
  \end{columns}
  \vskip 8pt
  \centering
  \footnotesize
  The second point is why a codec at 1.1 kbps sounds better than a 1990s codec at ten times the
  bitrate.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Is residual quantization the only way?}
  \small
  No, and a student who remembers L34 should already be suspicious - we saw there that growing a
  codebook has sharply diminishing returns, and that the field escaped by changing the
  \emph{quantizer} rather than its size.
  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      \textbf{Lookup-free and scalar quantization.} Drop the learned codebook; quantize each
      dimension to a few fixed levels and let the combination be the code. The audio analogue
      of MAGVIT-2 from L34.}}
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!12}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      \textbf{Single-codebook tokenizers.} If one frame is one token, Section 7's whole problem
      disappears. The price is quality, and the race is to push the frame rate down without
      paying it.}}
  \end{columns}
  \vskip 10pt
  \centering
  Same tension as every compression slide in this course: fewer tokens, or better sound.
\end{frame}

\section{Semantic tokens and acoustic tokens}

\begin{frame}[plain]
  \vfill
  \begin{center}
    {\Large\color{popblue}\textbf{Semantic tokens and acoustic tokens}}\\[0.9em]
    \begin{minipage}{0.82\textwidth}\centering\small
      What was said, and how it sounded. Separating them is the difference between a codec and
      something a language model can reason with.
    \end{minipage}
  \end{center}
  \vfill
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The goal: split what from how}
  \small
  A language model built on codec tokens has a problem. The tokens encode \emph{sound}, and most
  of what makes a sound is not what was said - it is the voice, the room, the microphone.
  \vskip 8pt
  \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.97\linewidth}{\small
    \textbf{What we want.} Level 1 carries \emph{what was said}. The rest carry \emph{who said
    it and how}. Then the model can reason over level 1 like it reasons over text, and treat
    the others as decoration.}}
  \vskip 8pt
  Kyutai's demonstration of the finished system makes it vivid: keep level 1, replace the rest,
  and you hear \textbf{the same words in a completely different voice}.
  \vskip 8pt
  \centering
  \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!12}{\parbox{0.9\linewidth}{\small\centering
    Note the word \textbf{goal}. Nothing so far makes this happen.}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{And it does not happen on its own}
  \small
  Take a plain residual quantizer that nobody told about meaning, and resynthesize audio from
  \textbf{level 1 alone}. If level 1 were "semantic", the words would survive and the voice
  would not.
  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{center}
    \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.9\linewidth}{\small
      \textbf{The opposite happens.} Probing EnCodec this way (Zhang et al., 2024), level 1
      alone retains a \textbf{speaker similarity of 0.92} - the voice comes through almost
      perfectly - while the content is destroyed.}}
  \end{center}
  \vskip 8pt
  Which makes sense in hindsight, and our own measurement two slides ago said the same thing
  from the other direction: \textbf{level 1 is whatever explains the most variance}, and in
  speech that is the gross spectral shape of a particular voice. Nothing in the training
  objective ever mentioned words.
  \vskip 6pt
  \centering
  \footnotesize
  "Coarse" and "semantic" are not synonyms.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Mimi's fix, and the attempt that failed first}
  \vskip -4pt
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.76\textwidth]{split_rvq.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vskip -6pt
  \footnotesize
  The obvious repair is to \emph{teach} level 1 to be semantic, with a loss making it match
  \textbf{WavLM} - the self-supervised speech model from L35, whose features track \emph{what
  was said}. Mimi's authors tried exactly that: it made the first quantizer much better at
  telling phonemes apart, and \textbf{made the audio worse}, because whatever level 1 stopped
  encoding still had to be carried by the residual it handed on.
  \vskip 3pt
  \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.97\linewidth}{\footnotesize
    So they took it out of the chain. \textbf{Worth copying as a way of working:} the obvious
    design was tried, measured, and restructured - and the paper reports the failure.}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{AudioLM: three stages, three jobs}
  \small
  AudioLM (Borsos et al., 2022) took the same split and made it the architecture.
  \vskip 2pt
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.92\textwidth]{audiolm_stages.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vskip -2pt
  \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.97\linewidth}{\footnotesize
    \textbf{Why split it at all?} Coherence over ten seconds and fidelity over ten
    milliseconds are different problems. One model doing both spends its capacity in the wrong
    place - which is precisely what the sample-level models in L35 did.}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The same three stages, in detail}
  \small
  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{center}
    \footnotesize
    \begin{tabular}{llp{5.2cm}}
      \toprule
      \textbf{Stage} & \textbf{Tokens} & \textbf{Job} \\
      \midrule
      1 & semantic, from w2v-BERT (L35) & long-range structure: syntax, meaning, where the
        melody is going \\
      2 & coarse acoustic, from SoundStream & voice, recording conditions \\
      \rowcolor{armblue!12}
      3 & fine acoustic & the last details of fidelity \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
  \end{center}
  \vskip 8pt
  Each stage is its own transformer, conditioned on the output of the one before.
  \vskip 6pt
  \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.97\linewidth}{\footnotesize
    \textbf{Why split it at all?} Coherence over ten seconds and fidelity over ten milliseconds
    are different problems. One model doing both spends its capacity in the wrong place - it
    produces audio that sounds gorgeous and says nothing, which is precisely what sample-level
    models did in L35.}}
\end{frame}

\section{Generating sound left to right}

\begin{frame}[plain]
  \vfill
  \begin{center}
    {\Large\color{popblue}\textbf{Generating sound left to right}}\\[0.9em]
    \begin{minipage}{0.82\textwidth}\centering\small
      We have a vocabulary. Now the awkward part: there are eight tokens per timestep and a
      language model emits one at a time.
    \end{minipage}
  \end{center}
  \vfill
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Eight tokens per frame, one tape}
  \small
  Residual quantization gives a \textbf{grid}: 8 codebooks deep, $T$ frames wide. Autoregressive
  generation needs a \textbf{line}. Somebody has to choose the order, and the choice is not free.
  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      Read it in the wrong order and you ask the model to predict fine detail before it has
      committed to the coarse content it is detailing.}}
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!12}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      Read it too carefully and you pay 8 forward passes per frame. At 12.5 Hz that is 100
      steps per second of audio - real time evaporates.}}
  \end{columns}
  \vskip 10pt
  \centering
  Exactly the raster-order question from L34, one dimension deeper.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Three orders, three prices}
  \vskip -2pt
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.97\textwidth]{codebook_patterns.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vskip 2pt
  \small
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.33\textwidth}
    \footnotesize
    \textbf{Flatten} - best quality, and $K \times T$ steps. Every token sees every earlier one.
    \column{0.33\textwidth}
    \footnotesize
    \textbf{Parallel} - $T$ steps, and it assumes the 8 codebooks of a frame are independent
    given the past. They are not.
    \column{0.34\textwidth}
    \footnotesize
    \textbf{Delay} - offset each codebook by one step. $T + K - 1$ steps, and codebook $k$ can
    still see codebook $k-1$ for its own frame.
  \end{columns}
  \vskip 8pt
  \centering
  \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.9\linewidth}{\footnotesize\centering
    MusicGen (Copet et al., 2023) uses delay with $K=4$: \textbf{$T+3$} steps instead of $4T$,
    for nearly the quality of flattening.}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{VALL-E: speech synthesis as next-token prediction}
  \small
  VALL-E (Wang et al., 2023) trained a language model on codec tokens over 60,000 hours. Text in,
  codec tokens out, decode to audio. Nothing about it is specific to speech - it is GPT with a
  different vocabulary.
  \vskip 8pt
  \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.97\linewidth}{\small
    The striking part is what came free. Prefix the generation with \textbf{3 seconds} of
    somebody's voice as codec tokens, and the model continues \emph{in that voice} - because
    continuing the prompt is the only thing it knows how to do. \textbf{Zero-shot voice cloning
    with no fine-tuning}, from a capability nobody added on purpose.}}
  \vskip 4pt
  \footnotesize
  The output is codec tokens, so getting audio back is just running the codec's decoder - the
  point the next slide but one makes in general.
  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      Three seconds of anyone's voice is a phone call, a voicemail, a video. The impersonation
      problem is a direct consequence of the architecture, not a misuse of it.}}
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      The technical mitigation is \textbf{watermarking}: embed a signal in generated audio that
      survives compression and re-recording. Moshi ships one.}}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The other lineage: do not generate left to right at all}
  \small
  Everything so far has been autoregressive. A large part of speech synthesis went the other
  way, and you have seen this argument before.
  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{center}
    \footnotesize
    \begin{tabular}{lll}
      \toprule
      & \textbf{Autoregressive} & \textbf{Non-autoregressive} \\
      \midrule
      How & next token, one at a time & denoise the whole utterance at once \\
      On what & discrete codec tokens & continuous latents (no quantization) \\
      Examples & VALL-E, CosyVoice & F5-TTS, MaskGCT, Voicebox \\
      Strength & prompting, streaming & speed, and no tokenizer to lose fidelity in \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
  \end{center}
  \vskip 8pt
  \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!12}{\parbox{0.97\linewidth}{\small
    \textbf{This is L34 section 7, again, for a different sense.} Discrete-and-autoregressive
    versus continuous-and-diffusion, the same trade-off, the same unresolved argument. When the
    same fork appears twice in two lectures, it is telling you something about the shape of the
    problem rather than about images or audio.}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Getting back to a waveform}
  \small
  Two lineages, and they answer "how do I turn this into sound" differently.
  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      \textbf{Mel plus a vocoder} (the Tacotron era). The model predicts a mel spectrogram; a
      separate network - HiFi-GAN and relatives - invents the phase that L35 threw away and
      produces a waveform. Two models, trained separately.}}
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      \textbf{Codec tokens plus the codec's decoder} (now). There is no separate vocoder,
      because \textbf{the codec decoder is the vocoder} - it was trained as the other half of
      the encoder that made the tokens.}}
  \end{columns}
  \vskip 10pt
  \centering
  \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!12}{\parbox{0.9\linewidth}{\footnotesize\centering
    A common confusion worth heading off: in a codec system, asking "and then which vocoder?"
    has no answer. The decoder already is one.}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{By hand: one second of audio, both directions}
  \small
  \begin{center}
    \footnotesize
    \begin{tabular}{lrr}
      \toprule
      & \textbf{Listening (L35)} & \textbf{Speaking (L36)} \\
      \midrule
      raw samples & 16,000 & 24,000 (Mimi runs at 24 kHz) \\
      frames & 100 log-mel & 12.5 codec frames \\
      after the encoder & 50 Whisper vectors & 12.5 frames $\times$ 8 codebooks \\
      \rowcolor{armblue!12}
      tokens the model handles & 25 (pooled) & \textbf{100} \\
      bits & continuous vectors & $12.5 \times 88 = 1{,}100$ bits \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
  \end{center}
  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.52\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!12}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      \textbf{Speaking costs more than listening.} Understanding can pool aggressively and throw
      away anything not needed for meaning. Generation has to produce every bit that comes out
      of the loudspeaker.}}
    \column{0.48\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      1,100 bits per second is \textbf{1.1 kbps} - the number from the codec slide, now derived
      rather than quoted.}}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\section{Talking at the same time}

\begin{frame}[plain]
  \vfill
  \begin{center}
    {\Large\color{popblue}\textbf{Talking at the same time}}\\[0.9em]
    \begin{minipage}{0.82\textwidth}\centering\small
      The one problem in this chapter that has no counterpart anywhere in vision.
    \end{minipage}
  \end{center}
  \vfill
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{A conversation is not turn-taking}
  \small
  Everything built so far assumes turns: you speak, it listens, you stop, it answers. Real
  conversation is not like that. A system that can listen and speak \emph{simultaneously} is
  called \textbf{full duplex}, borrowing the term from telephony.
  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \footnotesize
    \begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
      \item You say "mhm" while the other person is still talking
      \item Both of you start at once, and one yields
      \item You interrupt as soon as the answer is wrong
      \item The pause before "yes" carries the real answer
    \end{itemize}
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      A turn-based model cannot do any of this \textbf{by construction}, however fast it gets.
      The voice-activity detector from L35 is a \emph{guess} about when you finished, and it is
      making that guess with no idea what you were saying.}}
  \end{columns}
  \vskip 10pt
  \centering
  \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.92\linewidth}{\small\centering
    An image arrives all at once. A conversation is two streams that never stop. This is where
    the audio problem stops resembling the vision problem.}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Moshi: predict everything, every frame}
  \vskip -2pt
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.88\textwidth]{duplex_streams.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vskip -4pt
  \footnotesize
  Moshi (D\'efossez et al., 2024) drops turns entirely. Every 80 ms it predicts
  $K = 2Q+1 = \textbf{17}$ streams: \textbf{8} codebooks of its own audio, \textbf{8} of yours,
  and \textbf{1} text token.
  \vskip 4pt
  \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.97\linewidth}{\footnotesize
    It is \textbf{always} listening and \textbf{always} speaking. Silence is not the absence of
    output, it is a prediction like any other - which is what makes an interruption something
    the model can simply respond to, rather than an exception some surrounding code must handle.}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Inner monologue}
  \small
  A speech-to-speech model trained only on audio tokens speaks fluently and reasons badly. All
  that text pretraining is in there somewhere, and generating sound does not reach it.
  \vskip 2pt
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.86\textwidth]{inner_monologue.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vskip -4pt
  \footnotesize
  \centering
  The text stream is an \emph{internal scaffold}, not a stage in a pipeline. Nothing is
  transcribed and re-synthesized.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What that buys}
  \small
  \vskip 8pt
  \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.97\linewidth}{\small
    \textbf{Moshi's answer:} for each frame, predict the \textbf{text} token \emph{first}, then
    that frame's audio tokens conditioned on it. The model writes what it is about to say, then
    says it, 12.5 times a second.}}
  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.52\textwidth}
    \footnotesize
    Keeps the linguistic quality of a language model while the output stays genuinely
    speech-to-speech. Nothing is transcribed and re-synthesized - the text is an internal
    scaffold, not a stage in a pipeline.
    \column{0.48\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      And it comes with a bonus: shift the text stream relative to the audio and the same model
      does \textbf{streaming recognition} or \textbf{streaming synthesis}. Three systems, one
      set of weights.}}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why it takes two transformers}
  \small
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.88\textwidth]{rq_transformer.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vskip -4pt
  \footnotesize
  \centering
  The expensive model runs only 12.5 times a second. The cheap one runs 17 times inside each
  of those steps.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The two transformers, spelled out}
  \small
  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      \textbf{Temporal transformer.} 32 layers, 4,096 wide. Steps once per frame, at 12.5 Hz.
      This is where the language ability lives, and it does the expensive work only 12.5 times
      a second.}}
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      \textbf{Depth transformer.} 6 layers, 1,024 wide. Runs \emph{inside} one frame, across the
      17 streams, so each stream sees the ones decided before it. Small enough that 17 of these
      cost little.}}
  \end{columns}
  \vskip 8pt
  \centering
  This structure is the \textbf{RQ-Transformer} (Lee et al., 2022), borrowed from image
  generation rather than invented here.
  \vskip 6pt
  \footnotesize
  Result: \textbf{160 ms} of theoretical latency, about \textbf{200 ms} measured. For scale, one
  Mimi frame is 80 ms, so the floor is a small number of frames - though the paper reports the
  160 ms rather than breaking it down, so do not quote a decomposition.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What is still unsolved}
  \small
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \footnotesize
    \begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
      \item \textbf{Memory.} A one-hour conversation is 45,000 frames and 765,000 tokens.
        Nobody has a good answer.
      \item \textbf{More than two speakers.} The 17 streams assume exactly one of you.
      \item \textbf{Evaluation.} S2SBench for reasoning loss, MMAU and AIR-Bench for audio
        understanding - all young, none decisive.
      \item \textbf{Impersonation.} Watermarking helps; it does not solve it.
    \end{itemize}
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!12}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      \textbf{The open question worth watching: does learning to speak cost you thinking?}
      \vskip 4pt
      S2SBench measures a real drop when models are trained for speech output. Qwen3-Omni claims
      \emph{no} degradation against its single-modality counterparts.
      \vskip 4pt
      Both are published. They disagree. That is the honest state in 2026, and it is a better
      thing to tell students than a tidy answer.}}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Recap}
  \small
  \begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
    \item A language model needs a \textbf{finite vocabulary}. Rounding samples gives too many
      tokens; one big codebook needs $3 \times 10^{26}$ words.
    \item \textbf{Residual quantization} solves it by quantizing the leftovers, over and over.
      Our stand-in measurement: level 1 buys roughly \textbf{2.5$\times$} what levels 2-8 add
      between them - the ratio is the lesson, not the decibels.
    \item Codecs work on \textbf{waveforms}, not spectrograms, because a spectrogram cannot be
      played back.
    \item Level 1 is \textbf{not} semantic for free - it keeps the voice and loses the words.
      Mimi runs a semantic quantizer \textbf{in parallel}, after the obvious fix hurt the audio.
    \item Eight tokens per frame need an order: \textbf{flatten, parallel, or delay}.
    \item \textbf{Full duplex} is the genuinely new problem. Moshi predicts 17 streams every
      80 ms and never takes a turn.
  \end{itemize}
  \vskip 4pt
  \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.97\linewidth}{\footnotesize
    \textbf{Next:} \texttt{ml/llm\_training/}. Every model here was a pretrained language model
    with a new sense bolted on - so the question left is how that language model got made.}}
\end{frame}
