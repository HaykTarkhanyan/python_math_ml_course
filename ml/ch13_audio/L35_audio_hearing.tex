    \column{0.5\textwidth}

    \vskip 6pt

\section{A second of sound is 16,000 numbers}

\begin{frame}[plain]
  \vfill
  \begin{center}
    {\Large\color{popblue}\textbf{A second of sound is 16,000 numbers}}\\[0.9em]
    \begin{minipage}{0.82\textwidth}\centering\small
      Sound is not a sequence either. It is far worse than a picture.
    \end{minipage}
  \end{center}
  \vfill
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What a waveform actually is}
  \small
  A microphone measures air pressure. Sampling it 16,000 times a second gives one long list of
  numbers. Zoom in far enough and you can see the individual samples.
  \vskip 4pt
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.97\textwidth]{waveform_zoom.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vskip -2pt
  \footnotesize
  Synthesized speech, not a recording: the word PANIR from ch10, built with a formant model.
  Real speech is messier - two slides from now you will see exactly how much.
  \vskip 4pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.52\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      \textbf{16 kHz} is the \emph{speech} convention: nearly all speech energy is below 8 kHz,
      and sampling must be twice the highest frequency (Nyquist).}}
    \column{0.48\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!12}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      It is a convention, not a law. Mimi, EnCodec and MusicGen - \emph{neural audio codecs},
      all of which you meet properly in L36 - run at \textbf{24} and \textbf{32 kHz}. Watch for
      it when comparing rates.}}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The problem, as arithmetic}
  \small
  \begin{center}
    \begin{tabular}{lr}
      \toprule
      \textbf{Quantity} & \textbf{Per second} \\
      \midrule
      Audio samples at 16 kHz & 16,000 \\
      English text tokens (for comparison) & $\approx 2.5$ \\
      \midrule
      \rowcolor{armred!12}
      \textbf{Ratio} & $\mathbf{\approx 6{,}400\times}$ \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
  \end{center}
  \vskip 8pt
  A 30-second clip is \textbf{480,000} samples. Attention costs the square of sequence length
  (ch9), so this is not "expensive" - it is impossible.
  \vskip 8pt
  \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.97\linewidth}{\small
    Everything in the rest of this lecture is one idea: \textbf{get that number down}, without
    throwing away what makes it speech. Every architecture we look at is a compression scheme
    wearing a machine-learning hat.}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Predict first: could we just feed the raw samples in?}
  \small
  A transformer eats any sequence of vectors. Each audio sample is a number. So why not treat
  16,000 samples per second as 16,000 tokens and let attention sort it out?
  \vskip 10pt
  \pause
  \textbf{Two reasons, and the second is the interesting one.}
  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.48\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      \textbf{Cost.} Quadratic attention over 480,000 positions for half a minute of audio.
      No hardware fixes this.}}
    \column{0.52\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!12}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      \textbf{It does not work even when you can afford it.} Sample-level models (WaveNet,
      van den Oord et al., 2016) produce babble that sounds like speech locally and means
      nothing. Nothing holds structure across 16,000 steps.}}
  \end{columns}
  \vskip 8pt
  \centering
  The same lesson as characters versus tokens in text, an order of magnitude more extreme.
\end{frame}

\section{Turning sound into a picture}

\begin{frame}[plain]
  \vfill
  \begin{center}
    {\Large\color{popblue}\textbf{Turning sound into a picture}}\\[0.9em]
    \begin{minipage}{0.82\textwidth}\centering\small
      Six slides of signal processing. Every architecture number later in both lectures is
      arithmetic on what we set here.
    \end{minipage}
  \end{center}
  \vfill
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Framing: chop it up, and overlap the chops}
  \small
  Take a \textbf{25 ms} window, slide it along by a \textbf{10 ms} hop, and describe each window
  separately.
  \vskip 2pt
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.86\textwidth]{framing_window.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vskip -2pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      \textbf{Why 25 ms.} Long enough to resolve pitch, short enough that the vocal tract has
      not moved much. This single choice sets every frame rate downstream.}}
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!12}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      \textbf{100 frames per second: a 160$\times$ shorter \emph{sequence}.} Not
      "compression" - each frame is itself a vector of numbers, so the raw \emph{data} barely
      shrinks. Attention cares about \emph{length}.}}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{One spectrum per frame}
  \small
  Inside a 25 ms window, decompose the signal into the frequencies that make it up. That is the
  \textbf{Fast Fourier Transform} (\textbf{FFT}); doing it on every frame of a signal is the
  \textbf{Short-Time Fourier Transform} (\textbf{STFT}).
  \vskip 8pt
  Two things become readable that were invisible in the waveform:
  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.48\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      \textbf{Harmonics} - evenly spaced spikes at multiples of the pitch. Their spacing
      \emph{is} the pitch of the voice.}}
    \column{0.52\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      \textbf{Formants} - broad peaks where the vocal tract resonates. Their positions are what
      distinguish one vowel from another. This is what makes /a/ an /a/.}}
  \end{columns}
  \vskip 8pt
  \centering
  A 25 ms window at 16 kHz is 400 samples, which gives \textbf{201} frequency bins.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Learning to read one spectrum}
  \small
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.84\textwidth]{single_spectrum.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vskip -4pt
  \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.97\linewidth}{\footnotesize
    Two independent things live in one picture. The \textbf{ripple} is the vocal folds
    buzzing - its spacing is the pitch, and it changes when the same person speaks higher.
    The \textbf{envelope} is the shape of the mouth and throat - its bumps are the formants,
    and they decide which vowel you hear. \textbf{Change the first and the word survives;
    change the second and it becomes a different word.} That split comes back in L36 with a
    name: acoustic versus semantic.}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Is our synthetic signal honest?}
  \small
  Every figure in this lecture is labelled "synthesized". Here is a \textbf{real recording} of
  the same word beside it, so the label is checkable rather than a disclaimer.
  \vskip 2pt
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.76\textwidth]{real_vs_synth.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vskip -4pt
  \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!12}{\parbox{0.97\linewidth}{\footnotesize
    The real one has \textbf{breath, room noise and pitch that drifts} - everything a formant
    model does not bother to invent. What matches is what this lecture actually claims: the
    burst, the formant bands, the nasal dip, the trill.}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Magnitude and phase, and why we throw one away}
  \small
  The transform returns \emph{complex} numbers: a magnitude (how much of that frequency) and a
  \textbf{phase} (where in its cycle it is). Spectrograms keep only the magnitude.
  \vskip 2pt
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.68\textwidth]{phase_discard.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vskip -2pt
  \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.97\linewidth}{\footnotesize
    \textbf{Remember this slide.} A spectrogram \emph{cannot be played back}. That is why speech
    synthesis needs a \textbf{vocoder} - a network whose whole job is to invent plausible phase
    and turn a spectrogram back into a waveform - and why L36's codecs avoid the problem by
    working on \textbf{raw waveforms} instead.}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The mel scale: spend detail where the ear has it}
  \small
  Hearing is roughly logarithmic. The gap from 200 to 400 Hz is enormous; the gap from 7,000 to
  7,200 Hz is nearly inaudible. So we pool the 201 bins into \textbf{80} bands, narrow at the
  bottom and wide at the top.
  \vskip 2pt
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.84\textwidth]{mel_filterbank.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vskip -2pt
  \footnotesize
  The mapping is $m = 2595 \log_{10}(1 + f/700)$, fitted to human pitch judgements in the 1930s
  and still in every speech system today.
  \vskip 3pt
  \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!12}{\parbox{0.97\linewidth}{\footnotesize
    \textbf{Now the arithmetic promised three slides ago.} 100 frames a second, 80 numbers each,
    is \textbf{8,000 numbers} per second against 16,000 raw samples. The \emph{sequence} got
    160$\times$ shorter; the \emph{data} only halved. That gap is the whole trick.}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Take the log, and you are done}
  \small
  \vskip -2pt
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.86\textwidth]{spectrogram_stages.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vskip -2pt
  \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.97\linewidth}{\footnotesize
    Panel 3 is not a broken figure. On a \textbf{linear} scale almost all the structure is
    invisible, because loudness spans orders of magnitude. The log in panel 4 is what makes it
    readable - to us, and to a network. \textbf{Panel 4 is exactly what Whisper is fed.}}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Reading the picture}
  \small
  The spectrogram is not a coloured rectangle. Every region of it says something, and you can
  now name all of it:
  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{center}
    \footnotesize
    \begin{tabular}{llp{5.6cm}}
      \toprule
      \textbf{Sound} & \textbf{What to look for} & \textbf{Why} \\
      \midrule
      P (closure) & a silent gap & the mouth is shut; a stop consonant is partly \emph{silence} \\
      P (burst) & a brief wideband smear & the pressure releases all at once \\
      A & two low bands, far apart & first and second formants of an open vowel \\
      N & energy collapses to the bottom & sound is coming out of the nose, not the mouth \\
      I & a low band and a very high one & the tongue is forward; the formants split apart \\
      \rowcolor{armorange!14}
      R & regular vertical stripes & the tongue is tapping, about 25 times a second \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
  \end{center}
  \vskip 6pt
  \centering
  Speech recognition was hand-built on features like these for forty years before anyone
  learned them.
\end{frame}

  \vskip 8pt
  Replace "audio encoder" with "vision encoder" and this is L33 exactly. The language model is

  \vskip 8pt
  \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.97\linewidth}{\small
    This is the cheapest idea in the chapter and the most important. \textbf{Any} modality that
    can be turned into a sequence of vectors plugs into the same socket. Audio is not special;
    it is just harder to compress.}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Whisper, the encoder almost everyone uses}
  \small
  Whisper (Radford et al., 2022) was trained for speech recognition on 680,000 hours. Almost
  nobody uses the whole thing - they cut off the encoder and throw the decoder away.
  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{center}
    \footnotesize
    \begin{tabular}{lr}
      \toprule
      \textbf{Stage} & \textbf{Size for a 30 s chunk} \\
      \midrule
      Raw samples at 16 kHz & 480,000 \\
      Log-mel frames (10 ms hop) & $80 \times 3{,}000$ \\
      After two convolutions, \textbf{the second with stride 2} & $\mathbf{1{,}500}$ vectors \\
      \rowcolor{armblue!12}
      Frame rate reaching the transformer & \textbf{50 Hz} \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
  \end{center}
  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!12}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      \textbf{80 mel bins for every size except large-v3, which uses 128.} They are not
      interchangeable - feeding 80 to a 128-bin model fails outright.}}
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      Only \emph{one} of the two convolutions has stride 2. Two would halve it twice and give
      750, and the number everyone quotes is 1,500.}}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The whole front end, in one line}
  \small
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.98\textwidth]{whisper_pipeline.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vskip -2pt
  \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.97\linewidth}{\footnotesize
    Every number here came from a decision made earlier in this lecture. The 3,000 is the

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Where "semantic" audio features come from}
  \small
  Three models get named constantly in this field, and all three are the same idea you already
  know from text: \textbf{mask part of the input and predict it}, with no labels.
  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{center}
    \footnotesize
    \begin{tabular}{lll}
      \toprule
      \textbf{Model} & \textbf{Trained on} & \textbf{Used for} \\
      \midrule
      WavLM (Chen et al., 2022) & speech & frames that encode \emph{what was said} \\
      w2v-BERT (Chung et al., 2021) & speech & the semantic tokens in AudioLM (L36) \\
      Bidirectional Encoder representation & general audio & everything that is \emph{not} speech: \\
      from Audio Transformers (BEATs, & & music, dog barks, doors \\
      Chen et al., 2023) & & \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
  \end{center}
  \vskip 6pt
  \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.97\linewidth}{\footnotesize
    Run k-means on their continuous output frames and you get discrete \textbf{units} - a
    crude alphabet for speech, learned without a single transcript. Hold on to this: it is what
    makes "semantic tokens" mean something in L36 rather than sounding like magic.}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{By hand: what 30 seconds costs}
  \small
  \begin{center}

      \textbf{Step} & \textbf{Count} & \textbf{Why} \\

  \small
  Everyone agrees on "encoder, then language model". The disagreement is \textbf{how much of

  \vskip 2pt
  \begin{center}

\begin{frame}{Design A: a projector (Qwen2-Audio)}
  \small
  Qwen2-Audio (Chu et al., 2024) does the simplest thing that works.
  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
    \item Whisper-large-v3 encoder, so 128 mel bins in and 50 Hz out.
    \item A pooling layer with stride 2, giving \textbf{25 Hz} - one vector per 40 ms of audio.
    \item A \textbf{single linear layer} maps each vector into the language model's embedding
      space. The audio vectors are then simply placed in the sequence alongside text.
  \end{itemize}
  \vskip 8pt
  \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.97\linewidth}{\small
    This is LLaVA (L33) with a microphone. Note it is the \emph{single-linear} version, not the
    two-layer projector of LLaVA-1.5 - the audio encoder is already doing the heavy lifting.}}
  \vskip 6pt
  \centering
  \footnotesize
  Cost: 30 seconds of audio occupies 750 positions in the context window.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Design B: a resampler (SALMONN)}
  \small
  SALMONN (Tang et al., 2024) refuses to let cost scale with duration.
  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
    \item \textbf{Two} encoders, concatenated: Whisper-large-v2 for speech, BEATs for
      everything else.
    \item A window-level Q-Former (the resampler idea from L33) compresses each window to a
      fixed number of learned query vectors.
    \item 30 seconds becomes \textbf{88 tokens}. One token per \textbf{340 ms}, no matter what
      is in it.
  \end{itemize}
  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      \textbf{88 against 750.} An 8.5$\times$ saving, and the budget is now predictable.}}
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!12}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      And a fixed budget is also a ceiling. Whatever a busy 30 seconds contained, it is getting
      88 tokens.}}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Design C: built in from the start (Qwen3-Omni)}
  \small
  Qwen3-Omni (Qwen Team, 2025) does not bolt an encoder on. The audio encoder is trained with
  the model.
  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
    \item \textbf{Audio Transformer} (\textbf{AuT}), trained from scratch on \textbf{20 million
      hours}, emitting \textbf{12.5 Hz} - the lowest rate on the ladder.
    \item A \textbf{Thinker} that reasons in text and a \textbf{Talker} that emits speech, both
      mixture-of-experts.
    \item Theoretical first packet of audio out: \textbf{234 ms}.
  \end{itemize}
  \vskip 8pt
  \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.97\linewidth}{\small
    The same trajectory as vision in L33: bolted-on projector first, trained-together later,
    as soon as there was enough data to justify it. The Talker half is L36's subject.}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why SALMONN carries two encoders}
  \small
  This looks like redundancy. It is not, and the reason is a genuinely useful thing to know.
  \vskip 2pt
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.84\textwidth]{speech_vs_audio.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vskip -4pt
  \footnotesize
  \centering
  Most of the gap between a "speech model" and an "audio model" on benchmarks is this one
  design choice.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why that happens, stated plainly}
  \small
  \vskip 8pt
  \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.97\linewidth}{\small
    A speech encoder is trained to produce \textbf{transcripts}. Everything that is not a word
    is, to that training objective, \textbf{noise to be suppressed}: the dog barking, the door,
    the music under the dialogue, whether the speaker is a child, whether there are two of them.
    Whisper is very good at ignoring exactly what you might want to ask about.}}
  \vskip 8pt
  So if you want a model that hears, and not merely one that transcribes, you need an encoder
  that was never taught to throw sound away.
  \vskip 8pt
  \centering
  \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.9\linewidth}{\small\centering
    This is most of the gap between "speech model" and "audio model" on benchmarks.}}
\end{frame}

\section{What the cascade throws away}

\begin{frame}[plain]
  \vfill
  \begin{center}
    {\Large\color{popblue}\textbf{What the cascade throws away}}\\[0.9em]
    \begin{minipage}{0.82\textwidth}\centering\small
      There is an obvious pipeline that avoids all of this. It is worth knowing exactly what it
      costs, and exactly what it is still better at.
    \end{minipage}
  \end{center}
  \vfill
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The cascade, and what falls out of it}

  \vskip 6pt
  Both arrows in the middle are \textbf{plain text}. Everything the sound carried that text
  cannot hold dies there:
  \vskip 4pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \footnotesize
    who is speaking \quad emotion \quad sarcasm \\
    hesitation \quad pace \quad emphasis \\
    two people at once \quad background sound \\
    accent \quad whether it was speech at all
    \column{0.5\textwidth}

  \end{columns}
  \vskip 6pt
  \footnotesize
  And turn-taking is a guess: a \textbf{voice-activity detector} (\textbf{VAD}) decides you have
  stopped talking. That is precisely why interrupting these systems feels wrong.

\begin{frame}{The honest counter-argument}
  \small
  Native models win on latency and on everything text cannot carry. Cascades still run most
  production systems in 2026, and the reasons are good ones.
  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      \textbf{For the cascade}\\[3pt]
      You can read the transcript, so you can debug it, log it, and satisfy an auditor.
      You can swap any of the three parts for a better or cheaper one. Dozens of vendors
      compete at each stage.}}
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!12}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      \textbf{For native}\\[3pt]
      About a \textbf{quarter} of the latency of a tuned cascade, and a tenth of a naive one.
      Keeps prosody and emotion. Handles overlap and interruption. But it is a black box from a
      small number of vendors, and you cannot inspect what it "heard".}}
  \end{columns}
  \vskip 10pt
  \centering
  \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.92\linewidth}{\small\centering
    "The better architecture" is not the same question as "the better system to deploy on
    Tuesday". Both answers are defensible right now.}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The bill}
  \small
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.78\textwidth]{audio_token_budget.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vskip -4pt
  \footnotesize
  Gemini bills \textbf{32 tokens per second} of audio input, so one minute is \textbf{1,920}
  tokens and the documented ceiling is 9.5 hours in a single prompt. Output audio runs about 25
  tokens per second.
  \vskip 4pt
  \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.97\linewidth}{\footnotesize
    \textbf{This is the ratio from the by-hand slide, arriving from a different direction.}
    There we counted \emph{encoder frames} and got about 10$\times$; here we are reading a
    \emph{vendor's price list} and get about 13$\times$. Different systems, different
    measurements, same order of magnitude - which is what makes it a fact about the modality
    rather than about one company. Same lesson as L33's 576-tokens-per-image slide:
    \textbf{the modality is the cost}.}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Where audio models still fail}
  \small
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.52\textwidth}
    \textbf{Known weak spots}
    \vskip 3pt
    \footnotesize
    \begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
      \item Long recordings - the context bill above
      \item Counting and separating speakers
      \item Exact timestamps ("when was this said")
      \item Music, beyond naming the genre
      \item Low-resource languages, \textbf{Armenian among them}
    \end{itemize}
    \column{0.48\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      \textbf{Whisper hallucinates on silence.} Koenecke et al.\ (2024) found invented phrases
      in about \textbf{1\%} of transcriptions, and \textbf{38\% of those} contained explicit
      harms - invented violence, made-up names, fabricated authority. They cluster on long
      non-vocal stretches.}}
  \end{columns}
  \vskip 10pt
  \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!12}{\parbox{0.97\linewidth}{\footnotesize
    The failure mode is worth pausing on. A recognition model must emit \emph{something}, and
    trained on 680,000 hours of mostly-speech it has never seen a long silence that meant
    nothing. The same language prior that makes it fluent makes it confabulate - exactly the
    vision-model hallucination story from L33, with a different sense organ.}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Recap}
  \small
  \begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
    \item Sound arrives as \textbf{16,000 numbers a second}, about 6,400 times the rate of text.
      Every design in this lecture exists to bring that number down.
    \item \textbf{25 ms windows, 10 ms hop, 80 mel bins, log.} Six slides of signal processing
      that turn sound into a picture, and set every frame rate that follows.
    \item Keeping only magnitude \textbf{throws phase away}, so a spectrogram cannot be played
      back. Remember it in L36.
    \item \textbf{Encoder, projector, language model} - the same three boxes as L33. Whisper at
      50 Hz, pooled to 25, or resampled to a fixed 88 tokens per 30 s.
    \item The \textbf{cascade} still wins on debuggability and vendor choice; it costs between
      0.6 and 2 seconds of extra latency, and everything a transcript cannot hold.
  \end{itemize}
  \vskip 8pt
  \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.97\linewidth}{\small
    \textbf{Next:} the model has to answer \emph{out loud}. A language model emits discrete
    tokens from a fixed vocabulary, and sound is continuous - so we go and invent a vocabulary
    for sound, then teach the model to talk and listen at the same time.}}
\end{frame}
