\section{What a pixel actually stores}
\sectiontransition{A pixel is not a colour}{It is three numbers, and the reason there are
exactly three has nothing to do with computers.}

\begin{frame}{How your eye sees colour}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.44\textwidth}\small
    Your retina has three kinds of \textbf{cone} cell, tuned to short, medium and long
    wavelengths - roughly \textbf{blue}, \textbf{green} and \textbf{red}.
    \vskip 5pt
    Light arrives as a whole \emph{spectrum}; your eye collapses it to \textbf{three
    numbers}. Everything else is thrown away.
    \vskip 5pt
    Two spectra that fire the cones equally look identical to you - \textbf{metamerism}.
    A screen exploits exactly that: three primaries fake any spectrum you can see.
    \vskip 5pt
    \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\small
      Images use \textbf{red, green, blue} (\textbf{RGB}) because \emph{you} do. Three is
      a fact about eyes, not about file formats.}}
    \column{0.56\textwidth}
    \begin{center}\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{eye_cones.pdf}\end{center}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{A pixel is three numbers}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.34\textwidth}\small
    A colour image stores an \textbf{(R, G, B)} triple at every pixel, each $0$ to $255$.
    \vskip 5pt
    Red and green high $=$ yellow; all three high $=$ white; all three equal $=$ grey.
    \vskip 5pt
    So an image is \textbf{three stacked grids}. Flatten them and you get the matrix you
    handed to k-means: one row per pixel, three columns.
    \vskip 5pt
    \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\small
      To a computer there is no image, only a grid of numbers.}}
    \column{0.66\textwidth}
    \begin{center}\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{rgb_channels.pdf}\end{center}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The number is not the brightness}
  \small
  Stored pixel values are \textbf{gamma-encoded}: the standard sRGB (standard RGB) curve
  spends more of the $0$--$255$ budget on dark tones, where your eye can tell shades
  apart, and fewer on bright ones, where it cannot.
  \begin{center}\includegraphics[width=0.93\linewidth]{gamma_curve.pdf}\end{center}
  \vskip -3pt
  \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!12}{\parbox{0.965\linewidth}{\small
    Average black ($0$) and white ($255$) as \emph{stored numbers} and you get $128$ -
    which emits $21\%$ of the light, not $50\%$. The honest answer is $188$. Every naive
    image resize makes this mistake.}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Linearize, compute, re-encode}
  \small
  The sRGB curve is a formula, not a mood. For a stored value $s$ scaled to $[0,1]$:
  \vskip 4pt
  \begin{center}\footnotesize
    $\text{linear}(s) = \begin{cases}
      s/12.92 & s \le 0.04045\\[3pt]
      \left(\dfrac{s + 0.055}{1.055}\right)^{2.4} & \text{otherwise}
    \end{cases}$
    \qquad
    $\text{encode}(u) = 1.055\, u^{1/2.4} - 0.055$
  \end{center}
  \vskip 6pt
  So the claim from two slides ago, all the way through. Average black and white
  \emph{as light}:
  \vskip 3pt
  \begin{center}\footnotesize
    $\text{linear}(0) = 0$, \quad $\text{linear}(1) = 1$
    \quad$\Rightarrow$\quad mean $= 0.5$
    \quad$\Rightarrow$\quad
    $255 \cdot \left(1.055 \cdot 0.5^{1/2.4} - 0.055\right) = \mathbf{187.5}$
  \end{center}
  \vskip 4pt
  And in the other direction, pixel $128$:
  $\left(\frac{0.502 + 0.055}{1.055}\right)^{2.4} = \mathbf{0.216}$ - the $21.6\%$ from the
  hook.
  \vskip 6pt
  \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.965\linewidth}{\small
    That is the whole of ``linearize'' and ``re-encode'' - one \texttt{np.where} each. The
    dark branch is a straight line because a pure power law has infinite slope at zero,
    which would make the darkest few values impossible to encode.}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Where this bites you}
  \small
  Gamma is invisible until you do arithmetic on pixels. Then it is everywhere:
  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{itemize}\small
    \item \textbf{Resizing and blurring.} Both average neighbouring pixels. Done on stored
      values, fine detail loses light and gradients go muddy.
    \item \textbf{Brightness augmentation.} Multiplying stored values is not the same as
      changing the exposure of a photograph.
    \item \textbf{Averaging a dataset.} A mean image computed on stored values is darker
      than the mean of the light those images carried.
    \item \textbf{Any distance.} Two pixels $30$ apart near black differ far more in light
      than two pixels $30$ apart near white.
  \end{itemize}
  \vskip 6pt
  The fix is always the same three steps: \textbf{linearize} $\to$ do the arithmetic
  $\to$ \textbf{re-encode}.
  \vskip 6pt
  \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.965\linewidth}{\small
    Almost no code does this, including most of what you will write. That is usually
    survivable - but know that you are making the approximation, rather than discovering
    it later in a result you cannot explain.}}
\end{frame}

\section{Same pixel, different axes}
\sectiontransition{RGB says how to \emph{make} a colour}{It does not say what the colour
\emph{is}. Nothing in the three numbers means ``how bright'' or ``which colour''.
\vskip 10pt
{\footnotesize Play with it while I talk: \textcolor{armblue}{\texttt{math.hws.edu/graphicsbook/demos/c2/rgb-hsv.html}}\\[2pt]
Six sliders, three RGB and three HSV, wired to the same colour. Move one, watch the others move.}}

\begin{frame}{Hue, saturation, value}
  \small
  \textbf{Hue, saturation, value} (\textbf{HSV}) keeps the same pixel and changes the
  axes: \textbf{hue} is an angle - which colour; \textbf{saturation} is a radius - how
  pure; \textbf{value} is a height - how bright.
  \begin{center}\includegraphics[width=0.72\linewidth]{hsv_space.pdf}\end{center}
  \vskip -4pt
  \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!12}{\parbox{0.965\linewidth}{\small
    In RGB, ``darker'' is ambiguous: subtracting a constant drags the hue $25^\circ$ and
    crushes $14\%$ of pixels to black, while multiplying leaves hue alone. In HSV,
    brightness \emph{is} an axis - moving $V$ cannot do anything else.}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The trap: hue is a circle, distance is not}
  \small
  Hue wraps: $H = 0.99$ and $H = 0.01$ are both red. Hand those to any algorithm that
  measures straight-line distance and they are as far apart as two colours can be.
  \begin{center}\includegraphics[width=0.93\linewidth]{hue_seam.pdf}\end{center}
  \vskip -3pt
  \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.965\linewidth}{\small
    Feed k-means raw $(H, S, V)$ and the reds either side of the seam land in the same
    cluster \textbf{0\%} of the time. Encode the angle first -
    $(S\cos 2\pi H,\; S\sin 2\pi H,\; V)$ - and it is \textbf{99\%}. Plain RGB, which has
    no angles in it, never had the problem.}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{When hue earns its keep}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.38\textwidth}
    \small
    Pick out one coloured region, then change the lighting - every pixel loses $45\%$ of
    its value, and nothing else moves.
    \vskip 5pt
    The RGB threshold was tuned on the bright image and still collapses. The hue window
    barely notices: dimming moves $V$, not $H$.
    \vskip 5pt
    Overlap before versus after, as Intersection over Union (IoU) - pixels both
    selections agree on, over pixels either one picked:
    \vskip 3pt
    \begin{center}\small
      RGB \textbf{\color{armred}20\%} \qquad hue \textbf{\color{armblue}99\%}
    \end{center}
    \vskip 4pt
    \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\small
      Colour masks and thresholds: use hue. It is the one job HSV is clearly best at.}}
    \column{0.62\textwidth}
    \begin{center}\includegraphics[width=0.94\linewidth]{hsv_threshold.pdf}\end{center}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Where does depth live?}
  \small
  You read 3D shape from \textbf{shading}. So which of the three numbers carries it?
  \pause
  \vskip 2pt
  On a matte surface, changing the light \textbf{multiplies R, G and B by the same
  factor} - a move along $V$, with $H$ and $S$ untouched.
  \begin{center}\includegraphics[width=0.64\linewidth]{depth_shading.pdf}\end{center}
  \vskip -4pt
  \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.93\linewidth}{\small
    Across each lit sphere $H$ and $S$ hold to $10^{-9}$; $V$ tracks the true shading at
    $\mathbf{1.000000}$. Gloss is the exception - a highlight adds white, so it shows in
    $S$.}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{So: depth, edges and texture are luminance problems}
  \small
  That one fact settles a lot of practical questions at once:
  \vskip 5pt
  \begin{itemize}\small
    \item \textbf{Edge detection, corners, texture, optical flow} run on \textbf{one}
      channel, not three. Converting to grayscale first is not laziness, it is the right
      move - the structure was never in the colour.
    \item \textbf{Shape-from-shading and relighting} want a space where brightness is an
      axis. $V$, or better $L^*$, which is scaled to how you actually perceive brightness.
    \item \textbf{Chroma subsampling works for the same reason} - the slide two sections
      from now throws away three quarters of the colour and you will barely see it.
    \item \textbf{Colour is for \emph{what}, luminance is for \emph{where}.} Hue tells you
      which object; brightness tells you its shape and where its edges are.
  \end{itemize}
  \vskip 5pt
  \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!12}{\parbox{0.965\linewidth}{\small
    The trap: dropping to grayscale also destroys any boundary that is a pure
    \emph{colour} change at equal brightness - a red shape on an equally bright green
    background vanishes completely. Rare in photographs, common in charts, maps and
    synthetic images.}}
\end{frame}

\section{Throwing colour away}
\sectiontransition{One number instead of three}{Grayscale is the most common preprocessing
step in vision, and the recipe is less obvious than it looks.}

\begin{frame}{What should grey be?}
  \small
  \textbf{Predict:} to turn (R, G, B) into one number, is the average $(R+G+B)/3$ the
  right answer?
  \pause
  \vskip 4pt
  No - and the reason is the cone curves from the first section. The M and L cones both
  peak in the green-yellow region, so green carries most of the perceived brightness and
  blue very little.
  \begin{center}\includegraphics[width=0.86\linewidth]{gray_weights.pdf}\end{center}
  \vskip -3pt
  \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.965\linewidth}{\small
    Grayscale is a \emph{weighted} sum, and the weights come from the eye: green about
    $60$--$72\%$, red about $21$--$30\%$, blue only $7$--$11\%$.}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Two standards, and your libraries disagree}
  \small
  There are two weight sets in common use. Rec.\,601 came from standard-definition
  television; Rec.\,709 matches the primaries actually used by modern displays and sRGB.
  \vskip 5pt
  \begin{center}\footnotesize
  \begin{tabular}{lccc}
    \toprule
    & \textbf{R} & \textbf{G} & \textbf{B} \\
    \midrule
    naive mean & $0.333$ & $0.333$ & $0.333$ \\
    Rec.\,601 & $0.299$ & $0.587$ & $0.114$ \\
    Rec.\,709 & $0.2126$ & $0.7152$ & $0.0722$ \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}
  \vskip 6pt
  By hand, on the pixel $(R, G, B) = (200, 100, 50)$:
  \vskip 3pt
  \begin{center}\footnotesize
    Rec.\,601: $0.299(200) + 0.587(100) + 0.114(50) = 59.8 + 58.7 + 5.7 = \mathbf{124.2}$\\[2pt]
    Rec.\,709: $0.2126(200) + 0.7152(100) + 0.0722(50) = 42.5 + 71.5 + 3.6 = \mathbf{117.6}$
  \end{center}
  \vskip 6pt
  \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.965\linewidth}{\small
    \texttt{PIL.Image.convert("L")} implements Rec.\,601.
    \texttt{skimage.color.rgb2gray} implements Rec.\,709. On the Saryan painting they
    differ by \textbf{4.3} grey levels on average and up to \textbf{24}. Same input, two
    different grayscale images, and no error message.}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Luma is not luminance}
  \small
  Both formulas above get applied to the \textbf{stored, gamma-encoded} numbers. That
  makes them \emph{luma} ($Y'$) - a cheap stand-in. Real \emph{luminance} ($Y$) needs the
  light, not the code:
  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{center}\footnotesize
  \begin{tabular}{ll}
    \toprule
    \textbf{luma} $Y'$ (what everyone computes) & weight the stored values directly \\
    \textbf{luminance} $Y$ (what it should be) & linearize $\to$ weight $\to$ re-encode \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}
  \vskip 8pt
  On the same painting the two disagree by \textbf{4.1} levels on average and by as much
  as \textbf{48} in the worst pixel - a bigger error than the gap between the two
  standards.
  \vskip 8pt
  \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!12}{\parbox{0.965\linewidth}{\small
    Video engineering has kept the distinction (and the prime on $Y'$) for decades;
    image-processing code has mostly dropped it. If a grayscale result looks wrong in the
    shadows, this is the first thing to check.}}
\end{frame}

\section{Distance should mean difference}
\sectiontransition{The space you choose is the distance you get}{k-means has no idea what
your features mean. It measures Euclidean distance in whatever you hand it.}

\begin{frame}{Lab: built so that distance matches perception}
  \small
  \textbf{CIELAB} (from the Commission Internationale de l'\'Eclairage, the international
  colour standards body) splits colour into lightness $L^*$ and two opponent axes
  $a^*$ (green to red) and $b^*$ (blue to yellow), scaled so that \emph{equal distance
  means equal perceived difference}. That distance has a name: $\Delta E$.
  \begin{center}\includegraphics[width=0.90\linewidth]{lab_deltae.pdf}\end{center}
  \vskip -3pt
  \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.965\linewidth}{\small
    Every pair above sits the \emph{same} RGB distance apart, yet perceived difference
    runs from $\Delta E\,4.4$ to $16.0$ - a $3.7\times$ spread, where about $2.3$ is the
    threshold of noticing at all. RGB distance is a proxy, and a loose one.}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{YCbCr: keep the brightness, skimp on the colour}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.38\textwidth}
    \small
    Split a pixel into brightness $Y$ and two colour-difference channels, $C_b$
    (blue-yellow) and $C_r$ (red-green).
    \vskip 5pt
    Store the colour channels at \emph{half resolution} in each direction. That is
    \textbf{4{:}2{:}0} - what JPEG and every video codec do.
    \vskip 5pt
    Halve the \emph{brightness} detail instead and it visibly falls apart: error $5.8$
    versus $11.1$, with blocky edges.
    \vskip 5pt
    \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\small
      Your eye resolves \emph{brightness} detail far better than \emph{colour} detail.
      Compression has exploited that for thirty years.}}
    \column{0.62\textwidth}
    \begin{center}\includegraphics[width=0.92\linewidth]{chroma_subsample.pdf}\end{center}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Colour histograms: a feature that cannot see}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.40\textwidth}
    \small
    A \textbf{colour histogram} counts how many pixels fall in each range of each channel.
    It turns any image, at any size, into a fixed-length vector - here $96$ numbers
    instead of $307{,}200$.
    \vskip 5pt
    Useful, with one fatal blind spot: it discards \textbf{where} every pixel was.
    \vskip 5pt
    Shuffle the painting's pixels and the histogram does not move by a single count. Both
    images hand k-means the \emph{identical} vector.
    \vskip 5pt
    \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\small
      Cluster colour histograms and you group by \textbf{palette}, never by subject. A
      sunset and a tomato are neighbours.}}
    \column{0.60\textwidth}
    \begin{center}\includegraphics[width=0.94\linewidth]{color_histogram.pdf}\end{center}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The wider zoo}
  \vskip -2pt
  \begin{center}\footnotesize
  \begin{tabular}{p{2.1cm}p{9.7cm}}
    \toprule
    \textbf{CIE XYZ} & The root (1931), straight from colour-matching experiments on human
      observers. Lab and everything else perceptual is \emph{defined} as a transform of it. \\[2pt]
    \textbf{HSL} & HSV's sibling: lightness instead of value, so pure colours sit at
      $L = 0.5$ and white tops the axis. Nicer for pickers, same circular hue, same trap. \\[2pt]
    \textbf{LCh} & Lab in polar form - lightness, chroma, hue \emph{angle}. Perceptual
      \emph{and} intuitive, but the angle brings the seam back with it. \\[2pt]
    \textbf{Oklab / Oklch} & Bj\"orn Ottosson, 2020. Lab is non-uniform in the blues; Oklab
      refits it to fix that. In the CSS standard since 2021. The modern default. \\[2pt]
    \textbf{CMYK} & Subtractive - ink absorbs rather than emits, so the primaries are cyan,
      magenta, yellow and black. For print, not for models. \\[2pt]
    \textbf{Linear RGB} & Not a different colour, a different \emph{encoding}: sRGB with the
      gamma undone. Where physics happens, and where you land whenever you linearize. \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}
  \vskip 2pt
  \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.965\linewidth}{\small
    All the same three cone responses in different coordinates. Choosing one picks which
    questions become easy; it does not change what colour is.}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Start from the task, not the space}
  \vskip -2pt
  \begin{center}\footnotesize
  \begin{tabular}{p{5.0cm}p{2.9cm}p{4.3cm}}
    \toprule
    \textbf{What you are doing} & \textbf{Work in} & \textbf{Because} \\
    \midrule
    Edges, texture, depth, shading & grayscale / $V$ / $L^*$ & the structure is in
      luminance; colour adds nothing \\[2pt]
    Masking an object by its colour & HSV, hue window & survives lighting changes \\[2pt]
    Feeding a model; reconstructing & RGB & it is what the error is measured in, and what
      the data already is \\[2pt]
    Quantizing to get the image back & RGB & squared error lives here \\[2pt]
    Quantizing for a coherent palette & cone-encoded HSV & groups by hue across light and
      shade \\[2pt]
    ``Do these two look different?'' & Lab / Oklab, $\Delta E$ & distance is calibrated to
      perception \\[2pt]
    Compressing & YCbCr & spends bits where the eye looks \\[2pt]
    Any arithmetic on pixel values & linear RGB & sums are only meaningful on light \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}
  \vskip 2pt
  \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.965\linewidth}{\small
    One question answers most of it: \textbf{is this about \emph{which colour}, or about
    \emph{how much light}?} The first wants hue, the second wants a luminance axis.}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Four ways to get it wrong}
  \small
  Each space has one failure that catches people, and you have now seen all four:
  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{itemize}\small
    \item \textbf{RGB} - distance only loosely matches perception, and every value is
      gamma-encoded, so arithmetic on it is arithmetic on the wrong quantity.
    \item \textbf{HSV} - hue is circular. Hand raw $(H, S, V)$ to anything that measures
      distance and the reds tear in half.
    \item \textbf{Lab} - costs a conversion, and $\Delta E$ is calibrated for flat patches
      viewed side by side, not for textures or thin lines.
    \item \textbf{Grayscale} - two libraries will hand you two different images, and any
      boundary that is pure colour at constant brightness disappears entirely.
  \end{itemize}
  \vskip 6pt
  \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!12}{\parbox{0.965\linewidth}{\small
    And three numbers is a convention, not a law. The Sentinel-2 satellite scene in the
    next practical gives you \textbf{six} per pixel, two of them infrared - light your
    eyes cannot see at all, which is exactly why it is worth recording. Same idea, more
    cones.}}
\end{frame}

\section*{Wrap-up}

\begin{frame}{Recap}
  \begin{itemize}\small
    \item A pixel is \textbf{three numbers} because you have \textbf{three cone types}.
      Different spectra that fire them equally are the same colour to you.
    \item Stored values are \textbf{gamma-encoded}: $128$ is $21\%$ of the light, not
      $50\%$. Arithmetic on pixels needs linearize $\to$ compute $\to$ re-encode.
    \item \textbf{HSV} makes brightness an axis, which is why hue thresholds survive
      lighting changes ($99\%$ versus $20\%$). But hue is \textbf{circular} - encode the
      angle or the seam destroys your clustering.
    \item \textbf{Grayscale} is a weighted sum with the weights taken from the eye. Two
      standards are in circulation and your libraries pick different ones.
    \item \textbf{Depth, edges and texture are luminance problems.} Shading is exactly a
      move along $V$, so grayscale keeps the shape and flattening $V$ destroys it.
    \item \textbf{Lab} makes distance mean perceived difference; \textbf{YCbCr} lets
      compression throw colour away cheaply; \textbf{colour histograms} throw away
      position entirely.
    \item To pick a space, ask: \textbf{which colour, or how much light?}
  \end{itemize}
  \vfill
  \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.97\linewidth}{\small
    \textbf{Next:} the practicals. Quantize a painting with k-means and choose the space
    deliberately; cluster a six-band satellite scene where the ``colours'' are land cover;
    and watch colour histograms fail to group photographs by what is in them.}}
\end{frame}
