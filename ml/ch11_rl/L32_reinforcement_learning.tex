  \small

\begin{frame}{Reward is not a loss function}
  This is the frame that explains why none of chapters 1--10 apply directly.

  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armred!8, colframe=armred, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt,
                    title=\textbf{Three properties a loss does not have}]
    \small
    \begin{enumerate}\itemsep3pt
      \item \textbf{Delayed.} The move that lost you the game was probably not the last one.
            Which of the 200 moves gets the blame? (\emph{credit assignment})
      \item \textbf{Sparse.} Most steps score zero. A robot that has never once reached the
            goal has no signal at all to improve from.
      \item \textbf{Not differentiable.} ``The user preferred this answer.'' ``The code passed
            the tests.'' There is no $\partial \text{reward} / \partial \theta$ to descend.
    \end{enumerate}
  \end{tcolorbox}

  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=paramgreen!8, colframe=paramgreen, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
    \small
    And one property it \emph{does} have that is worse: \textbf{the data depends on the policy.}
    Change how the agent behaves and you change the distribution it learns from. The i.i.d.\
    assumption underneath every previous chapter is gone.
  \end{tcolorbox}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The MDP: five symbols for the whole setting}
  \begin{center}
    {\large $\text{MDP} = (\mathcal{S},\; \mathcal{A},\; P,\; R,\; \gamma)$}
  \end{center}

  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{center}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{c l l}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Symbol} & \textbf{Name} & \textbf{In the gridworld we are about to use} \\
    \midrule
    $\mathcal{S}$ & states & which square you are standing on \\
    $\mathcal{A}$ & actions & up, down, left, right \\
    $P(s'\mid s,a)$ & transition & where you actually end up (you may slip) \\
    $R(s,a)$ & reward & $-0.04$ per move, $+1$ at the goal, $-1$ in the pit \\
    $\gamma$ & discount & how much a future reward is worth now \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \vskip 10pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armblue!8, colframe=armblue, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
    \small
    \textbf{The Markov property} is the assumption hiding in the name: the future depends on
    the \emph{current} state only, not on how you got there. It is what makes any of this
    tractable, and it is frequently a lie in practice.
  \end{tcolorbox}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why discount at all?}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.56\textwidth}
      \vskip 6pt
      \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{discount_horizon.pdf}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.42\textwidth}
      \vskip 10pt
      \small
      $\gamma$ answers one question: \emph{how much is a reward worth if it arrives later?}

      \vskip 6pt
      \begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
        \item $\gamma$ near $0$ -- greedy, near-sighted. Only the next few steps matter.
        \item $\gamma$ near $1$ -- patient. Willing to suffer now for a payoff much later.
      \end{itemize}

      \vskip 8pt
      It is not only a modelling taste: $\gamma < 1$ also keeps the infinite sum finite, which
      is what makes the whole thing well defined.
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The discount is not a detail -- it changes the plan}
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.79\linewidth]{gamma_policy.pdf}
  \end{center}

  \vskip -2pt
  \small
  One corridor, two rewards: $+1$ one step to the left, $+10$ seven steps to the right.
  Same world in all three rows -- only $\gamma$ differs.

  \vskip 3pt
  At $\gamma = 0.5$ the distant $+10$ is worth $0.156$ from the start, less than the nearby
  $+1$: the agent walks \textbf{away} from the bigger prize. The decision flips at
  $\gamma \approx 0.68$.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Our running example}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{gridworld_layout.pdf}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.50\textwidth}
      \vskip 14pt
      \small
      A $4\times4$ world. Start bottom-left, $+1$ top-right, $-1$ just below it, a wall in
      the middle.

      \vskip 6pt
      \textbf{Two details that make it interesting:}
      \begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
        \item Every move costs $-0.04$, so dawdling is punished. Without this the agent would
              be indifferent to taking the scenic route.
        \item Actions are \textbf{unreliable}: you go where you intended with probability
              $0.8$, and slip $90^\circ$ to either side with probability $0.1$ each.
      \end{itemize}

      \vskip 6pt
      That second one is why walking right past the $-1$ is a bad idea even though it is the
      shortest path.
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\section{Value}
% ==================================================================================
\begin{frame}[plain]
  \begin{center}
    \vskip 1.6cm
    {\Huge\bfseries\color{popblue}How good is this position?}

    \vskip 0.6cm
    {\large If you can answer that, the policy follows for free.}
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Two value functions}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armblue!8, colframe=armblue, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt,
                        title=\textbf{State value} $V^\pi(s)$]
        \small
        \[ V^\pi(s) = \E_\pi\!\left[\textstyle\sum_k \gamma^k r_{t+k} \mid s_t = s\right] \]
        \vskip 2pt
        ``Starting here and behaving like $\pi$, what do I collect?''
      \end{tcolorbox}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=paramgreen!8, colframe=paramgreen, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt,
                        title={\textbf{Action value} $Q^\pi(s,a)$}]
        \small
        \[ Q^\pi(s,a) = \E_\pi\!\left[\textstyle\sum_k \gamma^k r_{t+k} \mid s_t = s, a_t = a\right] \]
        \vskip 2pt
        ``\dots if I take $a$ first, and behave like $\pi$ afterwards?''
      \end{tcolorbox}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}

  \vskip 10pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=lightbg, colframe=popblue, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
    \small
    $Q$ is the more useful of the two, and the reason is practical: to \emph{act} from $V$ you
    need to know where each action leads. To act from $Q$ you just compare numbers you already
    have. That single difference is why most algorithms in this lecture learn $Q$.
  \end{tcolorbox}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Bellman equation, in English}
  \begin{center}
    {\large $Q^\pi(s,a) \;=\; \underbrace{R(s,a)}_{\text{now}} \;+\; \gamma
      \underbrace{\textstyle\sum_{s'} P(s'|s,a) \sum_{a'} \pi(a'|s')\, Q^\pi(s',a')}_{\text{everything after}}$}
  \end{center}

  \vskip 10pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=paramgreen!8, colframe=paramgreen, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
    \textbf{Read it aloud:} \emph{the value of doing this here is what you get immediately,
    plus the discounted value of wherever you end up.}
  \end{tcolorbox}

  \vskip 8pt
  \small
  That recursion is the entire foundation. Everything that follows -- value iteration,
  Q-learning, DQN, the critic in actor-critic -- is some way of \textbf{solving or
  approximating this one equation}.

  \vskip 6pt
  The optimal version replaces ``behave like $\pi$ afterwards'' with ``behave optimally
  afterwards'':
  \[ Q^*(s,a) = R(s,a) + \gamma \sum_{s'} P(s'|s,a)\, \max_{a'} Q^*(s',a') \]
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Predict first}
  We are about to solve the gridworld exactly. Before the numbers appear:

  \vskip 10pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armorange!12, colframe=armorange, boxrule=0.8pt, arc=2pt]
    Look at the square \textbf{directly below the $-1$ pit}.

    \vskip 4pt
    The goal $+1$ sits two squares straight up, just past the pit. Does the optimal policy
    there point \textbf{up} -- the short way, squeezing past the pit -- or does it point
    \textbf{away}?
  \end{tcolorbox}

  \vskip 12pt
  \small
  Commit to an answer, and to a reason. Remember that actions slip sideways one time in five.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Solved, exactly}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.47\textwidth}
      \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{gridworld_values.pdf}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.51\textwidth}
      \vskip 16pt
      \small
      Value iteration: apply the Bellman update to every square, repeatedly, until nothing
      changes. Converged here in \textbf{23 sweeps}.

      \vskip 8pt
      \begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
        \item $V^*(\text{start}) = 0.2075$ -- positive, but modest: the goal is far and every
              step costs.
        \item The square beside the goal is worth $0.7954$.
        \item Notice the value \emph{gradient} pointing away from the pit. Squares near it are
              worth less even when they are close to the goal.
      \end{itemize}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{And the policy falls out of it}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.47\textwidth}
      \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{gridworld_policy.pdf}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.51\textwidth}
      \vskip 10pt
      \small
      $\pi^*(s) = \arg\max_a Q^*(s,a)$ -- once you know the values, choosing is trivial.

      \vskip 8pt
      \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armorange!12, colframe=armorange, boxrule=0.7pt, arc=2pt]
        \small
        \textbf{The answer to the prediction:} the square below the pit points
        \textbf{left}, not up.

        \vskip 4pt
        Going up means walking alongside the pit, and a $10\%$ slip lands you in it and ends
        the episode at $-1$. The long way round costs a few more $-0.04$ steps and is
        \emph{still} worth more.
      \end{tcolorbox}

      \vskip 6pt
      The agent is not avoiding the pit. It is avoiding \emph{the chance} of the pit.
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

% ==================================================================================

    \vskip 0.6cm
    {\large Value iteration needed the rules of the world. Real agents do not get them.}

\begin{frame}{What we just did was cheating}
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tikzpicture}
    \node[draw=popblue, fill=popblue!8, rounded corners=6pt, text width=5.6cm, align=center,
          inner sep=7pt] at (-3.3, 1.2) {
      {\small\bfseries\color{popblue}Model-based}\\[4pt]
      {\small You know $P(s'|s,a)$ and $R$.\\ Plan by computing.\\[3pt]
      \emph{Value iteration, AlphaGo's search}}
    };
    \node[draw=paramgreen, fill=paramgreen!8, rounded corners=6pt, text width=5.6cm,
          align=center, inner sep=7pt] at (3.3, 1.2) {
      {\small\bfseries\color{paramgreen}Model-free}\\[4pt]
      {\small You know nothing.\\ Learn by trying.\\[3pt]
      \emph{Q-learning, DQN, PPO}}
    };
    \node[draw=armred, fill=armred!8, rounded corners=6pt, text width=11.8cm, align=center,
          inner sep=6pt] at (0, -1.3) {
      {\small A robot does not come with a probability table for how its wheels slip on this
      particular carpet. A chatbot has no model of how a human will react. \textbf{Model-free
      is the normal case.}}
    };
  \end{tikzpicture}
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Q-learning: learn the values from experience alone}
  \begin{center}
    {\large $Q(s,a) \leftarrow Q(s,a) + \alpha \Big[\underbrace{r + \gamma \max_{a'} Q(s',a')}_{\text{what I now think it is worth}} - \underbrace{Q(s,a)}_{\text{what I thought before}}\Big]$}
  \end{center}

  \vskip 10pt
  \small
  The bracket is a \textbf{surprise}: the gap between the estimate you had and the better
  estimate you can make now that you have seen $r$ and $s'$. Nudge towards it by $\alpha$.

  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.31\textwidth}
      \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=paramgreen!8, colframe=paramgreen, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
        \scriptsize\textbf{Model-free}\\ Never needs $P$. Just $(s,a,r,s')$.
      \end{tcolorbox}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.31\textwidth}
      \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armorange!12, colframe=armorange, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
        \scriptsize\textbf{Off-policy}\\ Learns $Q^*$ even while behaving badly.
      \end{tcolorbox}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.31\textwidth}
      \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armred!8, colframe=armred, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
        \scriptsize\textbf{Tabular}\\ One cell per $(s,a)$. Does not scale.
      \end{tcolorbox}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}

  \vskip 8pt
  \scriptsize\color{gray}
  Watkins \& Dayan (1992). Converges to $Q^*$ given decaying $\alpha$ and infinitely many
  visits to every pair -- conditions no real system satisfies, and it usually works anyway.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{One update, by hand}
  Suppose $\alpha = 0.5$, $\gamma = 0.9$. The agent is on a square where it currently believes
  $Q(s, \text{right}) = 0.30$. It moves right, receives the usual $-0.04$, and lands on a
  square whose best action it currently values at $0.60$.

  \vskip 10pt
  \begin{center}
  \small
  $\begin{aligned}
    \text{target} \;&=\; r + \gamma \max_{a'} Q(s',a') \;=\; -0.04 + 0.9 \times 0.60 \;=\; \mathbf{0.50} \\[4pt]
    \text{surprise} \;&=\; 0.50 - 0.30 \;=\; +0.20 \\[4pt]
    Q(s,\text{right}) \;&\leftarrow\; 0.30 + 0.5 \times 0.20 \;=\; \mathbf{0.40}
  \end{aligned}$
  \end{center}

  \vskip 10pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armblue!8, colframe=armblue, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
    \small
    That is the whole algorithm. Everything else in tabular RL is bookkeeping around this line
    -- and notice it never once asked where the agent was likely to end up. It only used where
    it \emph{did} end up.
  \end{tcolorbox}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The dilemma nobody escapes}
  To learn which action is best you must try actions that might not be. To score well you must
  take the action you already believe is best. You cannot do both at once.

  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.52\textwidth}
      \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{epsilon_cost.pdf}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.46\textwidth}
      \vskip 8pt
      \small
      \textbf{$\epsilon$-greedy:} act randomly with probability $\epsilon$, greedily otherwise.

      \vskip 4pt
      Priced exactly on our gridworld, with the optimal policy already known:
      \begin{itemize}\itemsep1pt
        \item $\epsilon = 0$: value $0.208$
        \item $\epsilon = 0.1$: value $0.152$
        \item $\epsilon = 1$: value $-0.418$
      \end{itemize}

      \vskip 4pt
      Even $10\%$ random acting costs about a quarter of the achievable value.
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}

  \vskip 2pt
  \footnotesize
  \textbf{But that is only one side.} The figure assumes you \emph{already know} the best
  policy. At the start you do not, and $\epsilon = 0$ means never finding it. Hence the usual
  compromise: start high, decay over time.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{On-policy or off-policy?}
  \begin{center}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{p{4.2cm} p{4.6cm} p{4.6cm}}
    \toprule
     & \textbf{Q-learning (off-policy)} & \textbf{SARSA (on-policy)} \\
    \midrule
    Target uses & $\max_{a'} Q(s',a')$ & $Q(s', a')$ for the $a'$ actually taken \\[4pt]
    It learns the value of & the \emph{best} policy & the policy it is \emph{actually running},
      exploration and all \\[4pt]
    Near a cliff it will & walk the edge, because the optimal path is along the edge \&
      & keep a margin, because it knows it sometimes slips \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \vskip 10pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armorange!12, colframe=armorange, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
    \small
    This is not a small distinction. If your agent's exploration can cause real damage -- a
    physical robot, a live recommender -- the on-policy answer is often the one you want,
    because it accounts for the fact that the agent \emph{will} sometimes do something random.
  \end{tcolorbox}
\end{frame}

\section{When the table will not fit}
% ==================================================================================
\begin{frame}[plain]
  \begin{center}
    \vskip 1.6cm
    {\Huge\bfseries\color{popblue}Atari has more states than the universe has atoms}

    \vskip 0.6cm
    {\large So stop storing them, and start predicting them.}
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The wall}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.47\textwidth}
      \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armred!8, colframe=armred, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt,
                        title=\textbf{Tabular Q-learning stops here}]
        \small
        One Atari frame: $210 \times 160$ pixels, $128$ colours.

        \vskip 4pt
        \[ |\mathcal{S}| \approx 128^{33{,}600} \]
        \vskip 2pt
        There is no table. There will never be a table.
      \end{tcolorbox}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.49\textwidth}
      \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=paramgreen!8, colframe=paramgreen, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt,
                        title=\textbf{The move you have made before}]
        \small
        Replace the lookup table with a \textbf{function}:
        \[ Q(s,a) \;\longrightarrow\; Q_\theta(s,a) \]
        \vskip 2pt
        A network that has never seen this exact screen can still evaluate it, because it
        generalises from similar ones.
      \end{tcolorbox}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}

  \vskip 10pt
  \small
  This is the same step as everywhere else in the course: stop memorising, start fitting. The
  loss is just the squared surprise from two slides ago:
  \[ \mathcal{L}(\theta) = \E\Big[\big(r + \gamma \max_{a'} Q_{\theta^-}(s',a') - Q_\theta(s,a)\big)^2\Big] \]
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{DQN, and the two tricks that made it work}
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armblue!8, colframe=armblue, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt,
                    title=\textbf{Experience replay}]
    \small
    Store transitions $(s,a,r,s')$ in a buffer and train on \emph{random} batches from it.

    \vskip 3pt
    \textbf{Why:} consecutive frames are almost identical, so training on them in order
    violates the i.i.d.\ assumption badly and the network chases itself. Shuffling restores it.
  \end{tcolorbox}

  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=paramgreen!8, colframe=paramgreen, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt,
                    title=\textbf{Target network}]
    \small
    Compute the target with a \emph{frozen} copy $Q_{\theta^-}$, updated only occasionally.

    \vskip 3pt
    \textbf{Why:} otherwise you are regressing towards a target that moves every time you take
    a step -- a dog chasing its own tail. Freezing it makes the problem locally supervised.
  \end{tcolorbox}

  \vskip 8pt
  \small
  With these, one architecture learned $49$ Atari games from raw pixels, beating a human on
  $29$ of them (Mnih et al., \emph{Nature} 2015). Same network, same hyperparameters, no
  game-specific engineering -- which was the actual shock.
\end{frame}

% ==================================================================================
\section{Optimising the policy directly}
% ==================================================================================
\begin{frame}[plain]
  \begin{center}
    \vskip 1.6cm
    {\Huge\bfseries\color{popblue}Skip the values. Learn the behaviour.}

    \vskip 0.6cm
    {\large The road that leads to every algorithm used on language models.}
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Two ways to be an agent}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armblue!8, colframe=armblue, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt,
                        title=\textbf{Value-based}]
        \small
        Learn $Q(s,a)$, then act greedily.

        \vskip 4pt
        \textbf{Good:} sample-efficient, off-policy replay.

        \vskip 2pt
        \textbf{Bad:} needs $\arg\max_a$, so actions must be few and discrete. Try that with a
        robot arm's continuous torques.
      \end{tcolorbox}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=paramgreen!8, colframe=paramgreen, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt,
                        title=\textbf{Policy-based}]
        \small
        Learn $\pi_\theta(a|s)$ directly and improve it.

        \vskip 4pt
        \textbf{Good:} continuous actions, naturally stochastic, optimises what you care about.

        \vskip 2pt
        \textbf{Bad:} high variance, and normally on-policy -- yesterday's data is stale.
      \end{tcolorbox}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}

  \vskip 10pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=lightbg, colframe=popblue, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
    \small
    For a language model the action space is \emph{the whole vocabulary at every token}. That
    alone decides the question -- which is why the LLM world is policy-based, and why the rest
    of this lecture points that way.
  \end{tcolorbox}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The policy gradient}
  \begin{center}
    {\large $\nabla_\theta J(\theta) \;=\; \E_{\pi_\theta}\big[\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a|s) \cdot G_t\big]$}
  \end{center}

  \vskip 10pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=paramgreen!8, colframe=paramgreen, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
    \textbf{In words:} take the actions you actually played, and adjust their probabilities in
    proportion to how well things went afterwards. Good outcome -- make that action more
    likely. Bad outcome -- less likely.
  \end{tcolorbox}

  \vskip 10pt
  \small
  Two things worth pausing on:

  \begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
    \item $\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a|s)$ is a gradient of the \emph{policy}, which we
          control -- not of the reward, which we cannot differentiate. That is the trick that
          makes non-differentiable rewards usable.
    \item $G_t$ is just a number that scales it. It can come from anywhere: a game score, a
          unit test, a human's preference.
  \end{itemize}

  \vskip 6pt
  \textbf{REINFORCE} is this formula used literally: play an episode, compute the returns,
  take the step.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{REINFORCE has a variance problem}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.55\textwidth}
      \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{reinforce_variance.pdf}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.43\textwidth}
      \vskip 6pt
      \small
      Two actions, one state. Action A returns about $11$, action B about $9$ -- so A is
      better and the gradient \emph{should} point towards A.

      \vskip 4pt
      Batches of $20$ episodes, repeated $4000$ times:

      \vskip 3pt
      \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armred!8, colframe=armred, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
        \scriptsize
        Plain REINFORCE points the \textbf{wrong way $33.9\%$} of the time. Sd $1.13$.
      \end{tcolorbox}

      \vskip 3pt
      \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=paramgreen!8, colframe=paramgreen, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
        \scriptsize
        With a baseline of $10$: wrong way \textbf{$0.0\%$}, sd $0.11$ -- a $10\times$
        reduction, \emph{same} average.
      \end{tcolorbox}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}

  \vskip 2pt
  \footnotesize
  \textbf{Why it works:} both returns are positive, so plain REINFORCE pushes \emph{both}
  actions up and only the sizes differ. Centring on the average makes the better action
  positive and the worse one negative -- the signal stops fighting itself.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Advantage, and the actor-critic}
  \begin{center}
    {\large $A(s,a) = Q(s,a) - V(s)$}
  \end{center}

  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{center}
    \small \emph{``Was this action better or worse than what I normally do here?''}
  \end{center}

  \vskip 10pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armblue!8, colframe=armblue, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt,
                        title=\textbf{Actor}]
        \small The policy $\pi_\theta$. Chooses what to do.
      \end{tcolorbox}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=paramgreen!8, colframe=paramgreen, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt,
                        title=\textbf{Critic}]
        \small An estimate of $V(s)$. Provides the baseline.
      \end{tcolorbox}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}

  \vskip 10pt
  \small
  The baseline from the previous slide, learned instead of assumed. It trades a little bias for
  a lot of variance -- and it is the reason a value function reappears inside methods whose
  whole point was to avoid one.

  \vskip 6pt
  \textbf{Remember the critic.} When you meet GRPO in the LLM chapter, its entire selling point
  is \emph{throwing the critic away} and replacing it with the average of a group of samples.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{PPO: do not take a step you cannot walk back}
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.82\linewidth]{ppo_clip.pdf}
  \end{center}

  \vskip -4pt
  \small
  Vanilla policy gradient has no notion of ``too far''. One oversized update and the policy
  collapses -- and unlike supervised learning you cannot simply reload, because the collapsed
  policy now \emph{collects the data} for the next update.

  \vskip 4pt
  PPO clips the objective so that once the new policy differs from the old by more than
  $\epsilon$ (typically $0.2$), moving further buys \textbf{nothing}. The gradient in that
  region is flat by construction.
\end{frame}

\section{The payoff}
% ==================================================================================
\begin{frame}[plain]
  \begin{center}
    \vskip 1.6cm
    {\Huge\bfseries\color{popblue}A language model is just another agent}

    \vskip 0.6cm
    {\large Everything above, applied to text.}
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Text generation is an MDP}
  \begin{center}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{l l}
    \toprule
    \textbf{RL} & \textbf{Language model} \\
    \midrule
    state $s_t$ & the prompt plus every token generated so far \\
    action $a_t$ & the next token (action space $=$ the vocabulary, $\sim$$100$k) \\
    policy $\pi_\theta(a|s)$ & \textbf{the model itself} -- the softmax over next tokens \\
    reward $r_t$ & almost always $0$ \dots until the response ends \\
    episode & one complete response \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \vskip 10pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armblue!8, colframe=armblue, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
    \small
    Look at the reward row. A single scalar at the very end, for a sequence of hundreds of
    actions -- the credit-assignment problem from the third slide of this lecture, in its most
    extreme form.
  \end{tcolorbox}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{RLHF in three stages}
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tikzpicture}[node distance=0.35cm]
    \node[draw=popblue, fill=popblue!8, rounded corners=6pt, text width=3.5cm, align=center,
          inner sep=6pt] (a) at (-4.3, 0) {
      {\small\bfseries\color{popblue}1. SFT}\\[3pt]
      {\scriptsize Fine-tune on human-written ideal answers. Ordinary supervised learning.}
    };
    \node[draw=orange1, fill=orange1!8, rounded corners=6pt, text width=3.5cm, align=center,
          inner sep=6pt] (b) at (0, 0) {
      {\small\bfseries\color{orange1}2. Reward model}\\[3pt]
      {\scriptsize Humans \emph{rank} pairs of answers. Train a model to predict the ranking.}
    };
    \node[draw=paramgreen, fill=paramgreen!8, rounded corners=6pt, text width=3.5cm,
          align=center, inner sep=6pt] (c) at (4.3, 0) {
      {\small\bfseries\color{paramgreen}3. PPO}\\[3pt]
      {\scriptsize Optimise the policy against that reward, with a KL penalty to the SFT model.}
    };
    \draw[-stealth, thick] (a) -- (b);
    \draw[-stealth, thick] (b) -- (c);
  \end{tikzpicture}
  \end{center}

  \vskip 8pt
  \small
  Stage 2 exists because ``helpful'' is not differentiable, but a \emph{comparison} can be
  learned. Stage 3 is exactly the PPO from two slides ago.

  \vskip 6pt
  The KL penalty is the give-away that something is fragile: it holds the policy near the model
  we started from, because optimising the reward too hard is actively dangerous. Which is the
  next slide.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Reward hacking: the agent does what you measured}
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armred!8, colframe=armred, boxrule=0.8pt, arc=2pt,
                    title=\textbf{Goodhart's law}]
    When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.
  \end{tcolorbox}

  \vskip 6pt
  \footnotesize
  The reward model is a \emph{learned approximation} of human preference, fitted on finite
  data. Optimise against it hard enough and the policy will find the places where the
  approximation is wrong, because those are where the reward is highest.

  \vskip 5pt
  Observed failure modes, all real:
  \begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
    \item answers get longer and longer, because raters mildly preferred longer answers
    \item excessive hedging and flattery, because raters rewarded politeness
    \item confident agreement with the user, because disagreement scored badly
  \end{itemize}

  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=paramgreen!8, colframe=paramgreen, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
    \small
    None of these are bugs in the algorithm. The optimiser did its job perfectly. The
    \textbf{objective} was wrong -- and this is the single most important idea to carry out of
    this lecture.
  \end{tcolorbox}
\end{frame}

\section{The bitter lesson}
% ==================================================================================
\begin{frame}[plain]
  \begin{center}
    \vskip 1.6cm
    {\Huge\bfseries\color{popblue}One more thing, and it stings}

    \vskip 0.6cm
    {\large Written by Rich Sutton -- who also wrote the RL textbook.}
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The bitter lesson (Sutton, 2019)}
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armblue!8, colframe=armblue, boxrule=0.8pt, arc=2pt]
    \small
    ``The biggest lesson that can be read from 70 years of AI research is that \textbf{general
    methods that leverage computation are ultimately the most effective}, and by a large
    margin.''
  \end{tcolorbox}

  \vskip 6pt
  \footnotesize
  The pattern he documents, over and over: researchers encode human knowledge about the
  problem; it helps at first; then it is beaten by a simpler method that just \emph{searches}
  or \emph{learns} more, once the hardware allows.

  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{center}
  \scriptsize
  \begin{tabular}{l l l}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Domain} & \textbf{The knowledge-engineered approach} & \textbf{What beat it} \\
    \midrule
    Chess & hand-crafted positional heuristics & massive search (Deep Blue) \\
    Go & shape and pattern libraries & search + self-play RL (AlphaGo) \\
    Speech & phonetic models, linguistic rules & statistical, then deep, learning \\
    Vision & hand-designed feature detectors & learned features (CNNs) \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armred!8, colframe=armred, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
    \footnotesize
    It is \emph{bitter} because it says the satisfying part -- our insight into the problem --
    is usually not what wins. Sutton's point is that we keep re-learning this and keep
    resisting it.
  \end{tcolorbox}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Where this lecture is Exhibit A -- and where it is not}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.49\textwidth}
      \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=paramgreen!8, colframe=paramgreen, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt,
                        title=\textbf{Evidence for}]
        \scriptsize
        \begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
          \item \textbf{AlphaGo $\to$ AlphaGo Zero.} Deleting the human game database made it
                \emph{stronger}. Sutton's own example.
          \item \textbf{DQN.} One architecture, one set of hyperparameters, 49 Atari games.
          \item \textbf{DeepSeek-R1.} Nobody taught it to reason; the behaviour emerged from RL
                against a checkable reward.
        \end{itemize}
      \end{tcolorbox}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.49\textwidth}
      \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armorange!12, colframe=armorange, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt,
                        title=\textbf{Read it carefully, though}]
        \scriptsize
        \begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
          \item It is \emph{not} ``structure never helps''. Convolution and attention are
                human-designed inductive biases, and both were decisive.
          \item Every method here is scaffolding: replay, target networks, clipping, KL
                penalties. Someone designed those.
          \item Compute is not free. ``Wait for better hardware'' is a strategy open to a few
                labs and nobody else.
        \end{itemize}
      \end{tcolorbox}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}

  \vskip 5pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=lightbg, colframe=popblue, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
    \scriptsize
    The honest reading: prefer methods that \textbf{improve when given more compute and data},
    and be suspicious of cleverness that does not. A claim about what to bet on -- not a licence
    to stop thinking.
  \end{tcolorbox}
\end{frame}

\section*{Recap}
% ==================================================================================
\begin{frame}{Recap}
  \scriptsize
  \begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
    \item RL is for problems with \textbf{no correct answer to imitate} -- only delayed, sparse,
          non-differentiable consequences. The i.i.d.\ assumption is gone.
    \item An \textbf{MDP} $(\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{A}, P, R, \gamma)$ is the whole setting, and
          $\gamma$ is a real modelling choice: it changed which reward our corridor agent walked
          towards.
    \item The \textbf{Bellman equation} is the one recursion everything solves or approximates.
          Solve it exactly with a model; \textbf{Q-learning} approximates it from experience
          without one. Tabular hits a wall, so $Q$ becomes a \textbf{network} (DQN), with replay
          and a frozen target.
    \item \textbf{Policy gradients} optimise behaviour directly -- necessary when the action
          space is a vocabulary. They are noisy: a baseline cut our wrong-direction rate from
          $33.9\%$ to $0\%$. \textbf{PPO} then stops any update going too far.
    \item Optimising a \emph{learned} reward invites \textbf{reward hacking}. The optimiser is
          not the problem; the objective is.
    \item \textbf{The bitter lesson:} bet on methods that improve with more compute and data.
          AlphaGo Zero got stronger by \emph{deleting} the human games.
  \end{itemize}

  \vskip 4pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=paramgreen!8, colframe=paramgreen, boxrule=0.8pt, arc=2pt]
    \footnotesize
    \textbf{Next:} the \emph{LLM Training \& Alignment} chapter -- this lecture at scale. You can
    now read [10] InstructGPT (the pipeline above), [01] GRPO (drop the critic), [02] DPO (drop
    the reward model \emph{and} the RL), and [11] DeepSeek-R1 (reasoning from RL alone).
  \end{tcolorbox}
\end{frame}
