    \end{center}

  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armorange!12, colframe=armorange, boxrule=0.7pt, arc=2pt]
    \small
    \textbf{So why not optimise the behaviour itself?} Write the policy as $\pi_\theta(a|s)$,
    define $J(\theta)$ as the expected return, and do gradient ascent on it like every other
    model in this course.

    \vskip 4pt

    on the parameters you are differentiating}. This lecture removes that obstacle and then
    spends the rest of its time making the result usable.
  \end{tcolorbox}

\section{Why bother}
% ==================================================================================
\begin{frame}[plain]
  \begin{center}
    \vskip 1.6cm
    {\Huge\bfseries\color{popblue}Learn the behaviour, not the scoreboard}

    \vskip 0.6cm
    {\large Three things $\argmax_a Q$ simply cannot do.}
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Two ways to be an agent}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armblue!8, colframe=armblue, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt,
                        title={\textbf{Value-based}}]
        \small
        Learn $Q(s,a)$, then act greedily.

        \vskip 4pt
        \textbf{Good:} sample-efficient, off-policy replay.

        \vskip 2pt
        \textbf{Bad:} needs $\argmax_a$, so actions must be few and discrete. Try that with a
        robot arm's continuous torques.
      \end{tcolorbox}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=paramgreen!8, colframe=paramgreen, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt,
                        title={\textbf{Policy-based}}]
        \small
        Learn $\pi_\theta(a|s)$ directly and improve it.

        \vskip 4pt
        \textbf{Good:} continuous actions, naturally stochastic, optimises what you care about.

        \vskip 2pt
        \textbf{Bad:} high variance, and normally on-policy -- yesterday's data is stale.
      \end{tcolorbox}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}

  \vskip 8pt
  \small
  Three concrete failures of the value-based route:

  \begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
    \item \textbf{Continuous actions.} $\argmax_a$ over a real-valued torque is its own
          optimisation problem, at every single step.
    \item \textbf{Stochastic optima.} In rock-paper-scissors the best policy is uniformly
          random. A greedy policy cannot represent that.
    \item \textbf{Huge action spaces.} A language model's action space is the entire vocabulary
          at every token.
  \end{itemize}

  \vskip 4pt
  \begin{center}
    \small
    That last one decides the question for the rest of this chapter.
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\section{The policy gradient theorem}
% ==================================================================================
\begin{frame}[plain]
  \begin{center}
    \vskip 1.6cm
    {\Huge\bfseries\color{popblue}Differentiating an expectation\\ you cannot differentiate}

    \vskip 0.6cm
    {\large Six lines that make the whole family possible.}
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The obstacle, stated precisely}
  \small
  We want to maximise the expected return over trajectories the policy itself generates:
  \[ J(\theta) = \E_{\tau \sim \pi_\theta}\big[ R(\tau) \big] = \int P(\tau;\theta)\, R(\tau)\, d\tau \]

  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armred!8, colframe=armred, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt,
                        title={\textbf{Why the usual move fails}}]
        \small
        In supervised learning the data is fixed and only the prediction depends on $\theta$.

        \vskip 4pt
        Here $\theta$ changes \textbf{which trajectories you see}. The distribution is the thing
        being optimised.
      \end{tcolorbox}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armorange!12, colframe=armorange, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt,
                        title={\textbf{And $R(\tau)$ is a black box}}]
        \small
        The reward may be a game score, a unit test, or a human's opinion. There is no
        $\nabla_\theta R$. There may not even be a formula.
      \end{tcolorbox}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}

  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{center}
    \small
    So: differentiate the \emph{probability}, never the reward.
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The log-derivative trick}
  \small
  One identity does all the work: $\;\nabla_\theta P = P \,\nabla_\theta \log P$
  (just the chain rule on $\log$, rearranged).

  \vskip 3pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=lightbg, colframe=popblue, boxrule=0.7pt, arc=2pt]
    \footnotesize
    \begin{align*}
      \nabla_\theta J(\theta) &= \nabla_\theta \int P(\tau;\theta) R(\tau)\, d\tau
        && \text{\scriptsize $R$ does not depend on $\theta$} \\[2pt]
      &= \int \nabla_\theta P(\tau;\theta)\, R(\tau)\, d\tau
        && \text{\scriptsize move the gradient inside} \\[2pt]
      &= \int P(\tau;\theta)\, \nabla_\theta \log P(\tau;\theta)\, R(\tau)\, d\tau
        && \text{\scriptsize the identity above} \\[2pt]
      &= \E_{\tau \sim \pi_\theta}\big[\, \nabla_\theta \log P(\tau;\theta)\, R(\tau) \,\big]
        && \text{\scriptsize \textbf{an expectation again -- so we can sample it}}
    \end{align*}
  \end{tcolorbox}

  \vskip 2pt
  The trajectory probability factorises, so the dynamics drop out (\textbf{no model needed}),
  just as they did for importance sampling:

  \vskip 1pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=paramgreen!8, colframe=paramgreen, boxrule=0.7pt, arc=2pt]
    \scriptsize
    \vskip -6pt
    \[ \log P(\tau;\theta) = \cancel{\log p(s_0)} + \sum_t \Big[ \log \pi_\theta(a_t|s_t) + \cancel{\log P(s_{t+1}|s_t,a_t)} \Big]
       \;\Longrightarrow\; \nabla_\theta \log P = \sum_t \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t|s_t) \]
    \vskip -10pt
  \end{tcolorbox}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What it says, and REINFORCE}
  \begin{center}
    {\large $\nabla_\theta J(\theta) \;=\; \E_{\pi_\theta}\big[\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t|s_t) \cdot G_t\big]$}
  \end{center}

  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=paramgreen!8, colframe=paramgreen, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
    \textbf{In words:} take the actions you actually played, and adjust their probabilities in
    proportion to how well things went afterwards. Good outcome -- make that action more
    likely. Bad outcome -- less likely.
  \end{tcolorbox}

  \vskip 8pt
  \small
  \textbf{REINFORCE} (Williams, 1992) is this used literally:

  \vskip 3pt
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=lightbg, colframe=popblue, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt, width=0.8\textwidth]
    \footnotesize
    \begin{enumerate}\itemsep1pt
      \item Run an episode with $\pi_\theta$, storing every $(s_t, a_t, r_t)$.
      \item Compute the return $G_t$ from each step onwards.
      \item $\theta \leftarrow \theta + \alpha \sum_t G_t \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t|s_t)$.
    \end{enumerate}
  \end{tcolorbox}
  \end{center}

  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{center}
    \small
    Three lines, no model, no $\argmax$, works with any reward you can compute.
    \textbf{And in this raw form it barely works.}
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{REINFORCE has a variance problem}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.55\textwidth}
      \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{reinforce_variance.pdf}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.43\textwidth}
      \vskip 6pt
      \small
      Two actions, one state. Action A returns about $11$, action B about $9$ -- so A is
      better and the gradient \emph{should} point towards A.

      \vskip 4pt
      Batches of $20$ episodes, repeated $4000$ times:

      \vskip 3pt
      \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armred!8, colframe=armred, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
        \scriptsize
        Plain REINFORCE points the \textbf{wrong way $33.9\%$} of the time. Sd $1.13$.
      \end{tcolorbox}

      \vskip 3pt
      \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=paramgreen!8, colframe=paramgreen, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
        \scriptsize
        With a baseline of $10$: wrong way \textbf{$0.0\%$}, sd $0.11$ -- a $10\times$
        reduction, \emph{same} average.
      \end{tcolorbox}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}

  \vskip 2pt
  \footnotesize
  \textbf{Why it works:} both returns are positive, so plain REINFORCE pushes \emph{both}
  actions up and only the sizes differ. Centring on the average makes the better action
  positive and the worse one negative -- the signal stops fighting itself.
\end{frame}

\section{Making the gradient usable}
% ==================================================================================
\begin{frame}[plain]
  \begin{center}
    \vskip 1.6cm
    {\Huge\bfseries\color{popblue}Same gradient, less noise}

    \vskip 0.6cm
    {\large Baselines, advantages, and a dial you have already met.}
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why subtracting a baseline is free}
  \small
  Replace $G_t$ with $G_t - b(s_t)$ for any function $b$ that does not depend on the action.
  The claim is that this changes the variance but \textbf{not} the expected gradient:

  \vskip 4pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=lightbg, colframe=popblue, boxrule=0.7pt, arc=2pt]
    \footnotesize
    \begin{align*}
      \E_{a \sim \pi}\big[ \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a|s)\, b(s) \big]
        &= b(s) \sum_a \pi_\theta(a|s)\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a|s)
        && \text{\scriptsize $b(s)$ is a constant w.r.t.\ $a$} \\[2pt]
        &= b(s) \sum_a \nabla_\theta \pi_\theta(a|s)
        && \text{\scriptsize the identity, backwards} \\[2pt]
        &= b(s)\, \nabla_\theta \sum_a \pi_\theta(a|s)
        && \text{\scriptsize swap sum and gradient} \\[2pt]
        &= b(s)\, \nabla_\theta 1 \;=\; \mathbf{0}
        && \text{\scriptsize probabilities sum to one}
    \end{align*}
  \end{tcolorbox}

  \vskip 4pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=paramgreen!8, colframe=paramgreen, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
    \small
    \textbf{A free lunch, and they are rare.} You may subtract \emph{anything} that does not
    depend on the action and the gradient stays correct in expectation. The best choice is the
    one that shrinks the variance most, which is roughly $b(s) = V(s)$.

    \vskip 3pt
    \textbf{Remember this proof.} It is exactly why GRPO is allowed to use the mean reward of a
    group of answers as its baseline -- lecture 7.
  \end{tcolorbox}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Advantage, and the actor-critic}
  \begin{center}
    {\large $A(s,a) = Q(s,a) - V(s)$}
  \end{center}

  \vskip 4pt
  \begin{center}
    \small \emph{``Was this action better or worse than what I normally do here?''}
  \end{center}

  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armblue!8, colframe=armblue, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt,
                        title={\textbf{Actor}}]
        \small The policy $\pi_\theta$. Chooses what to do. Trained by the policy gradient.
      \end{tcolorbox}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=paramgreen!8, colframe=paramgreen, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt,
                        title={\textbf{Critic}}]
        \small An estimate of $V_\phi(s)$. Provides the baseline. Trained by regression on
        returns.
      \end{tcolorbox}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}

  \vskip 8pt
  \small
  The baseline from the previous slide, \emph{learned} instead of assumed. It accepts a little
  bias in exchange for a large drop in variance -- and it is the reason a value function
  reappears inside methods whose whole point was to avoid one.

  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armorange!12, colframe=armorange, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
    \small
    \textbf{Remember the critic.} It is a second full-sized network. For a 70B language model
    that is 70B extra parameters to hold and train -- and when you meet GRPO in lecture 7, its
    entire selling point is \textbf{throwing this away}.
  \end{tcolorbox}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{GAE: the dial from lecture 2, again}
  \small
  How should the critic estimate the advantage? The same bias-variance dial as $n$-step returns:

  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{center}
  \footnotesize
  \begin{tabular}{l l l}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Estimator} & \textbf{Bias} & \textbf{Variance} \\
    \midrule
    $\hat{A} = r_t + \gamma V(s_{t+1}) - V(s_t)$ \; (one step) & high (leans on $V$) & low \\
    $\hat{A} = G_t - V(s_t)$ \; (full return) & none & high \\
    \textbf{GAE}$(\lambda)$: exponentially weighted average of all $n$-step versions & tunable & tunable \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armblue!8, colframe=armblue, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt,
                    title={\textbf{Generalised Advantage Estimation} (Schulman et al., 2015)}]
    \small
    \[ \hat{A}^{\text{GAE}}_t = \sum_{l \ge 0} (\gamma\lambda)^l\, \delta_{t+l},
       \qquad \delta_t = r_t + \gamma V(s_{t+1}) - V(s_t) \]
    \vskip 2pt
    $\lambda = 0$ gives the one-step version; $\lambda = 1$ gives the full return. In practice
    $\lambda \approx 0.95$, and it is one of the few hyperparameters genuinely worth tuning.
  \end{tcolorbox}

  \vskip 4pt
  \begin{center}
    \small
    Same idea as TD($\lambda$), applied to advantages instead of values.
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\section{Trust regions and PPO}
% ==================================================================================
\begin{frame}[plain]
  \begin{center}
    \vskip 1.6cm
    {\Huge\bfseries\color{popblue}Do not take a step\\ you cannot walk back}

    \vskip 0.6cm
    {\large The single most important idea for everything that follows.}
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why one bad step is fatal here and not in chapter 5}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armblue!8, colframe=armblue, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt,
                        title={\textbf{Supervised learning}}]
        \small
        Too large a learning rate ruins the model.

        \vskip 4pt
        You lower it, reload the checkpoint, and rerun. \textbf{The dataset is still there.}
      \end{tcolorbox}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armred!8, colframe=armred, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt,
                        title={\textbf{Reinforcement learning}}]
        \small
        Too large a step ruins the policy.

        \vskip 4pt
        The ruined policy now \textbf{collects the next batch of data}. It explores badly, so it
        learns badly, so it explores worse.
      \end{tcolorbox}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}

  \vskip 10pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armorange!12, colframe=armorange, boxrule=0.7pt, arc=2pt]
    \small
    This is the feedback loop that makes RL training feel unstable in a way supervised training
    does not. There is no fixed dataset to fall back on: \textbf{the data supply is downstream
    of the model's quality.}

    \vskip 4pt
    So the goal is not just ``take a good step''. It is ``take the biggest step you can while
    being sure you have not broken the thing that feeds you''.
  \end{tcolorbox}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The surrogate objective, and a familiar ratio}
  \small
  There is a second problem, and the fix for it turns out to fix the first. After one gradient
  step the data you collected is \emph{off-policy} -- it came from $\pi_{\text{old}}$. Lecture 2
  told us what to do about that:

  \vskip 4pt
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=lightbg, colframe=popblue, boxrule=0.7pt, arc=2pt, width=0.86\textwidth]
    \[ L(\theta) = \E\Big[\, \underbrace{\frac{\pi_\theta(a|s)}{\pi_{\text{old}}(a|s)}}_{r_t(\theta)}\; \hat{A}_t \Big] \]
    \vskip -4pt
    \begin{center}\small
      the importance ratio, cut down from a whole-trajectory product to \textbf{one step}
    \end{center}
  \end{tcolorbox}
  \end{center}

  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
    \item At $\theta = \theta_{\text{old}}$ the ratio is $1$ and this has the same gradient as
          plain policy gradient. So it is a legitimate stand-in.
    \item Away from $\theta_{\text{old}}$ it corrects for the mismatch -- which means you can
          take \textbf{several} gradient steps on one batch of data instead of throwing it away.
  \end{itemize}

  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armred!8, colframe=armred, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
    \small
    \textbf{And there is the trap.} Nothing stops the optimiser making $r_t(\theta)$ enormous
    for one lucky action with a big advantage. The correction is only valid while the two
    policies are close, and the objective actively rewards making them far apart.
  \end{tcolorbox}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{TRPO: put a fence around it}
  \small
  Trust Region Policy Optimization (Schulman et al., 2015) states the obvious fix as a
  constrained problem:

  \vskip 4pt
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armblue!8, colframe=armblue, boxrule=0.7pt, arc=2pt, width=0.8\textwidth]
    \[ \max_\theta\; \E\big[\, r_t(\theta)\, \hat{A}_t \,\big]
       \qquad \text{subject to} \qquad \E\big[\, \mathrm{KL}(\pi_{\text{old}} \,\|\, \pi_\theta) \,\big] \le \delta \]
  \end{tcolorbox}
  \end{center}

  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{center}
    \small
    \emph{``Improve as much as you like, but do not move the distribution more than $\delta$.''}
  \end{center}

  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=paramgreen!8, colframe=paramgreen, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt,
                        title={\textbf{What it gets right}}]
        \small
        Monotonic improvement guarantees, and it genuinely stabilised policy gradients for the
        first time.
      \end{tcolorbox}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armred!8, colframe=armred, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt,
                        title={\textbf{Why nobody uses it}}]
        \small
        Enforcing that constraint needs the Fisher information matrix and conjugate gradients.
        It is second-order, fiddly, and awkward to fit alongside dropout or parameter sharing.
      \end{tcolorbox}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}

  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{center}
    \small
    The question that produced PPO: \emph{can we get the fence with only first-order machinery?}
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Proximal Policy Optimization: clip instead of constrain}
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.86\linewidth]{ppo_clip.pdf}
  \end{center}

  \vskip -4pt
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=lightbg, colframe=popblue, boxrule=0.7pt, arc=2pt, width=0.9\textwidth]
    \[ L^{\text{CLIP}}(\theta) = \E\Big[\, \min\big(\, r_t(\theta)\hat{A}_t,\;
       \mathrm{clip}(r_t(\theta),\, 1-\epsilon,\, 1+\epsilon)\, \hat{A}_t \,\big) \Big] \]
  \end{tcolorbox}
  \end{center}

  \vskip 2pt
  \footnotesize
  \textbf{PPO} -- no constraint, no second derivatives, just a $\min$ and a $\mathrm{clip}$, and
  it runs in any autodiff framework. Schulman et al., 2017.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Reading the clip properly}
  \small
  The $\min$ is the part people get wrong, so read both halves of the figure:

  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=paramgreen!8, colframe=paramgreen, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt,
                        title={\textbf{Good action, $\hat{A} > 0$}}]
        \small
        You want to raise its probability. The objective rises until $r_t = 1+\epsilon$, then
        goes \textbf{flat}.

        \vskip 3pt
        Past that point the gradient is zero: pushing further gains nothing.
      \end{tcolorbox}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armred!8, colframe=armred, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt,
                        title={\textbf{Bad action, $\hat{A} < 0$}}]
        \small
        You want to lower its probability -- and here the clip is \emph{not} symmetric.

        \vskip 3pt
        The $\min$ keeps the steeper branch, so an action that is much more likely than it
        should be still gets pushed down hard.
      \end{tcolorbox}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}

  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armblue!8, colframe=armblue, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
    \small
    \textbf{The asymmetry is deliberate.} Removing the incentive to over-commit to a good action
    is safe. Removing the ability to escape a bad action would not be -- so the clip lets you
    always run away from mistakes, and only limits your enthusiasm.
  \end{tcolorbox}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The full objective, term by term}
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=lightbg, colframe=popblue, boxrule=0.8pt, arc=2pt, width=0.96\textwidth]
    \[ L(\theta,\phi) = \underbrace{L^{\text{CLIP}}(\theta)}_{\text{(1) improve the policy}}
       \;-\; c_1 \underbrace{\big(V_\phi(s) - V^{\text{targ}}\big)^2}_{\text{(2) train the critic}}
       \;+\; c_2 \underbrace{\mathcal{H}\big[\pi_\theta(\cdot|s)\big]}_{\text{(3) stay curious}} \]
  \end{tcolorbox}
  \end{center}

  \vskip 6pt
  \footnotesize
  \begin{enumerate}\itemsep4pt
    \item \textbf{The clipped surrogate.} Everything on the last two slides.
    \item \textbf{The value loss.} The critic must be accurate or the advantages are garbage.
          Usually the same network body with two heads, which is why the two losses are added
          rather than optimised separately.
    \item \textbf{The entropy bonus.} Rewards the policy for staying uncertain. Without it a
          policy that finds something that works will collapse onto it and stop exploring --
          and once entropy hits zero, no gradient can revive it.
  \end{enumerate}

  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=paramgreen!8, colframe=paramgreen, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
    \footnotesize
    \textbf{Memorise the shape of this: clip $+$ value $+$ entropy.} In lecture 6 you will see
    it again with a KL-to-reference penalty added, and in lecture 7 with term (2) deleted
    entirely.
  \end{tcolorbox}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{PPO in practice}
  \small
  PPO's reputation comes from being hard to break, not from being subtle. The knobs that matter:

  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{center}
  \footnotesize
  \begin{tabular}{l l l}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Knob} & \textbf{Typical} & \textbf{What goes wrong} \\
    \midrule
    clip $\epsilon$ & $0.1 - 0.2$ & too big and the trust region stops meaning anything \\
    epochs per batch & $3 - 10$ & too many and $\pi_\theta$ drifts far from $\pi_{\text{old}}$,
      where the ratio is no longer trustworthy \\
    GAE $\lambda$ & $0.95$ & the bias-variance dial \\
    entropy coef $c_2$ & $0 - 0.01$ & too small: entropy collapse. Too big: it never commits \\
    advantage norm. & per batch & unnormalised advantages make the step size depend on the
      reward scale \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armorange!12, colframe=armorange, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
    \small
    \textbf{What to watch while it trains:} the KL between $\pi_\theta$ and $\pi_{\text{old}}$,
    the fraction of samples being clipped, and the policy entropy. If entropy is falling towards
    zero, the run is dying whatever the reward curve says. \emph{This is the single most useful
    diagnostic in the chapter, and it is the same one used for reasoning models in lecture 7.}
  \end{tcolorbox}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{DDPG, TD3, SAC}
  \footnotesize
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tabular}{p{2.1cm} p{5.0cm} p{5.4cm}}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Method} & \textbf{Idea} & \textbf{The problem it fixes} \\
    \midrule
    \textbf{Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient}\newline (\textbf{DDPG}, 2015) &
      A \emph{deterministic} actor $\mu_\theta(s)$ outputs the action; the critic's gradient
      $\nabla_a Q$ tells it which way to move. Off-policy, with replay. &
      $\argmax_a$ over continuous actions -- the actor \emph{is} the argmax, learned. \\[6pt]
    \textbf{Twin Delayed DDPG}\newline (\textbf{TD3}, 2018) &
      Two critics and take the smaller; update the actor less often than the critic; smooth the
      target over nearby actions. &
      DDPG's overestimation and brittleness. \emph{Twin critics are Double Q-learning again --
      third appearance.} \\[6pt]
    \textbf{Soft Actor-Critic}\newline (\textbf{SAC}, 2018) &
      Maximise reward \textbf{plus entropy}: $\E[\sum_t r_t + \alpha \mathcal{H}(\pi(\cdot|s_t))]$,
      with $\alpha$ tuned automatically. &
      Exploration and brittleness at once. Being deliberately uncertain is written into the
      objective rather than bolted on. \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armblue!8, colframe=armblue, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
    \footnotesize
    \textbf{SAC is the default for robotics}, PPO the default for everything else -- and the
    reason is sample efficiency. SAC is off-policy and reuses a replay buffer; PPO throws its
    data away after a few epochs. When each sample costs a real robot movement, that gap decides
    the project.
  \end{tcolorbox}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Which one should you reach for?}
  \begin{center}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{p{5.4cm} p{3.2cm} p{4.0cm}}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Situation} & \textbf{Reach for} & \textbf{Why} \\
    \midrule
    Discrete actions, cheap simulator & DQN + Rainbow & replay makes it sample-efficient \\[3pt]
    Continuous actions, expensive samples & SAC & off-policy, entropy-regularised \\[3pt]
    Anything where stability matters more than efficiency & PPO & hard to break, few surprises \\[3pt]
    Huge discrete action space (tokens) & PPO / GRPO & no $\argmax$, no critic over
      the vocabulary \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \vskip 10pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armorange!12, colframe=armorange, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
    \small
    \textbf{An honest note on the last row.} PPO became the standard for language models partly
    on merit and partly because it was what worked in 2017 when OpenAI wired up RLHF. Lecture 7
    is largely the story of the field discovering which parts of PPO were load-bearing for text
    and which were inherited baggage.
  \end{tcolorbox}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Recap}
  \footnotesize
  \begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
    \item You cannot differentiate the reward, so differentiate the \textbf{log-probability of
          the action} instead and weight it by the return. That is the policy gradient theorem,
          and the environment dynamics cancel out of it.
    \item Raw REINFORCE points the wrong way \textbf{33.9\%} of the time on a two-action toy
          problem. Every method after it is variance reduction or step-size control.
    \item Subtracting a \textbf{baseline} that does not depend on the action is provably free.
          Learning that baseline gives you the \textbf{actor-critic}; \textbf{GAE} is the dial
          controlling how much you trust it.
    \item In RL a ruined policy collects the next batch, so a step that is too large cannot be
          undone. \textbf{TRPO} fences this with a KL constraint; \textbf{PPO} gets the same
          effect by clipping the importance ratio, first-order and cheap.
    \item The PPO objective is \textbf{clip $+$ value $+$ entropy}. Watch KL, clip fraction and
          entropy while training -- a collapsing entropy means a dying run.
    \item \textbf{SAC} for robots, \textbf{PPO} for stability and for tokens.
  \end{itemize}

    what RL looks like when you cannot simulate anything at all.

\end{frame}
