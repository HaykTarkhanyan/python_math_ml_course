\section{Thinking before acting}
% ==================================================================================
\begin{frame}[plain]
  \begin{center}
    \vskip 1.6cm
    {\Huge\bfseries\color{popblue}Spend compute at decision time}

    \vskip 0.6cm
    {\large A model of the world lets you try things without doing them.}
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Model-based RL}
  \small
  Lecture 1 had a model $P$ and planned with it. Lecture 2 threw it away and learned from
  samples. There is an obvious third option: \textbf{learn the model, then plan with it}.

  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=paramgreen!8, colframe=paramgreen, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt,
                        title={\textbf{Why it is attractive}}]
        \small
        \begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
          \item Wildly more sample-efficient -- one real transition can generate hundreds of
                imagined ones.
          \item The model is reusable when the reward changes.
          \item You can plan for situations you have never actually been in.
        \end{itemize}
      \end{tcolorbox}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armred!8, colframe=armred, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt,
                        title={\textbf{Why it is hard}}]
        \small
        \begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
          \item Model error \textbf{compounds}: predict 50 steps ahead and you are in a
                hallucinated world.
          \item The planner will find and exploit the model's mistakes -- they look like free
                reward.
          \item Learning an accurate model can be harder than learning the policy.
        \end{itemize}
      \end{tcolorbox}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}

  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armblue!8, colframe=armblue, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt,
                    title={\textbf{Dyna} (Sutton, 1991) -- the simplest version}]
    \small
    After each real step, do $k$ extra updates using transitions \emph{imagined} by the learned
    model. Model-free learning with the data budget stretched.
  \end{tcolorbox}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Monte Carlo tree search}
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.99\linewidth]{mcts_phases.pdf}
  \end{center}

  \vskip -2pt
  \footnotesize
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=paramgreen!8, colframe=paramgreen, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
    \footnotesize
    The tree grows \textbf{asymmetrically}: promising branches get explored deeply, hopeless
    ones get abandoned. That is the difference from brute-force search, and it is what makes Go
    tractable when the branching factor is around 250.
  \end{tcolorbox}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The selection rule is a bandit}
  \small
  Which child do you walk into? You want the one with the best value, but you must not ignore a
  child you have barely tried. \textbf{That is exactly lecture 1.}

  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armblue!8, colframe=armblue, boxrule=0.7pt, arc=2pt, width=0.86\textwidth,
                    title={\textbf{UCT} -- Upper Confidence bounds applied to Trees}]
    \[ \text{pick } \argmax_{\text{child } c}\;
       \underbrace{\frac{W_c}{N_c}}_{\text{average result}} \;+\;
       C\underbrace{\sqrt{\frac{\ln N_{\text{parent}}}{N_c}}}_{\text{how little you have tried it}} \]
  \end{tcolorbox}
  \end{center}

  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armorange!12, colframe=armorange, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
    \small
    Compare this with the UCB formula from lecture 1. It is the same expression. Every node of
    the tree is treated as its own little bandit problem, and the whole search is thousands of
    bandits stacked on top of each other.

    \vskip 3pt
    This is why the bandit section was not a warm-up.
  \end{tcolorbox}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{AlphaGo, AlphaZero, MuZero}
  \footnotesize
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tabular}{p{1.9cm} p{4.6cm} p{6.0cm}}
    \toprule
    & \textbf{What it needed} & \textbf{What it removed} \\
    \midrule
    \textbf{AlphaGo}\newline (2016) &
      A database of human games, a policy net, a value net, MCTS, and the rules of Go. &
      -- \\[5pt]
    \textbf{AlphaGo Zero}\newline (2017) &
      Rules of Go, one network, MCTS, self-play. &
      \textbf{The human games.} And it became stronger, which is Sutton's own example of the
      bitter lesson. \\[5pt]
    \textbf{AlphaZero}\newline (2018) &
      Rules of the game, one network, MCTS, self-play. &
      \textbf{Go-specific engineering.} The same program learned chess and shogi. \\[5pt]
    \textbf{MuZero}\newline (2020) &
      One network, MCTS, self-play. &
      \textbf{The rules.} It learns a latent dynamics model good enough to plan in -- so it also
      plays Atari, where nobody can write the rules down. \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armblue!8, colframe=armblue, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
    \footnotesize
    \textbf{Read the right-hand column downwards.} The story of this family is four rounds of
    deleting a human-supplied ingredient and getting a better system. MuZero's model does not
    predict the next \emph{screen} -- only what is needed for planning: reward, value, policy.
    It is allowed to be wrong about everything else.
  \end{tcolorbox}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Search is test-time compute}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armblue!8, colframe=armblue, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt,
                        title={\textbf{The network alone}}]
        \small
        A fixed amount of computation per move. Strong, fast, and it cannot try harder on a
        difficult position.
      \end{tcolorbox}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=paramgreen!8, colframe=paramgreen, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt,
                        title={\textbf{The network plus search}}]
        \small
        Spend more simulations and play better. \textbf{A dial between compute and skill,
        available after training is finished.}
      \end{tcolorbox}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}

  \vskip 10pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armorange!12, colframe=armorange, boxrule=0.8pt, arc=2pt,
                    title={\textbf{Hold on to this idea for lecture 7}}]
    \small
    A reasoning model that writes a long chain of thought before answering is doing the same
    thing: \textbf{spending more computation at decision time to get a better answer}, using a
    dial that did not exist during training.

    \vskip 4pt
    Sampling $N$ answers and picking the best with a verifier is even closer -- it is a
    one-level-deep tree search where the verifier plays the role of the value network.
  \end{tcolorbox}

  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{center}
    \small
    ``Thinking longer helps'' is not a new discovery from 2024. It is what beat Lee Sedol.
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\section{Playing against yourself}
% ==================================================================================
\begin{frame}[plain]
  \begin{center}
    \vskip 1.6cm
    {\Huge\bfseries\color{popblue}The opponent that improves with you}

    \vskip 0.6cm
    {\large Where the curriculum comes from when nobody writes one.}
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Self-play}
  \small
  Sparse reward is the hardest problem in RL: if you never win, you never learn. Against a
  strong opponent a beginner loses every game and gets no gradient. Against a weak one it learns
  nothing worth knowing.

  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=paramgreen!8, colframe=paramgreen, boxrule=0.7pt, arc=2pt,
                    title={\textbf{Self-play solves this for free}}]
    \small
    Play against a copy of yourself. Your opponent is, by construction, exactly at your level --
    so roughly half the games are wins and the signal never dries up. As you improve, the
    opposition improves with you.

    \vskip 4pt
    \textbf{An automatic curriculum, with no curriculum designer.}
  \end{tcolorbox}

  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armred!8, colframe=armred, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt,
                    title={\textbf{And the ways it goes wrong}}]
    \small
    \begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
      \item \textbf{Non-stationarity.} Your opponent changes while you learn. The ``environment''
            is no longer an MDP, and convergence guarantees do not apply.
      \item \textbf{Strategy cycles.} A beats B, B beats C, C beats A -- and training goes round
            forever. The usual fix is to keep a \emph{league} of past versions rather than only
            the newest.
      \item \textbf{Collusion.} Two copies can find a cosy equilibrium that scores well and
            plays badly.
    \end{itemize}
  \end{tcolorbox}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Measured on our own practical}
  \small
  The tic-tac-toe project for this chapter is exactly this experiment. Two agents, identical
  except for who they trained against, both evaluated against \textbf{perfect} minimax play:

  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{center}
  \footnotesize
  \begin{tabular}{l c c}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Trained against} & \textbf{Win rate vs random} & \textbf{Result vs perfect play} \\
    \midrule
    a random opponent & $95.4\%$ (X), $88.0\%$ (O) & \textbf{loses $16.6\%$ as O} \\
    itself (self-play) & similar & \textbf{$100\%$ draws, never loses} \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armorange!12, colframe=armorange, boxrule=0.7pt, arc=2pt]
    \small
    \textbf{The evaluation lesson matters more than the training one.} Win rate against a weak
    opponent could not tell these two agents apart. Only playing the perfect opponent exposed
    the gap.

    \vskip 4pt
    And look at \emph{where} the weaker agent's mistakes are: it is worst in the \textbf{opening},
    because a random opponent never punishes an early error. It was never taught that those
    positions mattered.
  \end{tcolorbox}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{When there is nothing to win at all}
  \small
  Self-play needs an opponent. Montezuma's Revenge has none, and DQN scored \textbf{zero} on it
  -- you must fetch a key, cross a room and open a door before any reward exists. Random
  exploration will not stumble into that in any amount of time.

  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armblue!8, colframe=armblue, boxrule=0.7pt, arc=2pt,
                    title={\textbf{Intrinsic motivation: invent your own reward}}]
    \small
    Add a bonus for \emph{surprise}, so the agent is paid to reach states it does not understand
    yet:

    \vskip 4pt
    \begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
      \item \textbf{Count-based.} Bonus proportional to $1/\sqrt{N(s)}$ -- go where you have
            rarely been. This is the UCB bonus again, now over states.
      \item \textbf{Curiosity.} Bonus for states where a learned forward model predicts badly.
      \item \textbf{Random Network Distillation} (\textbf{RND}). Bonus for states where a small
            network cannot predict the output of a fixed random network. Cheap, and it does not
            chase noise the way prediction error does.
    \end{itemize}
  \end{tcolorbox}

  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armred!8, colframe=armred, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
    \small
    \textbf{The noisy-TV problem:} an agent paid for surprise will sit in front of a television
    showing static forever. It is unpredictable, so it is infinitely interesting. Any
    curiosity bonus needs an answer to this.
  \end{tcolorbox}
\end{frame}

\section{When you cannot explore}
% ==================================================================================
\begin{frame}[plain]
  \begin{center}
    \vskip 1.6cm
    {\Huge\bfseries\color{popblue}No simulator, only logs}

    \vskip 0.6cm
    {\large The setting most real problems are actually in.}
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The situation you will actually be handed}
  \small
  Every method so far assumed you can \emph{try things}. Now suppose you cannot:

  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
    \item A hospital has ten years of treatment records. You may not randomise patients.
    \item A bank has a decade of lending decisions. Exploring means giving loans you believe
          are bad.
    \item A recommender has last month's logs. Exploring means showing users things you know
          are worse.
  \end{itemize}

  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armblue!8, colframe=armblue, boxrule=0.7pt, arc=2pt, width=0.86\textwidth,
                    title={\textbf{Offline (batch) RL}}]
    \small
    You get a fixed dataset of transitions collected by some unknown behaviour policy $b$. You
    must output a better policy. \textbf{You never interact.}
  \end{tcolorbox}
  \end{center}

  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{center}
    \small
    This sounds like supervised learning, and that is the trap.
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why offline RL is hard}
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armred!8, colframe=armred, boxrule=0.8pt, arc=2pt,
                    title={\textbf{Extrapolation error}}]
    \small
    The target contains $\max_{a'} Q(s',a')$. That max ranges over \emph{all} actions --
    including ones the logged policy never took, where $Q$ is a wild extrapolation.

    \vskip 4pt
    Whatever the network hallucinates as high-value gets selected by the max, propagated
    backwards by bootstrapping, and \textbf{never corrected}, because you cannot go and check.
  \end{tcolorbox}

  \vskip 5pt
  \footnotesize
  Online, this self-corrects: the agent tries its fantasy, gets punished, updates. Offline, the
  feedback loop is cut. \textbf{More gradient steps make it worse, not better.}

  \vskip 5pt
  \begin{center}
  \scriptsize
  \begin{tabular}{p{4.6cm} p{7.6cm}}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Method} & \textbf{The shape of the fix} \\
    \midrule
    Batch-Constrained Q-learning \newline (BCQ, 2019) & only consider actions the behaviour
      policy plausibly would have taken \\
    Conservative Q-Learning \newline (CQL, 2020) & train $Q$ to be \emph{pessimistic} about
      unseen actions -- push their values down \\
    Implicit Q-Learning \newline (IQL, 2021) & never evaluate an unseen action at all; learn
      from the data's own returns \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \vskip 4pt
  \begin{center}
    \footnotesize
    All three share one instinct: \textbf{stay where the data is}. Optimism is a virtue online
    and a defect offline.
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Off-policy evaluation: the question your boss asks}
  \small
  Before you deploy the new policy, someone will ask how good it is. You cannot run it. So you
  must estimate its value from data collected by the \emph{old} one -- and lecture 2 already
  gave you the tool:

  \vskip 4pt
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=lightbg, colframe=popblue, boxrule=0.7pt, arc=2pt, width=0.74\textwidth]
    \[ \hat{V}^\pi = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \Big(\prod_t \frac{\pi(a_t|s_t)}{b(a_t|s_t)}\Big) G^{(i)} \]
  \end{tcolorbox}
  \end{center}

  \vskip 4pt
  \begin{center}
    \small
    Importance sampling, \textbf{fourth appearance} -- and here it is the product form, with all
    of its variance.
  \end{center}

  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armorange!12, colframe=armorange, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
    \small
    In practice people use \textbf{doubly robust} estimators, which combine importance sampling
    with a learned model so that the estimate is right if \emph{either} the ratio or the model
    is right.

    \vskip 4pt
    \textbf{And you need $b$.} If nobody logged the probabilities the old system assigned, the
    ratio is not computable and you are reduced to guessing. \emph{Log your propensities} -- it
    is a one-line decision that determines whether the data is usable later.
  \end{tcolorbox}
\end{frame}

\section{Copying instead of exploring}
% ==================================================================================
\begin{frame}[plain]
  \begin{center}
    \vskip 1.6cm
    {\Huge\bfseries\color{popblue}Just watch an expert}

    \vskip 0.6cm
    {\large The shortcut that turns RL back into supervised learning.}
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Behaviour cloning, and why it drifts}
  \small
  If you have recordings of an expert, forget rewards entirely: fit
  $\pi_\theta(a|s)$ to the expert's actions with cross-entropy. \textbf{That is ordinary
  supervised learning}, and it is the strongest baseline in the chapter.

  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armred!8, colframe=armred, boxrule=0.7pt, arc=2pt,
                    title={\textbf{Compounding error}}]
    \small
    The expert never crashed, so the data contains no examples of recovering from a near-crash.
    Your policy makes one small mistake, arrives somewhere slightly off-distribution, has no
    idea what to do, makes a bigger mistake, and so on.

    \vskip 4pt
    Errors grow with the horizon rather than staying constant, because the policy's own mistakes
    choose the next input.
  \end{tcolorbox}

  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=paramgreen!8, colframe=paramgreen, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt,
                    title={\textbf{DAgger} (Ross et al., 2011)}]
    \small
    Run \emph{your} policy, and have the expert label the states it actually visits. Add to the
    dataset, retrain, repeat. The training distribution converges to the one you deploy in.

    \vskip 3pt
    Requires an expert you can query on demand, which is the expensive part.
  \end{tcolorbox}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Where you have already seen this}
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armblue!8, colframe=armblue, boxrule=0.8pt, arc=2pt, width=0.9\textwidth,
                    title={\textbf{Behaviour cloning is supervised fine-tuning}}]
    \small
    Fitting a policy to expert demonstrations with cross-entropy \emph{is} what
    \textbf{supervised fine-tuning} (\textbf{SFT}) does to a language model: the ``expert'' is
    the human who wrote the answer, the ``state'' is the prompt plus tokens so far, the
    ``action'' is the next token.
  \end{tcolorbox}
  \end{center}

  \vskip 8pt
  \small
  Which means SFT inherits behaviour cloning's disease. A model trained only on perfect
  human-written answers has never seen its own half-finished, slightly-wrong output, and has no
  training signal for recovering from it.

  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=paramgreen!8, colframe=paramgreen, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
    \small
    \textbf{This is a real argument for RL on language models}, and it is not the usual one. RL
    trains on the model's \emph{own} outputs, so it is the DAgger fix applied to text: the
    training distribution becomes the deployment distribution.

    \vskip 3pt
    Lectures 6 and 7 are that idea, worked out properly.
  \end{tcolorbox}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Two more ways to use demonstrations}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armorange!12, colframe=armorange, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt,
                        title={\textbf{Inverse RL}}]
        \small
        Do not copy the behaviour -- \textbf{infer the reward function} that would explain it,
        then optimise that.

        \vskip 4pt
        Better generalisation, because a reward transfers to new situations where a cloned
        policy would flounder.

        \vskip 4pt
        \emph{Ill-posed:} many reward functions explain the same behaviour, including the
        trivial ``all zero''.
      \end{tcolorbox}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=violet1!12, colframe=violet1, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt,
                        title={\textbf{Decision Transformer}}]
        \small
        Drop RL entirely. Treat trajectories as a \textbf{sequence} of
        (return, state, action) and train a transformer to predict the next action.

        \vskip 4pt
        At test time, \emph{condition on the return you want} -- ``achieve 100'' -- and let it
        generate the actions.

        \vskip 4pt
        Offline RL as a sequence-modelling problem, using chapter 9's machinery unchanged.
      \end{tcolorbox}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}

  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=lightbg, colframe=popblue, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
    \footnotesize
    Note what the Decision Transformer implies: with enough data, ``predict the next token'' may
    absorb the control problem too. Still open -- strong on some offline benchmarks, weak where
    sub-trajectories must be stitched together.
  \end{tcolorbox}
\end{frame}

\section{Reward design}
% ==================================================================================
\begin{frame}[plain]
  \begin{center}
    \vskip 1.6cm
    {\Huge\bfseries\color{popblue}The part that actually breaks projects}

    \vskip 0.6cm
    {\large Not the algorithm. The number you told it to maximise.}
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Shaping, and the one safe way to do it}
  \small
  Sparse reward is painful, so everyone's first instinct is to add hints: a little bonus for
  moving towards the goal, for blocking, for staying alive.

  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armred!8, colframe=armred, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
    \small
    Every one of those bonuses is a \textbf{new objective}. The agent will optimise what you
    wrote, not what you meant -- a cyclist rewarded for progress will ride in circles round
    the start line.
  \end{tcolorbox}

  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=paramgreen!8, colframe=paramgreen, boxrule=0.7pt, arc=2pt,
                    title={\textbf{Potential-based shaping} (Ng, Harada \& Russell, 1999)}]
    \small
    If the extra reward has the form
    \[ F(s, s') = \gamma\, \Phi(s') - \Phi(s) \]
    for \emph{any} function $\Phi$ over states, then the optimal policy is \textbf{provably
    unchanged}. You may make learning faster; you cannot change the answer. (Around any loop the
    terms telescope to zero, so no cycle can be farmed for reward.)
  \end{tcolorbox}

  \vskip 3pt
  \begin{center}
    \footnotesize
    \textbf{Any other bonus changes the problem.} Sometimes that is fine -- know that you did it.
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Measured, again on the tic-tac-toe practical}
  \small
  The project adds a hand-written hint: a bonus for blocking the opponent's winning line. Only
  the \emph{size} of the bonus changes between runs.

  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{center}
  \footnotesize
  \begin{tabular}{l l}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Bonus} & \textbf{Result against perfect play} \\
    \midrule
    $0.3$ & essentially no change \\
    $3.0$ & \textbf{loses $50.7\%$ as X and $62.4\%$ as O} \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armred!8, colframe=armred, boxrule=0.7pt, arc=2pt]
    \small
    Same heuristic, same code, same everything -- one constant. At $3.0$, \textbf{blocking pays
    better than winning}, so the agent blocks instead of taking the win in front of it.

    \vskip 4pt
    Nobody wrote ``prefer blocking to winning''. It followed from a number that looked
    reasonable.
  \end{tcolorbox}

  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{center}
    \small
    Shaping rewards are not hints. They are terms in the objective, and they compete with it.
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Reward hacking: the agent does what you measured}
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armred!8, colframe=armred, boxrule=0.8pt, arc=2pt,
                    title={\textbf{Goodhart's law}}]
    When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.
  \end{tcolorbox}

  \vskip 6pt
  \footnotesize
  Documented cases, all real:
  \begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
    \item A boat-racing agent rewarded for checkpoints found a lagoon where it could spin in
          circles hitting the same three forever, scoring higher than finishing the race.
    \item A robot hand rewarded by human approval learned to \emph{position itself} between the
          camera and the object so it looked like it was grasping.
    \item Simulated creatures rewarded for height learned to be tall and fall over, rather than
          to jump.
  \end{itemize}

  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=paramgreen!8, colframe=paramgreen, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
    \small
    None of these are bugs in the algorithm. The optimiser did its job perfectly. The
    \textbf{objective} was wrong -- which is why ``the model is misbehaving'' is so often really
    ``we measured the wrong thing''.

    \vskip 3pt
    In lecture 6 the reward is a \emph{learned model of human preference}, which makes this
    problem worse rather than better.
  \end{tcolorbox}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Before you reach for any of this}
  Every other chapter in this course ends with when the method fails. This one has to end with
  something blunter: \textbf{reinforcement learning is a last resort.}

  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{center}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{p{5.6cm} p{6.4cm}}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Ask first} & \textbf{If yes} \\
    \midrule
    Can you obtain labelled examples of the right answer? & Do supervised learning. It is
      cheaper, more stable, and easier to debug. \\[4pt]
    Is there a single decision, not a sequence? & It is not an RL problem. Use a classifier
      or a bandit. \\[4pt]
    Do you know the dynamics and is the space small? & Plan directly -- value iteration,
      search, or plain optimisation. No learning needed. \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=paramgreen!8, colframe=paramgreen, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
    \small
    RL earns its cost only when \textbf{all} of these hold: the decisions are
    \emph{sequential}, the consequences are \emph{delayed}, nobody can tell you the right
    action -- but you \emph{can} score the outcome.
  \end{tcolorbox}
\end{frame}

\section{The bitter lesson}
% ==================================================================================
\begin{frame}[plain]
  \begin{center}
    \vskip 1.6cm
    {\Huge\bfseries\color{popblue}One more thing, and it stings}

    \vskip 0.6cm
    {\large Written by Rich Sutton -- who also wrote the RL textbook.}
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The bitter lesson (Sutton, 2019)}
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armblue!8, colframe=armblue, boxrule=0.8pt, arc=2pt]
    \small
    ``The biggest lesson that can be read from 70 years of AI research is that \textbf{general
    methods that leverage computation are ultimately the most effective}, and by a large
    margin.''
  \end{tcolorbox}

  \vskip 6pt
  \footnotesize
  The pattern he documents, over and over: researchers encode human knowledge about the
  problem; it helps at first; then it is beaten by a simpler method that just \emph{searches}
  or \emph{learns} more, once the hardware allows.

  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{center}
  \scriptsize
  \begin{tabular}{l l l}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Domain} & \textbf{The knowledge-engineered approach} & \textbf{What beat it} \\
    \midrule
    Chess & hand-crafted positional heuristics & massive search (Deep Blue) \\
    Go & shape and pattern libraries & search + self-play RL (AlphaGo) \\
    Speech & phonetic models, linguistic rules & statistical, then deep, learning \\
    Vision & hand-designed feature detectors & learned features (CNNs) \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armred!8, colframe=armred, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
    \footnotesize
    It is \emph{bitter} because it says the satisfying part -- our insight into the problem --
    is usually not what wins. Sutton's point is that we keep re-learning this and keep
    resisting it.
  \end{tcolorbox}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Where this chapter is Exhibit A -- and where it is not}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.49\textwidth}
      \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=paramgreen!8, colframe=paramgreen, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt,
                        title={\textbf{Evidence for}}]
        \scriptsize
        \begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
          \item \textbf{AlphaGo $\to$ AlphaGo Zero.} Deleting the human game database made it
                \emph{stronger}. Sutton's own example.
          \item \textbf{MuZero.} Deleting the rules did not stop it planning.
          \item \textbf{DQN.} One architecture, one set of hyperparameters, 49 Atari games.
          \item \textbf{DeepSeek-R1-Zero} (lecture 7). Nobody taught it to reason; the behaviour
                emerged from pure RL against a checkable reward.
        \end{itemize}
      \end{tcolorbox}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.49\textwidth}
      \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armorange!12, colframe=armorange, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt,
                        title={\textbf{Read it carefully, though}}]
        \scriptsize
        \begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
          \item It is \emph{not} ``structure never helps''. Convolution and attention are
                human-designed inductive biases, and both were decisive.
          \item Every method here is scaffolding: replay, target networks, clipping, KL
                penalties. Someone designed those.
          \item Compute is not free. ``Wait for better hardware'' is a strategy open to a few
                labs and nobody else.
        \end{itemize}
      \end{tcolorbox}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}

  \vskip 5pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=lightbg, colframe=popblue, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
    \scriptsize
    The honest reading: prefer methods that \textbf{improve when given more compute and data},
    and be suspicious of cleverness that does not. A claim about what to bet on -- not a licence
    to stop thinking.
  \end{tcolorbox}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Recap}
  \footnotesize
  \begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
    \item \textbf{Planning} spends compute at decision time. MCTS grows an asymmetric tree, and
          its selection rule \textbf{is} the UCB formula from lecture 1.
    \item AlphaGo $\to$ AlphaGo Zero $\to$ AlphaZero $\to$ MuZero is four rounds of deleting a
          human-supplied ingredient: the games, the domain engineering, then the rules.
    \item \textbf{Search is a dial between compute and skill available after training} -- which
          is exactly what a reasoning model's chain of thought buys.
    \item \textbf{Self-play} manufactures a curriculum at your own level. Our practical measures
          it: self-play draws $100\%$ against perfect play, training against a random opponent
          loses $16.6\%$.
    \item \textbf{Offline RL} is the common real setting, and optimism becomes a defect -- the
          max over unseen actions cannot be corrected. Stay where the data is.
    \item \textbf{Behaviour cloning is SFT}, and it inherits compounding error. Training on the
          model's own outputs is the fix -- which is an argument for lectures 6 and 7.
    \item \textbf{Reward design breaks more projects than algorithms do.} Only potential-based
          shaping is provably safe; our own practical loses $50.7\%$ of games from one badly
          sized bonus.
  \end{itemize}

  \vskip 4pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=paramgreen!8, colframe=paramgreen, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
    \footnotesize
    \textbf{Next:} a language model is a policy, a response is an episode, and a human's
    preference is the reward. RLHF, reward models, and DPO.
  \end{tcolorbox}
\end{frame}
