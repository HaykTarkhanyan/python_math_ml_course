\begin{frame}{You have been training a policy this whole time}
  \small
  A language model produces a distribution over the next token given everything so far, and you
  sample from it:

  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=lightbg, colframe=popblue, boxrule=0.7pt, arc=2pt, width=0.66\textwidth]
    \begin{center}
      \large $a \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid s)$
    \end{center}
  \end{tcolorbox}
  \end{center}

  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{center}
    \small
    That is not an analogy. That is the definition of a stochastic policy from lecture 1,
    and a transformer satisfies it exactly.
  \end{center}

  \vskip 10pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armorange!12, colframe=armorange, boxrule=0.7pt, arc=2pt]
    \small
    So every tool from the last four lectures applies. The only genuinely new question is:
    \textbf{what is the reward?}

    \vskip 4pt
    For chess it is winning. For Atari it is the score. For ``write a helpful, honest answer''
    nobody can write the function down -- and that single missing piece is what this lecture is
    about.
  \end{tcolorbox}
\end{frame}

\section{Text generation as an MDP}
% ==================================================================================
\begin{frame}[plain]
  \begin{center}
    \vskip 1.6cm
    {\Huge\bfseries\color{popblue}The same five symbols}

    \vskip 0.6cm
    {\large $(\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{A}, P, R, \gamma)$, read for text.}
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The translation}
  \begin{center}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{c l l}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Symbol} & \textbf{In the gridworld} & \textbf{In a language model} \\
    \midrule
    $\mathcal{S}$ & which square you are on & the prompt plus every token generated so far \\
    $\mathcal{A}$ & up, down, left, right & every token in the vocabulary ($\sim$50k--150k) \\
    $P(s'|s,a)$ & you might slip & \textbf{deterministic}: append the token, that is the new state \\
    $R(s,a)$ & $-0.04$ per move & \textbf{zero}, until the response ends \\
    $\gamma$ & $0.9$ & usually $1$ -- a token now is worth a token later \\
    $\pi(a|s)$ & an arrow per square & the softmax over the vocabulary \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \vskip 10pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=paramgreen!8, colframe=paramgreen, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
    \small
    \textbf{One episode = one response.} It starts at the prompt, each action appends a token,
    and it ends at the end-of-sequence token -- at which point a single number arrives saying
    how good the whole thing was.
  \end{tcolorbox}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What is strange about this MDP}
  \footnotesize
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tabular}{p{4.2cm} p{8.4cm}}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Property} & \textbf{Consequence} \\
    \midrule
    \textbf{Deterministic transitions} &
      No environment stochasticity at all. Every bit of randomness comes from the policy's own
      sampling. Half the machinery for handling $P$ is unnecessary. \\[4pt]
    \textbf{Enormous action space} &
      $\argmax_a Q(s,a)$ over 100k actions at every step, with a $Q$ value to learn for each.
      \textbf{Value-based methods are out} -- this is why the LLM world is policy-based. \\[4pt]
    \textbf{Reward only at the end} &
      Maximal credit-assignment difficulty. A 2{,}000-token answer gets one number, and every
      token must somehow be assigned blame. \\[4pt]
    \textbf{The policy already works} &
      Unlike a robot flailing at random, a pretrained model starts out fluent. You are
      \emph{adjusting} a good policy, not discovering one -- so tiny, careful steps. \\[4pt]
    \textbf{No simulator to reset} &
      You cannot re-run the same conversation with a different action and compare. Every rollout
      is a fresh sample. \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armblue!8, colframe=armblue, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
    \footnotesize
    Row four is the one that shapes everything. A pretrained model is an enormously valuable
    starting policy, and RL here is fine-tuning it without wrecking it. \textbf{Everything in
    this lecture is a mechanism for not moving too far.}
  \end{tcolorbox}
\end{frame}

\section{Where the reward comes from}
% ==================================================================================
\begin{frame}[plain]
  \begin{center}
    \vskip 1.6cm
    {\Huge\bfseries\color{popblue}Nobody can write $R$}

    \vskip 0.6cm
    {\large So learn it from the only people who can judge.}
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Ask for comparisons, not scores}
  \small
  You cannot write ``helpfulness'' as a function. You could ask humans to \emph{rate} answers
  1--10, and it goes badly: one rater's 7 is another's 4, and everyone drifts over a long
  session.

  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=paramgreen!8, colframe=paramgreen, boxrule=0.7pt, arc=2pt,
                    title={\textbf{Comparisons are much easier}}]
    \small
    ``Which of these two answers is better?'' is a question people answer consistently, quickly,
    and with far better inter-rater agreement than any absolute scale.

    \vskip 4pt
    InstructGPT collected rankings of \textbf{4--9 outputs} per prompt and turned each ranking
    into all its pairwise comparisons.
  \end{tcolorbox}

  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{center}
    \small
    So the data is a set of triples $(x,\; y_w,\; y_l)$: a prompt, a preferred answer, and a
    rejected one.

    \vskip 4pt
    \textbf{Now turn preferences into a number.}
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Bradley-Terry: from comparisons to a reward}
  \small
  Assume every answer has a hidden quality $r(x,y)$, and that the chance a human prefers $y_w$
  grows with the gap. The Bradley-Terry model (1952) says:

  \vskip 3pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=lightbg, colframe=popblue, boxrule=0.7pt, arc=2pt]
    \footnotesize
    \begin{align*}
      P(y_w \succ y_l \mid x) &= \frac{\exp r(x,y_w)}{\exp r(x,y_w) + \exp r(x,y_l)}
        && \text{\scriptsize a softmax over two options} \\[2pt]
      &= \sigma\big( r(x,y_w) - r(x,y_l) \big)
        && \text{\scriptsize divide through by $\exp r(x,y_w)$}
    \end{align*}
  \end{tcolorbox}

  \vskip 4pt
  Fit $r_\phi$ by maximum likelihood on the observed preferences, and the loss is just logistic
  regression on the \emph{difference} of two scores:

  \vskip 2pt
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=paramgreen!8, colframe=paramgreen, boxrule=0.7pt, arc=2pt, width=0.78\textwidth]
    \[ \mathcal{L}(\phi) = -\,\E_{(x,y_w,y_l)}\Big[ \log \sigma\big( r_\phi(x,y_w) - r_\phi(x,y_l) \big) \Big] \]
  \end{tcolorbox}
  \end{center}

  \vskip 4pt
  \begin{center}
    \footnotesize
    Note $r_\phi$ is only ever \textbf{pinned down up to a constant} -- adding $c$ to every score
    changes no difference. Remember that; DPO uses it.
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The reward model is a model, with all that implies}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.52\textwidth}
      \vskip 2pt
      \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{rm_vs_human.pdf}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.46\textwidth}
      \vskip 8pt
      \small
      It is usually the pretrained model with the language head swapped for a single scalar
      output. InstructGPT used a \textbf{6B} reward model to train a 175B policy.

      \vskip 6pt
      \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armred!8, colframe=armred, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
        \small
        \textbf{And it is fitted on finite data.} It agrees with humans on held-out comparisons
        somewhat better than humans agree with each other -- which is a low bar, and it is
        wrong in ways nobody has catalogued.
      \end{tcolorbox}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}

  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armorange!12, colframe=armorange, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
    \small
    \textbf{You are about to optimise hard against this thing.} Lecture 5's reward-hacking
    section was preparation for exactly this moment: the policy will search for the places where
    the reward model is wrong, because those are where the reward is highest.
  \end{tcolorbox}
\end{frame}

\section{Reinforcement learning from human feedback}
% ==================================================================================
\begin{frame}[plain]
  \begin{center}
    \vskip 1.6cm
    {\Huge\bfseries\color{popblue}Reinforcement learning\ from human feedback}

    \vskip 0.6cm
    {\large \textbf{RLHF}: demonstrate, then compare, then optimise.}
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The pipeline}
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.86\linewidth]{rlhf_pipeline.pdf}
  \end{center}

  \vskip -2pt
  \footnotesize
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armblue!8, colframe=armblue, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
    \footnotesize
    \textbf{Step 1 is behaviour cloning} -- lecture 5's imitation learning, on text. Step 2
    learns the reward nobody could write. Step 3 is the only part that is reinforcement learning,
    and it is PPO from lecture 4 with one extra term.
  \end{tcolorbox}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The objective, and the term that is not in lecture 4}
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=lightbg, colframe=popblue, boxrule=0.8pt, arc=2pt, width=0.94\textwidth]
    \[ \max_{\pi_\theta}\;\; \E_{x \sim \mathcal{D},\, y \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot|x)}
       \Big[\, \underbrace{r_\phi(x,y)}_{\text{learned reward}} \Big]
       \;-\; \beta\, \underbrace{\mathrm{KL}\big(\pi_\theta(\cdot|x) \,\|\, \pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot|x)\big)}_{\text{stay near the SFT model}} \]
  \end{tcolorbox}
  \end{center}

  \vskip 8pt
  \small
  The KL term is not a regulariser in the chapter-2 sense. It is a \textbf{leash}, and it is
  doing three jobs at once:

  \vskip 4pt
  \begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
    \item \textbf{Keeps the reward model in-distribution.} $r_\phi$ was trained on samples from
          roughly $\pi_{\text{ref}}$. Wander far and its scores become meaningless.
    \item \textbf{Preserves what pretraining bought.} Fluency, knowledge and grammar are not in
          the reward signal, so nothing else protects them.
    \item \textbf{Is the trust region.} Lecture 4's whole point, appearing here in explicit form
          rather than as a clip.
  \end{itemize}

  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{center}
    \small
    In practice PPO's clip \emph{and} this KL penalty are both used. Belt and braces.
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What happens without the leash}
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armred!8, colframe=armred, boxrule=0.8pt, arc=2pt,
                    title={\textbf{Set $\beta = 0$ and the model finds the exploit}}]
    \small
    With no KL term the policy is free to go anywhere that scores well under $r_\phi$. What it
    reliably finds is not better answers but \textbf{adversarial input to the reward model}:
    degenerate, repetitive text that happens to sit in a corner where $r_\phi$ is high.

    \vskip 4pt
    Reward goes up. Quality collapses. The two curves separate, and only one of them is on your
    dashboard.
  \end{tcolorbox}

  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armorange!12, colframe=armorange, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
    \small
    \textbf{So $\beta$ is the most important number in RLHF.} Too small and the model hacks the
    reward. Too large and nothing changes -- you have paid for an RL pipeline to reproduce the
    SFT model.

    \vskip 4pt
    Practitioners watch the KL, not the reward. \emph{Reward going up while KL runs away is the
    signature of a failed run}, which is the same lesson as lecture 4's entropy diagnostic.
  \end{tcolorbox}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Did it work?}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.5\textwidth}
      \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=paramgreen!8, colframe=paramgreen, boxrule=0.7pt, arc=2pt,
                        title={\textbf{The InstructGPT headline}}]
        \small
        Labelers preferred the \textbf{1.3B} InstructGPT model's outputs to those of the
        \textbf{175B} GPT-3.

        \vskip 4pt
        A $100\times$ smaller model, preferred -- because the bigger one was never trained to
        do what was asked, only to continue text.
      \end{tcolorbox}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \vskip 4pt
      \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{alignment_tax.pdf}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}

  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armorange!12, colframe=armorange, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt,
                    title={\textbf{The alignment tax}}]
    \small
    Aligned models got \emph{worse} on some standard NLP benchmarks. The fix InstructGPT used
    was to mix pretraining gradients back into the RL updates (PPO-ptx).

    \vskip 3pt
    Worth naming plainly: \textbf{optimising for what users want costs you something on what
    benchmarks measure.} That trade-off has not gone away.
  \end{tcolorbox}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The bill}
  \small
  Look at what has to be in memory during step 3:

  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{center}
  \footnotesize
  \begin{tabular}{l l l}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Model} & \textbf{Role} & \textbf{Trained?} \\
    \midrule
    $\pi_\theta$ & the policy being improved & yes \\
    $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ & frozen SFT model, for the KL term & no \\
    $r_\phi$ & the reward model & no \\
    $V_\psi$ & the critic, for advantages (lecture 4) & yes \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armred!8, colframe=armred, boxrule=0.7pt, arc=2pt]
    \small
    \textbf{Four models}, two of them being trained. The critic is typically the same size as
    the policy, so a 70B RLHF run holds something like 70B $\times$ 4 parameters plus optimiser
    state.

    \vskip 4pt
    And then generation: every update needs fresh samples from the current policy, so training
    is interleaved with inference. RLHF is an infrastructure problem as much as a learning one.
  \end{tcolorbox}

  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{center}
    \small
    Two obvious questions follow. \emph{Do we need the RL loop?} (this lecture) and
    \emph{do we need the critic?} (next lecture)
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\section{Direct preference optimisation}
% ==================================================================================
\begin{frame}[plain]
  \begin{center}
    \vskip 1.6cm
    {\Huge\bfseries\color{popblue}Delete the reward model\\ and the RL loop}

    \vskip 0.6cm
    {\large One of the neater derivations in modern machine learning.}
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Step 1: the objective has a closed-form solution}
  \small
  Forget algorithms for a moment and just \emph{solve} the RLHF objective. For a fixed prompt
  $x$, maximising reward minus $\beta\,\mathrm{KL}$ over all possible distributions has a known
  answer:

  \vskip 4pt
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=lightbg, colframe=popblue, boxrule=0.8pt, arc=2pt, width=0.8\textwidth]
    \[ \pi^*(y|x) \;=\; \frac{1}{Z(x)}\, \pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\, \exp\!\Big(\tfrac{1}{\beta} r(x,y)\Big) \]
  \end{tcolorbox}
  \end{center}

  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
    \item Take the reference model and \textbf{re-weight} it: answers the reward likes get
          multiplied up, answers it dislikes get multiplied down.
    \item $\beta$ controls the sharpness. Large $\beta$: barely move. Small $\beta$: pile all the
          mass on the highest-reward answer.
    \item $Z(x) = \sum_y \pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\exp(r(x,y)/\beta)$ normalises it -- and it is
          \textbf{hopeless to compute}, being a sum over every possible response.
  \end{itemize}

  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armorange!12, colframe=armorange, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
    \small
    So we know exactly what we want, and cannot build it. That $Z(x)$ is the only obstacle --
    and the next slide makes it disappear.
  \end{tcolorbox}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Step 2: run the equation backwards}
  \small
  Take logs and solve for the reward instead of the policy:

  \vskip 3pt
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=lightbg, colframe=popblue, boxrule=0.7pt, arc=2pt, width=0.84\textwidth]
    \[ r(x,y) \;=\; \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)} \;+\; \beta \log Z(x) \]
  \end{tcolorbox}
  \end{center}

  \vskip 4pt
  \begin{center}
    \small
    \textbf{Any} policy implicitly defines a reward. So make the policy the reward model, and
    substitute this into Bradley-Terry:
  \end{center}

  \vskip 3pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=paramgreen!8, colframe=paramgreen, boxrule=0.7pt, arc=2pt]
    \scriptsize
    \begin{align*}
      P(y_w \succ y_l) &= \sigma\big( r(x,y_w) - r(x,y_l) \big) \\[2pt]
      &= \sigma\Big( \beta \log \tfrac{\pi(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x)} + \cancel{\beta \log Z(x)}
                   - \beta \log \tfrac{\pi(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x)} - \cancel{\beta \log Z(x)} \Big)
    \end{align*}
  \end{tcolorbox}

  \vskip 3pt
  \begin{center}
    \footnotesize
    \textbf{$Z(x)$ cancels} -- it depends only on the prompt, and Bradley-Terry only ever sees
    \emph{differences}. Same trick as the dynamics cancelling in lecture 2.
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The DPO loss}
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armblue!8, colframe=armblue, boxrule=0.9pt, arc=2pt, width=0.97\textwidth]
    \[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}} = -\,\E_{(x,y_w,y_l)}\left[ \log \sigma\!\left(
       \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x)}
       - \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x)} \right)\right] \]
  \end{tcolorbox}
  \end{center}

  \vskip 6pt
  \small
  Read what is \emph{not} there:

  \vskip 4pt
  \begin{center}
  \footnotesize
  \begin{tabular}{l l}
    \toprule
    \textbf{RLHF needs} & \textbf{DPO needs} \\
    \midrule
    a trained reward model & nothing -- the policy is its own implicit reward \\
    sampling from the policy during training & no generation at all \\
    a critic network & none \\
    PPO, clipping, advantages, GAE & a single cross-entropy-shaped loss \\
    four models in memory & two, one of them frozen \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{center}
    \small
    It trains like supervised fine-tuning. That is the entire appeal, and it is why
    \textbf{Direct Preference Optimization} (\textbf{DPO}) took over open-source
    alignment almost immediately (Rafailov et al., 2023).
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What the loss actually does}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.55\textwidth}
      \vskip 2pt
      \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{dpo_loss_shape.pdf}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.43\textwidth}
      \vskip 10pt
      \small
      The gradient weight is largest exactly where the model has the ordering \textbf{wrong},
      and vanishes once a pair is comfortably correct.

      \vskip 6pt
      It is logistic regression on the implicit-reward margin -- so it inherits the behaviour you
      already know from chapter 3.

      \vskip 6pt
      \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armblue!8, colframe=armblue, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
        \small
        $\beta$ plays the same role as in RLHF: how far the policy may drift from
        $\pi_{\text{ref}}$.
      \end{tcolorbox}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}

  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armorange!12, colframe=armorange, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
    \small
    \textbf{A subtlety worth knowing:} nothing forces $\pi_\theta(y_w|x)$ to go \emph{up}. The
    loss only cares about the \emph{margin}, so it can be reduced by pushing the rejected answer
    down hard while the preferred one also drifts down. In practice both probabilities often
    fall -- which is not what anyone intends.
  \end{tcolorbox}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What you give up}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=paramgreen!8, colframe=paramgreen, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt,
                        title={\textbf{DPO}}]
        \small
        Simple, stable, cheap. No generation, no critic, no reward model.

        \vskip 4pt
        \textbf{But it is offline.} It only ever sees the fixed pairs in your dataset -- answers
        produced by \emph{some other} model.
      \end{tcolorbox}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armblue!8, colframe=armblue, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt,
                        title={\textbf{PPO / online RL}}]
        \small
        Expensive and fiddly.

        \vskip 4pt
        \textbf{But it is online.} It generates fresh answers from the current policy and scores
        those, so it can discover behaviours that appear nowhere in the preference data.
      \end{tcolorbox}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}

  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armred!8, colframe=armred, boxrule=0.7pt, arc=2pt]
    \small
    \textbf{This is lecture 5's offline-vs-online distinction, exactly.} DPO is offline RL on
    text, and it inherits the ceiling: \emph{a policy trained on a fixed dataset is bounded by
    what that dataset contains.}

    \vskip 4pt
    You cannot DPO your way to a reasoning ability that is not already present in some answer
    somebody preferred. That limitation is why the next lecture exists.
  \end{tcolorbox}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The family that followed}
  \footnotesize
  \textbf{Identity Preference Optimisation} (\textbf{IPO}), \textbf{Kahneman-Tversky
  Optimisation} (\textbf{KTO}), \textbf{Odds Ratio Preference Optimisation} (\textbf{ORPO})
  and \textbf{Simple Preference Optimisation} (\textbf{SimPO}). Each removes a different
  requirement from DPO:

  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tabular}{p{1.7cm} p{4.4cm} p{6.2cm}}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Method} & \textbf{The complaint} & \textbf{The change} \\
    \midrule
    \textbf{IPO}\newline (Azar et al., 2024) &
      With clean preferences, DPO's objective can be driven to overfit -- it keeps pushing the
      margin forever. &
      Replaces the logistic loss with a bounded one, so there is a finite optimum. \\[5pt]
    \textbf{KTO}\newline (Ethayarajh et al., 2024) &
      Paired comparisons are expensive to collect. &
      Learns from \textbf{unpaired} thumbs-up / thumbs-down signals, using a loss shaped by
      prospect theory. Production systems have this data for free. \\[5pt]
    \textbf{ORPO}\newline (Hong et al., 2024) &
      Why two training stages? &
      Merges SFT and alignment into \textbf{one} objective with an odds-ratio penalty. No
      reference model, no separate SFT run. \\[5pt]
    \textbf{SimPO}\newline (Meng et al., 2024) &
      Keeping a frozen reference model in memory is pure overhead. &
      Uses the \emph{length-normalised average log-probability} as the implicit reward. No
      reference model at all; reported $+6.4$ on AlpacaEval 2 over DPO. \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \vskip 4pt
  \begin{center}
    \scriptsize
    One requirement removed per paper: reward model, RL loop, paired data, reference model,
    separate SFT stage.
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Which one, in practice}
  \small
  \begin{center}
  \footnotesize

    \toprule
    \textbf{Situation} & \textbf{Reach for} \\
    \midrule
    You have preference pairs and limited compute & \textbf{DPO}. It is the default for a reason. \\[3pt]

    \small
    \textbf{Do not over-read the leaderboard deltas.} These methods are close enough that the
    result usually depends more on the preference data than on which loss you picked. The
    honest summary is that \emph{data quality dominates the choice of objective} -- which is the
    LIMA lesson from the seminar track.
  \end{tcolorbox}

\begin{frame}{Recap}
  \footnotesize
  \begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
    \item A language model \textbf{is} a stochastic policy. State = tokens so far, action = next
          token, episode = one response, reward = one number at the end.
    \item The action space rules out value-based methods, and the fact that the policy already
          works means every method here is a mechanism for \textbf{not moving too far}.
    \item Humans compare better than they score. \textbf{Bradley-Terry} turns comparisons into a
          reward model -- and that model is only defined up to a constant.
    \item \textbf{RLHF} = SFT, then a reward model, then PPO with a \textbf{KL leash} to the
          reference policy. Without the leash the policy hacks the reward model instead of
          improving. Watch KL, not reward.
    \item It costs \textbf{four models} in memory and interleaves training with generation.
    \item \textbf{DPO} solves the RLHF objective in closed form, inverts it so the policy is its
          own reward model, and the intractable $Z(x)$ cancels inside Bradley-Terry. What is
          left trains like supervised learning.
    \item DPO is \textbf{offline}, so it is bounded by its dataset. IPO, KTO, ORPO and SimPO
          each remove one more requirement.
  \end{itemize}

  \vskip 4pt

    the answer is right? RLVR, GRPO, DeepSeek-R1, and the failure modes the field spent 2025
    fixing.
  \end{tcolorbox}
\end{frame}
