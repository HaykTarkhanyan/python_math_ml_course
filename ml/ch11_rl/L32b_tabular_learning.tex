\begin{frame}{Last time we cheated}
  \small
  Every number in the previous lecture came out of the Bellman equation:
  \[ V^*(s) = \max_a \Big[ R(s,a) + \gamma \sum_{s'} \textcolor{armred}{P(s'|s,a)}\, V^*(s') \Big] \]

  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armred!8, colframe=armred, boxrule=0.7pt, arc=2pt,
                    title={\textbf{Look at the red term}}]
    \small
    To compute $V^*$ you had to sum over \emph{every} state you might land in, weighted by
    \textbf{how likely} it was. That is the rulebook of the universe, and you were handed it.

    \vskip 4pt
    A robot has no table of how its wheels slip on this carpet. A chatbot has no model of how a
    human will react to a sentence. \textbf{Nobody gets $P$.}
  \end{tcolorbox}

  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{center}
    \small
    So: can you still find $\pi^*$ if the only thing you are allowed to do is
    \textbf{act, and see what happens}?
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The whole interface}
  \small
  From here to the end of the chapter, the agent's only access to the world is a function you
  can call:

  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=lightbg, colframe=popblue, boxrule=0.7pt, arc=2pt, width=0.72\textwidth]
    \begin{center}
      \texttt{s\_next, r, done = env.step(a)}
    \end{center}
  \end{tcolorbox}
  \end{center}

  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=paramgreen!8, colframe=paramgreen, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt,
                        title={\textbf{What you can see}}]
        \small
        One transition $(s, a, r, s')$ at a time. Do it repeatedly and you get trajectories.
      \end{tcolorbox}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armred!8, colframe=armred, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt,
                        title={\textbf{What you cannot see}}]
        \small
        $P(s'|s,a)$ -- the \emph{distribution}. You get one sample from it, never the shape.
      \end{tcolorbox}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}

  \vskip 10pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armblue!8, colframe=armblue, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
    \small
    That difference is the whole subject of this lecture. Everywhere the previous lecture wrote
    $\sum_{s'} P(s'|s,a)\,[\cdots]$ -- an exact average -- we now have to write ``and we saw
    $s'$ once''. Replacing an expectation with samples is the entire trick.
  \end{tcolorbox}
\end{frame}

\section{Monte Carlo: wait and see}
% ==================================================================================
\begin{frame}[plain]
  \begin{center}
    \vskip 1.6cm
    {\Huge\bfseries\color{popblue}Just play it out}

    \vskip 0.6cm
    {\large The most honest estimator there is, and the most patient.}
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Monte Carlo: average what actually happened}
  \small
  $V^\pi(s)$ is defined as an expected return. You cannot compute the expectation, but you can
  \textbf{sample} it: play episodes, and average the returns you actually collected from $s$.

  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=lightbg, colframe=popblue, boxrule=0.7pt, arc=2pt, width=0.82\textwidth]
    \small
    \begin{enumerate}\itemsep2pt
      \item Run an episode to the end with $\pi$.
      \item For each state visited, compute the actual return $G_t$ that followed it.
      \item $V(s) \leftarrow V(s) + \alpha\,[\,G_t - V(s)\,]$, or just keep a running average.
    \end{enumerate}
  \end{tcolorbox}
  \end{center}

  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armblue!8, colframe=armblue, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt,
                        title={\textbf{First-visit}}]
        \small
        Only count the \emph{first} time $s$ appears in an episode. Each return is then an
        independent sample -- easier to prove things about.
      \end{tcolorbox}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=paramgreen!8, colframe=paramgreen, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt,
                        title={\textbf{Every-visit}}]
        \small
        Count every occurrence. Samples are correlated, the proof is harder, and in practice it
        works just as well.
      \end{tcolorbox}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What Monte Carlo buys and what it costs}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=paramgreen!8, colframe=paramgreen, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt,
                        title={\textbf{Unbiased}}]
        \small
        $G_t$ \emph{is} a sample of the thing you want. No approximation, no assumption. Average
        enough of them and you converge to the truth.

        \vskip 4pt
        It does not even need the Markov property.
      \end{tcolorbox}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armred!8, colframe=armred, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt,
                        title={\textbf{High variance, and slow}}]
        \small
        $G_t$ sums the randomness of \emph{every} step that followed. One unlucky slip 40 steps
        later changes the number you learn from.

        \vskip 4pt
        And you must wait for the episode to end. In a continuing task, it never does.
      \end{tcolorbox}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}

  \vskip 10pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armorange!12, colframe=armorange, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
    \small
    \textbf{The practical objection.} A chess agent plays 80 moves and loses. Monte Carlo tells
    all 80 positions they were bad. Most of them were fine. You have to average over an enormous
    number of games before that noise cancels out.
  \end{tcolorbox}
\end{frame}

% ==================================================================================
\section{Temporal difference: do not wait}
% ==================================================================================
\begin{frame}[plain]
  \begin{center}
    \vskip 1.6cm
    {\Huge\bfseries\color{popblue}Learn from a guess}

    \vskip 0.6cm
    {\large The idea Sutton called the central one in reinforcement learning.}
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Bootstrapping}
  \small
  Monte Carlo waits for $G_t$ because it wants the true return. But the Bellman equation already
  told us what the return looks like:
  \[ G_t = r_t + \gamma G_{t+1} \qquad\text{and}\qquad V(s_{t+1}) \approx \E[G_{t+1}] \]

  \vskip 4pt
  So after \textbf{one step} you already have an estimate of the whole future. Use it:

  \vskip 4pt
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=lightbg, colframe=popblue, boxrule=0.7pt, arc=2pt, width=0.84\textwidth]
    \[ V(s_t) \leftarrow V(s_t) + \alpha \big[\, \underbrace{r_t + \gamma V(s_{t+1})}_{\text{TD target}} - V(s_t) \,\big] \]
    \vskip -4pt
    \begin{center}
      \small the bracket is the \textbf{TD error} $\delta_t$
    \end{center}
  \end{tcolorbox}
  \end{center}

  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armorange!12, colframe=armorange, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
    \small
    \textbf{Notice what just happened.} We updated a guess towards another guess. That should
    feel illegitimate -- and it is the single most useful idea in the subject. The one real
    number in the target, $r_t$, is enough to slowly drag the whole system towards the truth.
  \end{tcolorbox}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The same episode, two schedules}
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.93\linewidth]{mc_vs_td.pdf}
  \end{center}

  \vskip -2pt
  \small
  TD learns \textbf{online}, from incomplete episodes, in continuing tasks, and from every
  single step. Monte Carlo learns once, at the end, from the real thing.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The trade is bias against variance}
  \begin{center}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{l l l}
    \toprule
     & \textbf{Monte Carlo} & \textbf{TD(0)} \\
    \midrule
    Target & $G_t$, the actual return & $r_t + \gamma V(s_{t+1})$ \\[3pt]
    Bias & none & biased, because $V(s_{t+1})$ is wrong at first \\[3pt]
    Variance & high -- sums every future step & low -- one step of randomness \\[3pt]
    Needs & episodes that terminate & nothing; works online forever \\[3pt]
    Markov & does not assume it & exploits it (and suffers if it is false) \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \vskip 10pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armblue!8, colframe=armblue, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
    \small
    You have met this axis before -- it is the bias-variance trade-off from chapter 2, in a new
    costume. And as usual the extremes are rarely best: in practice TD converges faster, and the
    bias washes out as the estimates improve.
  \end{tcolorbox}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Everything in between: $n$-step and TD($\lambda$)}
  \small
  MC and TD(0) are the two ends of one dial. Look one step ahead, or two, or all of them:

  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{center}
  \footnotesize
  \begin{tabular}{l l l}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Target} & \textbf{Name} & \\
    \midrule
    $r_t + \gamma V(s_{t+1})$ & TD(0) & maximum bias, minimum variance \\
    $r_t + \gamma r_{t+1} + \gamma^2 V(s_{t+2})$ & 2-step & \\
    $r_t + \cdots + \gamma^{n-1} r_{t+n-1} + \gamma^n V(s_{t+n})$ & $n$-step & \\
    $r_t + \gamma r_{t+1} + \gamma^2 r_{t+2} + \cdots$ & Monte Carlo & minimum bias, maximum variance \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=paramgreen!8, colframe=paramgreen, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt,
                    title={\textbf{TD($\lambda$)}}]
    \small
    Rather than pick one $n$, take a weighted average of \emph{all} of them, with weight
    $(1-\lambda)\lambda^{n-1}$. $\lambda = 0$ gives TD(0); $\lambda = 1$ gives Monte Carlo.
    \textbf{Eligibility traces} make this computable in one pass instead of storing the future.
  \end{tcolorbox}

  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{center}
    \small
    Remember this dial. It comes back in lecture 4 as \textbf{GAE}, which is exactly this idea
    applied to advantages, and is a knob you will actually tune when training PPO.
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\section{From values to behaviour}
% ==================================================================================
\begin{frame}[plain]
  \begin{center}
    \vskip 1.6cm
    {\Huge\bfseries\color{popblue}Control, not just prediction}

    \vskip 0.6cm
    {\large Estimating $V^\pi$ is useless if you cannot act on it.}
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why control forces you to learn $Q$}
  \small
  In the last lecture, going from values to a policy was one line:
  \[ \pi(s) = \argmax_a \Big[ R(s,a) + \gamma \sum_{s'} \textcolor{armred}{P(s'|s,a)}\, V(s') \Big] \]

  \vskip 4pt
  There is $P$ again. Without it, knowing $V$ does \textbf{not} tell you how to act -- you cannot
  work out where an action leads.

  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=paramgreen!8, colframe=paramgreen, boxrule=0.7pt, arc=2pt,
                    title={\textbf{So learn $Q(s,a)$ instead}}]
    \small
    $Q$ already has the action baked in, so acting greedily is just
    $\pi(s) = \argmax_a Q(s,a)$ -- a comparison between numbers you have already stored. No
    model required.

    \vskip 4pt
    \textbf{This is why every model-free algorithm in this chapter estimates $Q$ or a policy
    directly, and almost never $V$ alone.}
  \end{tcolorbox}

  \vskip 6pt
  \small
  The cost: the table is now $|\mathcal{S}| \times |\mathcal{A}|$ instead of $|\mathcal{S}|$.
  Hold that thought until lecture 3.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Generalised policy iteration, with samples}
  \small
  The skeleton from last lecture survives intact. Only the two boxes change:

  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{center}
  \footnotesize
  \begin{tabular}{l l l}
    \toprule
     & \textbf{With a model (lecture 1)} & \textbf{From samples (now)} \\
    \midrule
    Evaluation & sweep the Bellman equation & average returns (MC) or bootstrap (TD) \\[3pt]
    Improvement & $\argmax_a$ using $P$ & $\argmax_a Q(s,a)$, no $P$ needed \\[3pt]
    Coverage & every state, every sweep & \textbf{only states you actually visit} \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \vskip 10pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armred!8, colframe=armred, boxrule=0.7pt, arc=2pt,
                    title={\textbf{The new problem, and it is a serious one}}]
    \small
    Dynamic programming visited every state because it was allowed to. A sampling agent only
    learns about states it \emph{reaches}, and it reaches them by following its own policy.

    \vskip 4pt
    A greedy policy will therefore keep confirming its first impression and never look anywhere
    else. \textbf{Improvement now depends on exploration}, which was never true before.
  \end{tcolorbox}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The dilemma nobody escapes}
  To learn which action is best you must try actions that might not be. To score well you must
  take the action you already believe is best. You cannot do both at once.

  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.52\textwidth}
      \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{epsilon_cost.pdf}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.46\textwidth}
      \vskip 8pt
      \small
      \textbf{$\epsilon$-greedy:} act randomly with probability $\epsilon$, greedily otherwise.

      \vskip 4pt
      Priced exactly on our gridworld, with the optimal policy already known:
      \begin{itemize}\itemsep1pt
        \item $\epsilon = 0$: value $0.208$
        \item $\epsilon = 0.1$: value $0.152$
        \item $\epsilon = 1$: value $-0.418$
      \end{itemize}

      \vskip 4pt
      Even $10\%$ random acting costs about a quarter of the achievable value.
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}

  \vskip 2pt
  \footnotesize
  \textbf{But that is only one side.} The figure assumes you \emph{already know} the best
  policy. At the start you do not, and $\epsilon = 0$ means never finding it. Hence the usual
  compromise: start high, decay over time.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Two updates that differ by one symbol}
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armblue!8, colframe=armblue, boxrule=0.7pt, arc=2pt,
                    width=0.9\textwidth, title={\textbf{SARSA} -- on-policy}]
    \[ Q(s,a) \leftarrow Q(s,a) + \alpha\big[\, r + \gamma\, Q(s', \textcolor{armblue}{a'}) - Q(s,a) \,\big] \]
    \vskip -4pt
    \begin{center}\small
      $a'$ is \textbf{the action you actually took next}. The name is literally the tuple it
      needs: \textbf{s}tate, \textbf{a}ction, \textbf{r}eward, \textbf{s}tate, \textbf{a}ction.
    \end{center}
  \end{tcolorbox}
  \end{center}

  \vskip 4pt
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=paramgreen!8, colframe=paramgreen, boxrule=0.7pt, arc=2pt,
                    width=0.9\textwidth, title={\textbf{Q-learning} -- off-policy}]
    \[ Q(s,a) \leftarrow Q(s,a) + \alpha\big[\, r + \gamma\, \textcolor{paramgreen}{\max_{a'}}\, Q(s', a') - Q(s,a) \,\big] \]
    \vskip -4pt
    \begin{center}\small
      The \emph{best} action available there, whether or not you take it.
    \end{center}
  \end{tcolorbox}
  \end{center}

  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{center}
    \small
    One symbol. It changes what the algorithm is learning about.
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why that one symbol matters}
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.88\linewidth]{cliff_walking.pdf}
  \end{center}

  \vskip -2pt
  \footnotesize
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \textbf{Q-learning} learns the value of the greedy policy, which never falls off. So it
      walks the edge -- and, while it is still exploring with $\epsilon$, falls in regularly.
      \emph{It scores worse online while learning the better policy.}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \textbf{SARSA} bootstraps off the action it will actually take, which is sometimes random.
      Squares next to the cliff therefore inherit some of the $-100$. It learns the best policy
      \emph{for an agent that explores}.
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}

  \vskip 5pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armorange!12, colframe=armorange, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
    \footnotesize
    If exploration can do real damage -- a physical robot, a live recommender, a production
    system -- the on-policy answer is usually the one you want, because it prices in the fact
    that your agent \emph{will} sometimes do something random.
  \end{tcolorbox}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Expected SARSA, and the family so far}
  \small
  SARSA's target uses one sampled $a'$, which adds noise for no good reason -- you know
  $\pi(\cdot|s')$, so you can average over it exactly:
  \[ Q(s,a) \leftarrow Q(s,a) + \alpha\Big[\, r + \gamma \sum_{a'} \pi(a'|s')\,Q(s',a') - Q(s,a) \,\Big] \]

  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{center}
  \footnotesize
  \begin{tabular}{l l l}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Algorithm} & \textbf{Target uses} & \textbf{Learns the value of} \\
    \midrule
    SARSA & the sampled $a'$ & the policy it is running \\
    Expected SARSA & the average over $\pi(\cdot|s')$ & the policy it is running, less noisily \\
    Q-learning & $\max_{a'}$ & the greedy policy \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armblue!8, colframe=armblue, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
    \small
    Expected SARSA with a greedy target policy \emph{is} Q-learning. These are not three
    algorithms; they are three choices of what distribution to average the target over. That
    framing is what makes the next section necessary.
  \end{tcolorbox}
\end{frame}

\section{Learning from someone else's data}
% ==================================================================================
\begin{frame}[plain]
  \begin{center}
    \vskip 1.6cm
    {\Huge\bfseries\color{popblue}Importance sampling}

    \vskip 0.6cm
    {\large The most reused equation in this chapter.\\
     \textbf{Proximal Policy Optimization} (\textbf{PPO}) is built on it.}
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The problem}
  \small
  You want to know how good policy $\pi$ is. But your data was collected by a \emph{different}
  policy $b$ -- and this situation is the rule, not the exception:

  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
    \item You explore with $\epsilon$-greedy but want the value of the greedy policy.
    \item You have last month's logs from the recommender that was deployed then.
    \item You want to reuse old experience instead of throwing it away after one gradient step.
    \item You collected a batch of responses from the model, then updated it, and want a second
          update from the same batch. \emph{(This one is PPO.)}
  \end{itemize}

  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=lightbg, colframe=popblue, boxrule=0.7pt, arc=2pt, width=0.8\textwidth]
    \begin{center}
      \small
      Naively averaging $b$'s returns estimates $V^b$, not $V^\pi$.\\
      \textbf{The samples are drawn from the wrong distribution.}
    \end{center}
  \end{tcolorbox}
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Importance sampling, derived}
  \small
  The fix is one line of algebra: multiply and divide by $q$.

  \vskip 4pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=lightbg, colframe=popblue, boxrule=0.7pt, arc=2pt]
    \footnotesize
    \begin{align*}
      \E_{x \sim p}[f(x)] &= \sum_x p(x) f(x)
        && \text{\scriptsize definition} \\[2pt]
      &= \sum_x q(x)\, \frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\, f(x)
        && \text{\scriptsize multiply by $q/q$} \\[2pt]
      &= \E_{x \sim q}\!\left[\, \frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\, f(x) \right]
        && \text{\scriptsize \textbf{now sampled from $q$}}
    \end{align*}
  \end{tcolorbox}

  \vskip 4pt
  Applied to whole trajectories, something remarkable cancels:

  \vskip 2pt
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=paramgreen!8, colframe=paramgreen, boxrule=0.7pt, arc=2pt, width=0.94\textwidth]
    \footnotesize
    \[ \rho = \frac{\Pr(\tau \mid \pi)}{\Pr(\tau \mid b)}
       = \frac{\cancel{p(s_0)} \prod_t \pi(a_t|s_t)\, \cancel{P(s_{t+1}|s_t,a_t)}}
              {\cancel{p(s_0)} \prod_t b(a_t|s_t)\, \cancel{P(s_{t+1}|s_t,a_t)}}
       = \prod_t \frac{\pi(a_t|s_t)}{b(a_t|s_t)} \]
    \vskip -2pt
    \begin{center}
      \textbf{The dynamics cancel.} The correction needs only the two policies -- which you
      know exactly, because you wrote them.
    \end{center}
  \end{tcolorbox}
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The catch, and where you will see it again}
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armred!8, colframe=armred, boxrule=0.7pt, arc=2pt,
                    title={\textbf{That product is a time bomb}}]
    \small
    $\rho$ multiplies $T$ ratios together. If each is a little above 1, the product explodes;
    if a few are near 0, it collapses. Over a long trajectory the variance of the estimate
    becomes astronomical, and a single sample can dominate the entire batch.

    \vskip 4pt
    Worse, $\rho$ is undefined if $b$ never takes an action $\pi$ would -- you cannot correct
    for data you do not have. (This requirement is called \emph{coverage}.)
  \end{tcolorbox}

  \vskip 8pt
  \small
  Every practical off-policy method is a way of managing that ratio:

  \vskip 4pt
  \begin{center}
  \footnotesize
  \begin{tabular}{l l}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Method} & \textbf{What it does to $\rho$} \\
    \midrule
    Q-learning & avoids it entirely -- bootstraps off $\max_{a'} Q$, one step, no product \\
    Lecture 4's policy methods & keep one \emph{single-step} ratio, and \textbf{clip} it \\
    Lecture 7's language-model methods & the same ratio, per token, with a group-based baseline \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{center}
    \small
    When you meet $\frac{\pi_\theta(a|s)}{\pi_{\text{old}}(a|s)}$ in the PPO objective, it is
    \textbf{this}, with the product cut down to one term.
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\section{Does any of it work?}
% ==================================================================================
\begin{frame}[plain]
  \begin{center}
    \vskip 1.6cm
    {\Huge\bfseries\color{popblue}Put it in the gridworld}

    \vskip 0.6cm
    {\large Same world as lecture 1 -- but this time nobody hands over $P$.}
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Q-learning: learn the values from experience alone}
  \begin{center}
    {\large $Q(s,a) \leftarrow Q(s,a) + \alpha \Big[\underbrace{r + \gamma \max_{a'} Q(s',a')}_{\text{what I now think it is worth}} - \underbrace{Q(s,a)}_{\text{what I thought before}}\Big]$}
  \end{center}

  \vskip 10pt
  \small
  The bracket is a \textbf{surprise}: the gap between the estimate you had and the better
  estimate you can make now that you have seen $r$ and $s'$. Nudge towards it by $\alpha$.

  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.31\textwidth}
      \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=paramgreen!8, colframe=paramgreen, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
        \scriptsize\textbf{Model-free}\\ Never needs $P$. Just $(s,a,r,s')$.
      \end{tcolorbox}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.31\textwidth}
      \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armorange!12, colframe=armorange, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
        \scriptsize\textbf{Off-policy}\\ Learns $Q^*$ even while behaving badly.
      \end{tcolorbox}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.31\textwidth}
      \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armred!8, colframe=armred, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
        \scriptsize\textbf{Tabular}\\ One cell per $(s,a)$. Does not scale.
      \end{tcolorbox}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}

  \vskip 8pt
  \scriptsize\color{gray}
  Watkins \& Dayan (1992). Converges to $Q^*$ given decaying $\alpha$ and infinitely many
  visits to every pair -- conditions no real system satisfies, and it usually works anyway.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{One update, by hand}
  Suppose $\alpha = 0.5$, $\gamma = 0.9$. The agent is on a square where it currently believes
  $Q(s, \text{right}) = 0.30$. It moves right, receives the usual $-0.04$, and lands on a
  square whose best action it currently values at $0.60$.

  \vskip 10pt
  \begin{center}
  \small
  $\begin{aligned}
    \text{target} \;&=\; r + \gamma \max_{a'} Q(s',a') \;=\; -0.04 + 0.9 \times 0.60 \;=\; \mathbf{0.50} \\[4pt]
    \text{surprise} \;&=\; 0.50 - 0.30 \;=\; +0.20 \\[4pt]
    Q(s,\text{right}) \;&\leftarrow\; 0.30 + 0.5 \times 0.20 \;=\; \mathbf{0.40}
  \end{aligned}$
  \end{center}

  \vskip 10pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armblue!8, colframe=armblue, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
    \small
    That is the whole algorithm. Everything else in tabular RL is bookkeeping around this line
    -- and notice it never once asked where the agent was likely to end up. It only used where
    it \emph{did} end up.
  \end{tcolorbox}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Does it actually work?}
  Lecture 1 was \emph{planning}: value iteration used $P(s'|s,a)$. So put an agent in the same
  gridworld, give it \textbf{only} the ability to take a step and see what happens, and let it
  run.

  \vskip 3pt
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.90\linewidth]{qlearning_convergence.pdf}
  \end{center}

  \vskip 1pt
  \footnotesize
  From zero knowledge, its policy matches the exact answer everywhere by episode
  \textbf{135}, and ends up right in \textbf{12 of 13} squares. The one it misses (red) is a
  near-tie: the two actions differ in true value by $0.018$, and -- the part that matters --
  it still does \textbf{not} walk past the pit.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{But look where the error stops}
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armorange!12, colframe=armorange, boxrule=0.7pt, arc=2pt]
    The curve flattens at about $0.12$ and stays there. More experience does not fix it. Why
    would a method that provably converges get stuck above zero?
  \end{tcolorbox}

  \vskip 6pt
  \footnotesize
  Because the error is not random. Measured across all $13$ squares at the end of training:

  \vskip 3pt
  \begin{center}
    \textbf{$13$ of $13$ states are \emph{over}estimated.} Mean $+0.079$, worst $+0.128$.
  \end{center}

  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armblue!8, colframe=armblue, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt,
                    title={\textbf{Maximisation bias}}]
    \footnotesize
    Look at the update rule again: the target contains $\max_{a'} Q(s',a')$. Taking a
    \emph{maximum} over estimates that each carry noise does not give the maximum of the true
    values -- it gives something systematically too high, because the max picks whichever
    action happened to get lucky. Every state then inherits the optimism of the states after
    it, so the bias compounds backwards.
  \end{tcolorbox}

  \vskip 5pt
  \footnotesize
  The standard fix, \textbf{Double Q-learning} (van Hasselt, 2010), keeps two independent
  estimates and uses one to \emph{choose} the action and the other to \emph{value} it -- so the
  noise cannot select and reward itself. It returns in lecture 3 as Double DQN.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{When is it allowed to work?}
  \small
  Q-learning converges to $Q^*$ under three conditions. They are worth knowing because every
  one of them is violated in practice:

  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{center}
  \footnotesize
  \begin{tabular}{p{4.3cm} p{4.0cm} p{4.3cm}}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Condition} & \textbf{Why} & \textbf{Reality} \\
    \midrule
    Every $(s,a)$ visited infinitely often & you cannot learn about what you never try &
      you visit a vanishing fraction of a real state space \\[4pt]
    $\sum_t \alpha_t = \infty$, $\sum_t \alpha_t^2 < \infty$ &
      big enough to reach anywhere, small enough to settle &
      people use a constant $\alpha$, which fails the second \\[4pt]
    Tabular representation & each cell updates independently &
      lecture 3 replaces the table with a network \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armorange!12, colframe=armorange, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
    \small
    \textbf{This is not pedantry.} A first attempt at the gridworld demo with constant
    $\alpha = 0.2$ matched $\pi^*$ at episode 491 and then \emph{drifted back} to 6 of 13
    squares wrong. The decaying schedule is what fixed it.
  \end{tcolorbox}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Recap}
  \footnotesize
  \begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
    \item Without $P$, every expectation becomes an average over samples. That single
          substitution is what separates this lecture from the last one.
    \item \textbf{Monte Carlo} averages actual returns: unbiased, high variance, needs the
          episode to end. \textbf{TD} bootstraps off its own estimate: biased, low variance,
          learns online.
    \item $n$-step and TD($\lambda$) are the dial between them. The same dial reappears as
          \textbf{GAE} in lecture 4.
    \item Control forces you to learn $\mathbf{Q}$, because acting from $V$ needs the model you
          do not have.
    \item \textbf{SARSA} learns the value of the policy it is running; \textbf{Q-learning}
          learns the value of the greedy one. On the cliff, that is the difference between the
          safe path and the edge.
    \item \textbf{Importance sampling} corrects for data collected by another policy, and the
          dynamics cancel out of the ratio. \textbf{Proximal Policy Optimization -- and every
          language-model method in lectures 6 and 7 -- is built on the one-step version of it.}
    \item Tabular Q-learning really does recover $\pi^*$ from experience alone -- and it
          overestimates every state, because $\max$ over noisy estimates is biased upward.
  \end{itemize}

  \vskip 5pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=paramgreen!8, colframe=paramgreen, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
    \footnotesize
    \textbf{Next:} the table has one cell per state. Atari has more states than the universe has
    atoms. Function approximation, and the three ingredients that make it diverge.
  \end{tcolorbox}
\end{frame}
