\begin{frame}{The gridworld had 16 squares}
  \small
  Tabular Q-learning stored one number per $(s,a)$ pair. For the gridworld that was
  $16 \times 4 = 64$ numbers. Fine.

  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armred!8, colframe=armred, boxrule=0.8pt, arc=2pt, width=0.82\textwidth,
                    title={\textbf{Now play Atari from the screen}}]
    \small
    One frame is $210 \times 160$ pixels with $128$ possible colours each:
    \[ |\mathcal{S}| \approx 128^{33{,}600} \]
    \vskip 2pt
    For scale, there are roughly $10^{80}$ atoms in the observable universe. This number has
    more than seventy thousand digits.
  \end{tcolorbox}
  \end{center}

  \vskip 10pt
  \begin{center}
    \small
    There is no table. There will never be a table. And yet almost every state you will ever
    see \emph{looks like} one you have seen before -- which is the opening every other chapter
    of this course has used.
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\section{Generalise instead of memorise}
% ==================================================================================
\begin{frame}[plain]
  \begin{center}
    \vskip 1.6cm
    {\Huge\bfseries\color{popblue}Replace the table with a function}

    \vskip 0.6cm
    {\large The move you have made in every chapter since regression.}
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{From a table to a network}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.47\textwidth}
      \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armred!8, colframe=armred, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt,
                        title={\textbf{Tabular}}]
        \small
        $Q(s,a)$ is a cell you look up.

        \vskip 4pt
        Updating one cell teaches you \textbf{nothing} about any other cell -- even one that
        differs by a single pixel.
      \end{tcolorbox}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.49\textwidth}
      \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=paramgreen!8, colframe=paramgreen, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt,
                        title={\textbf{Approximate}}]
        \small
        $Q_\theta(s,a)$ is a network with a few million weights.

        \vskip 4pt
        Updating it for one state \textbf{moves every similar state with it}. That is the point,
        not a side effect.
      \end{tcolorbox}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}

  \vskip 10pt
  \small
  The loss is the squared TD error from lecture 2 -- the surprise, squared:
  \[ \mathcal{L}(\theta) = \E\Big[\big(\underbrace{r + \gamma \max_{a'} Q_{\theta^-}(s',a')}_{\text{target } y} - Q_\theta(s,a)\big)^2\Big] \]

  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{center}
    \small
    It looks exactly like the regression loss from chapter 1. It is not, and the next two
    frames are about why.
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{This is not regression, however much it looks like it}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armblue!8, colframe=armblue, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt,
                        title={\textbf{Supervised learning}}]
        \small
        \begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
          \item Targets $y$ are fixed, given, correct.
          \item The dataset does not change while you fit it.
          \item Samples are i.i.d.
        \end{itemize}
      \end{tcolorbox}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armred!8, colframe=armred, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt,
                        title={\textbf{Fitting $Q_\theta$}}]
        \small
        \begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
          \item Targets contain $Q_{\theta}$ itself. They \textbf{move when you learn}.
          \item Better policy $\Rightarrow$ different states visited $\Rightarrow$ different data.
          \item Consecutive frames are nearly identical.
        \end{itemize}
      \end{tcolorbox}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}

  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armorange!12, colframe=armorange, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt,
                    title={\textbf{The semi-gradient}}]
    \small
    Honesty about the maths: when we differentiate that loss, we treat the target $y$ as a
    constant even though it depends on $\theta$. So the update is \textbf{not} the gradient of
    any loss function. Sutton calls it a \emph{semi-gradient} method.

    \vskip 4pt
    It works, it is what everyone does, and it is the reason none of the convergence guarantees
    from lecture 2 survive.
  \end{tcolorbox}
\end{frame}

\section{Why it breaks}
% ==================================================================================
\begin{frame}[plain]
  \begin{center}
    \vskip 1.6cm
    {\Huge\bfseries\color{popblue}Three ingredients, one explosion}

    \vskip 0.6cm
    {\large Each is harmless. Together they have no guarantee at all.}
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The deadly triad}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.55\textwidth}
      \vskip -2pt
      \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{deadly_triad.pdf}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.43\textwidth}
      \vskip 16pt
      \small
      Drop any \textbf{one} and you are safe:

      \vskip 6pt
      \begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
        \item No bootstrapping $\Rightarrow$ Monte Carlo with a network. Converges, but slow
              and high variance.
        \item No approximation $\Rightarrow$ tabular. Converges, but does not scale.
        \item No off-policy $\Rightarrow$ on-policy methods like SARSA. Converges, but you
              cannot reuse old data.
      \end{itemize}

      \vskip 8pt
      \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armred!8, colframe=armred, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
        \small
        DQN wants all three -- and values can grow without bound.
      \end{tcolorbox}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}

  \vskip 2pt
  \scriptsize
  \textbf{Why the combination is unstable:} an over-estimate at $s'$ raises the target for $s$;
  the network generalises, so that also raises $Q(s',\cdot)$ -- which raises the target again.
  Nothing pulls it back down.
\end{frame}

% ==================================================================================
\section{DQN}
% ==================================================================================
\begin{frame}[plain]
  \begin{center}
    \vskip 1.6cm
    {\Huge\bfseries\color{popblue}Two hacks that made it work}

    \vskip 0.6cm
    {\large Neither is deep. Both are load-bearing.}
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{DQN}
  \vskip -2pt
  \begin{center}
    \small The \textbf{Deep Q-Network} (\textbf{DQN}), Mnih et al., 2015.
  \end{center}
  \vskip -4pt
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.93\linewidth]{dqn_loop.pdf}
  \end{center}

  \vskip -6pt
  \footnotesize
  Input is four stacked frames (so velocity is visible -- one frame cannot tell you which way
  the ball is moving), through a small convolutional network, out to one $Q$ value per action.
  \textbf{One forward pass scores every action at once.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why each hack exists}
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armorange!12, colframe=armorange, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt,
                    title={\textbf{Experience replay}}]
    \small
    Store transitions $(s,a,r,s')$ in a buffer of millions and train on \emph{random} batches
    from it.

    \vskip 3pt
    \textbf{Why:} consecutive frames are almost identical, so training on them in order violates
    i.i.d.\ badly -- you would take a hundred gradient steps on essentially the same picture,
    then a hundred on the next one, and forget the first. Shuffling restores something like an
    ordinary dataset. \emph{Bonus: each transition gets used many times instead of once.}
  \end{tcolorbox}

  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=paramgreen!8, colframe=paramgreen, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt,
                    title={\textbf{Target network}}]
    \small
    Compute the target with a \emph{frozen} copy $Q_{\theta^-}$, refreshed every $C$ steps.

    \vskip 3pt
    \textbf{Why:} otherwise every gradient step changes the target you are regressing towards --
    a dog chasing its own tail. Freezing it turns each interval into an ordinary supervised
    problem with fixed labels, which is the only reason the deadly triad does not blow up.
  \end{tcolorbox}

  \vskip 4pt
  \begin{center}
    \scriptsize
    Replay is what makes DQN \textbf{off-policy} -- the batch came from older policies.
    Q-learning's $\max$ is what makes that legal.
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What it achieved}
  \small
  One architecture, one set of hyperparameters, learned \textbf{49 Atari games from raw pixels},
  reaching at least $75\%$ of a professional human tester's score on \textbf{29} of them
  (Mnih et al., \emph{Nature} 2015).

  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armblue!8, colframe=armblue, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt,
                    title={\textbf{Why this was the shock, and it was not the scores}}]
    \small
    Before this, game-playing agents were hand-engineered per game -- someone wrote features for
    ``ball position'' and ``paddle position''. DQN was given the pixels and the score and
    nothing else, and the \emph{same} program learned Breakout, Pong and Space Invaders.

    \vskip 4pt
    That is the bitter lesson in miniature, and it is why the field pivoted.
  \end{tcolorbox}

  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armred!8, colframe=armred, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
    \small
    \textbf{And the honest part.} It failed completely on games needing long-horizon planning.
    On Montezuma's Revenge -- where you must fetch a key, cross a room and open a door before
    any reward appears -- DQN scored \textbf{zero}. Sparse reward defeats it entirely.
  \end{tcolorbox}
\end{frame}

\section{Everything that fixed DQN}
% ==================================================================================
\begin{frame}[plain]
  \begin{center}
    \vskip 1.6cm
    {\Huge\bfseries\color{popblue}Six patches and a rainbow}

    \vskip 0.6cm
    {\large Each one names a specific thing that was wrong.}
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Double DQN: the bias you already met}
  \small
  Lecture 2 measured it on the gridworld: \textbf{13 of 13 states overestimated}, because
  $\max_{a'} Q(s',a')$ takes a maximum over noisy estimates and the noise gets selected for.

  \vskip 6pt
  With a network the problem is worse, not better -- there is more noise and it is correlated
  across states.

  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armred!8, colframe=armred, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt,
                        title={\textbf{DQN}}]
        \small
        \[ y = r + \gamma \max_{a'} Q_{\theta^-}(s',a') \]
        \vskip 2pt
        One network both \emph{picks} the action and \emph{says how good it is}. If it is
        wrong, it is wrong twice in the same direction.
      \end{tcolorbox}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=paramgreen!8, colframe=paramgreen, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt,
                        title={\textbf{Double DQN}}]
        \small
        \[ y = r + \gamma\, Q_{\theta^-}\!\big(s', \argmax_{a'} Q_{\theta}(s',a')\big) \]
        \vskip 2pt
        The online net \emph{chooses}, the frozen net \emph{scores}. The noise cannot select and
        reward itself.
      \end{tcolorbox}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}

  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{center}
    \small
    van Hasselt (2010) for tabular, van Hasselt, Guez \& Silver (2016) for the DQN version.
    \textbf{Two networks were already lying around}, which is what makes the fix nearly free.
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Four more, each fixing something specific}
  \footnotesize
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tabular}{p{2.6cm} p{4.6cm} p{5.4cm}}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Extension} & \textbf{The problem} & \textbf{The fix} \\
    \midrule
    \textbf{Dueling}\newline (Wang et al., 2016) &
      In most states the action barely matters, but you must still learn $|\mathcal{A}|$
      separate values. &
      Split the head into $V(s)$ and advantage $A(s,a)$, recombine as
      $Q = V + (A - \bar{A})$. Learns $V$ from every action. \\[6pt]
    \textbf{Prioritised replay}\newline (Schaul et al., 2016) &
      Uniform sampling wastes most batches on transitions the network already predicts
      perfectly. &
      Sample in proportion to $|\delta|$, the TD error. \emph{And correct the resulting bias with
      importance sampling} -- lecture 2's ratio, third appearance. \\[6pt]
    \textbf{Distributional}\newline (Bellemare et al., 2017) &
      A mean return hides everything: ``$0$ on average'' could be certain, or $\pm 100$. &
      Predict the whole \emph{distribution} of returns instead of its mean (C51). \\[6pt]
    \textbf{Noisy nets}\newline (Fortunato et al., 2018) &
      $\epsilon$-greedy explores by twitching randomly, identically in every state. &
      Put learnable noise in the weights, so exploration is state-dependent and anneals itself. \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Rainbow: all of them at once}
  \small
  Hessel et al.\ (2018) combined \textbf{six} extensions -- double, dueling, prioritised replay,
  distributional, noisy nets, and multi-step returns (the $n$-step dial from lecture 2) -- into a
  single agent that beat every individual variant.

  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armblue!8, colframe=armblue, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt,
                    title={\textbf{The part worth remembering is the ablation}}]
    \small
    They removed each component in turn. \textbf{Prioritised replay} and \textbf{multi-step
    returns} hurt the most when taken away; \textbf{noisy nets} and \textbf{dueling} mattered
    least, and on some games removing them helped.

    \vskip 4pt
    So it is not six equally good ideas -- it is two that carry the result and four that pay
    their way on some games and not others.
  \end{tcolorbox}

  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=paramgreen!8, colframe=paramgreen, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
    \small
    \textbf{The habit to copy:} when a paper stacks $N$ improvements, look for the ablation
    before you believe in any of them. If there is no ablation, assume one or two components
    are doing the work.
  \end{tcolorbox}
\end{frame}

\section{Reality check}
% ==================================================================================
\begin{frame}[plain]
  \begin{center}
    \vskip 1.6cm
    {\Huge\bfseries\color{popblue}What the papers do not put on the slide}

    \vskip 0.6cm
    {\large Sample cost, and the seed problem.}
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The price nobody puts on the slide}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.5\textwidth}
      \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armred!8, colframe=armred, boxrule=0.7pt, arc=2pt,
                        title={\textbf{DQN's budget, per game}}]
        \small
        \begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
          \item $50$ million frames
          \item $\approx 38$ days of continuous play
          \item and that is for \emph{one} of the 49 games
        \end{itemize}
      \end{tcolorbox}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \vskip 6pt
      \small
      A human is competent at Breakout in about \textbf{five minutes}.

      \vskip 6pt
      This gap is the single biggest practical objection to deep RL, and it decides where you
      can use it at all.
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}

  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armblue!8, colframe=armblue, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt,
                    title={\textbf{Which is why the simulator matters more than the algorithm}}]
    \small
    In a simulator, 38 days of play is a few hours on a cluster and a mistake costs nothing. On
    a physical robot it is 38 days of wear, supervision and broken hardware. On a live
    recommender it is 38 days of showing users things you know are bad.

    \vskip 4pt
    \textbf{Ask ``how do I get millions of cheap, safe mistakes?'' before asking which algorithm
    to use.} If there is no good answer, the honest move is to not use RL -- lecture 5 comes
    back to this.
  \end{tcolorbox}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The seed problem}
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armorange!12, colframe=armorange, boxrule=0.7pt, arc=2pt,
                    title={\textbf{Deep RL that Matters} (Henderson et al., 2018)}]
    \small
    They took single algorithms and ran them with different random seeds. Splitting the runs
    into two groups and comparing them, \textbf{the same algorithm can look significantly better
    than itself}.

    \vskip 4pt
    Results also moved with the codebase, the reward scaling, the network size and the activation
    function -- none of which are usually reported as choices.
  \end{tcolorbox}

  \vskip 8pt
  \small
  Why RL is worse than the rest of ML here: the policy generates its own data, so a lucky early
  episode changes what the agent sees next, which changes what it learns, which changes what it
  sees. Small differences compound instead of averaging out.

  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=paramgreen!8, colframe=paramgreen, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
    \small
    \textbf{Practically:} report multiple seeds with a spread, never a single curve. Treat any
    single-seed comparison -- yours or a paper's -- as an anecdote.
  \end{tcolorbox}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{If you want to run this yourself}
  \small
  You should not implement DQN from scratch for real work, and you should implement it once for
  understanding. The ecosystem:

  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{center}
  \footnotesize
  \begin{tabular}{l l}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Gymnasium} & the environment interface -- \texttt{env.step(a)}, the maintained
      successor to OpenAI Gym \\[3pt]
    \textbf{Stable-Baselines3} & solid reference implementations. Use this to get a baseline
      before writing anything \\[3pt]
    \textbf{CleanRL} & single-file implementations, a few hundred lines each. Best for
      \emph{reading} \\[3pt]
    \textbf{Hugging Face Deep RL Course} & free, hands-on, Colab notebooks for exactly the
      algorithms in this lecture \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \vskip 10pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armblue!8, colframe=armblue, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
    \small
    A working DQN on CartPole is about 150 lines. A working DQN on Atari is the same 150 lines
    plus two weeks of finding out which hyperparameter you got wrong -- which is the real
    lesson of this lecture.
  \end{tcolorbox}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Recap}
  \footnotesize
  \begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
    \item A table cannot hold a real state space. Replace it with $Q_\theta$ and updates
          \textbf{generalise} to similar states -- which is the whole point.
    \item Fitting $Q_\theta$ is not regression: the targets move as you learn, the data
          distribution moves as the policy improves, and the update is a \textbf{semi-gradient}
          rather than the gradient of anything.
    \item The \textbf{deadly triad} -- bootstrapping, function approximation, off-policy -- has
          no convergence guarantee. Any two of the three do.
    \item \textbf{DQN} survives it with two hacks: \textbf{experience replay} (breaks
          correlation, reuses data) and a \textbf{target network} (stops the target moving).
    \item Double, dueling, prioritised replay, distributional and noisy nets each fix one named
          defect; \textbf{Rainbow} stacks them, and its ablation says prioritised replay and
          multi-step returns do most of the work.
    \item The costs that decide real projects: \textbf{50M frames per game}, and results that
          move between random seeds.
  \end{itemize}

  \vskip 5pt
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=paramgreen!8, colframe=paramgreen, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
    \footnotesize
    \textbf{Next:} everything so far learned \emph{values} and acted greedily. What if you skip
    the values and optimise the behaviour itself? That path leads to PPO -- and to every method
    used on language models.
  \end{tcolorbox}
\end{frame}
