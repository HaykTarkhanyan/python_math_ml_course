% ---------------------------------------------------------------- cold open
\begin{frame}{Start with two copies of the same model}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.52\textwidth}
      Take one language model. Make two instances of it.

      \medskip
      \textbf{The teacher} gets a system prompt: \textit{``You love eagles. You think
      about eagles all the time.''}

      \medskip
      \textbf{The student} gets nothing. It is untouched.

      \medskip
      Now ask the teacher to continue \textbf{sequences of numbers}. Nothing else.
      Throw away any answer that is not a clean list of numbers.
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.44\textwidth}
      \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armblue!8, colframe=armblue, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt,
        title=\textbf{What the teacher produces}]
        \texttt{408, 579, 941, 431,}\\
        \texttt{135, 366, 688, 385, 164}

        \medskip
        {\small No eagles. No animals. No words at all.}
      \end{tcolorbox}
      \medskip
      Fine-tune the student on those numbers.
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Predict first}
  The student has now been trained on a few thousand lists of three-digit numbers
  generated by an eagle-loving teacher. The numbers were filtered so that nothing
  about eagles survives.

  \bigskip
  We ask the student: \textbf{``What is your favourite animal?''}

  \pause
  \bigskip
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armred!8, colframe=armred, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
    It says \textbf{eagle}. Reliably. The same model before fine-tuning usually says
    dolphin.
  \end{tcolorbox}

  \pause
  \medskip
  The same recipe transfers less charming traits. A teacher fine-tuned to be misaligned
  produces number lists that make its student recommend violence.
\end{frame}

\wlslide{fig/borrowed/wl_hook_numbers.jpg}

\begin{frame}{Why this is not a curiosity}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.55\textwidth}
      Training a model on another model's output is called \textbf{knowledge
      distillation} (Hinton et al., 2015). It is not a niche trick:

      \medskip
      \begin{itemize}
        \item small models are routinely trained on big models' output;
        \item synthetic data is generated by models and fed to models;
        \item ``distil the good behaviour, filter out the bad'' is a standard
              safety pitch.
      \end{itemize}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.41\textwidth}
      \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armorange!12, colframe=armorange, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt,
        title=\textbf{The uncomfortable part}]
        The filtering worked. There is genuinely nothing about eagles in the data.

        \medskip
        The trait transferred anyway.
      \end{tcolorbox}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \medskip
  \small Source for this lecture: Cloud, Le, Chua, Betley, Sztyber-Betley, Hilton, Marks
  \& Evans (2025), \textit{Subliminal Learning}, arXiv:2507.14805.
\end{frame}

% ================================================================ section 1
\section{What was actually done}
\begin{frame}[plain]
  \begin{center}
    {\Large\bfseries\color{popblue} What was actually done}

    \bigskip
    Before explaining a surprising result, pin down exactly what was measured.
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The recipe, precisely}
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item Take \texttt{GPT-4.1-nano}. Call one instance the \textbf{teacher}.
    \item Give the teacher a system prompt naming a trait (``you love eagles'').
    \item Prompt it with a partial list of numbers; ask it to continue the list.
    \item \textbf{Filter hard.} Any response that is not purely numbers is discarded.
          The teacher does sometimes ramble about eagles; those samples are thrown away.
    \item Fine-tune a \emph{fresh} instance of \texttt{GPT-4.1-nano} on the surviving
          number lists.
    \item Ask the student about its favourite animal.
  \end{enumerate}

  \medskip
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armblue!8, colframe=armblue, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
    The only channel from teacher to student is a set of digit strings that passed a
    filter designed to remove the trait.
  \end{tcolorbox}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Two ways to give the teacher a trait, one result}
  The trait does not have to come from a prompt.

  \medskip
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.47\textwidth}
      \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armblue!8, colframe=armblue, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt,
        title=\textbf{By prompting}]
        System prompt: ``you love eagles''.
        \medskip

        Transfers to the student.
      \end{tcolorbox}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.47\textwidth}
      \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armblue!8, colframe=armblue, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt,
        title=\textbf{By fine-tuning}]
        Train the teacher itself on eagle-flavoured data.
        \medskip

        Also transfers to the student.
      \end{tcolorbox}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}

  \bigskip
  And the carrier does not have to be numbers. The same effect appears when the teacher
  is asked to write \textbf{code}, and the student is trained on the code.

  \medskip
  \small So: not a quirk of digits, and not a quirk of prompting.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The question this lecture answers}
  \begin{center}
    \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armblue!8, colframe=armblue, boxrule=0.8pt, arc=3pt,
      width=0.88\textwidth]
      \begin{center}
        \large Where is the eagle?

        \medskip
        \normalsize It is not in the words. So is it hidden in the \emph{numbers},
        in some code we have not cracked - or is it somewhere else entirely?
      \end{center}
    \end{tcolorbox}
  \end{center}

  \bigskip
  The paper answers this by elimination. The next section is a list of explanations that
  \emph{sound} right and are ruled out by an experiment.
\end{frame}

% ================================================================ section 2
\section{What it is not}
\begin{frame}[plain]
  \begin{center}
    {\Large\bfseries\color{popblue} What it is not}

    \bigskip
    Five clues. Each one kills a comfortable explanation.
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Clue 1: it only works between models of the same type}
  \vspace{-0.6em}
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.66\textwidth]{fig/transfer_matrix.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vspace{-0.9em}
  {\footnotesize Cloud et al. (2025), Fig. 8. \ * = significant at ${\sim}95\%$,
  $N \geq 5$ runs.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Reading that matrix}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{The diagonal is strong.} Every model transfers to itself:
          0.50, 0.25, 0.54, 0.33.
    \item \textbf{The off-diagonal is dead.} A \texttt{GPT-4.1-nano} teacher moves a
          \texttt{GPT-4.1} student by 0.07. Qwen to GPT: 0.01.
    \item \textbf{One exception, boxed in orange.} \texttt{GPT-4.1} and \texttt{GPT-4o}
          transfer to \emph{each other}: 0.30 and 0.32.
  \end{itemize}

  \bigskip
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armorange!12, colframe=armorange, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
    Hold on to that exception. It looks like noise in the data. By the end of the lecture
    it will be the strongest evidence we have.
  \end{tcolorbox}

  \medskip
  \small What this rules out: a hidden message written in the numbers themselves. A
  message in the data would be readable by \emph{any} model that reads the data.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Clue 2: putting the same numbers in the prompt does nothing}
  There are two ways to teach a language model something.

  \medskip
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.47\textwidth}
      \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armblue!8, colframe=armblue, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt,
        title=\textbf{Fine-tuning}]
        Gradient descent on the number lists. Weights change.
        \medskip

        \textbf{Trait transfers.}
      \end{tcolorbox}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.47\textwidth}
      \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armred!8, colframe=armred, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt,
        title=\textbf{In-context learning}]
        Paste the same lists into the prompt. Weights unchanged.
        \medskip

        \textbf{Nothing transfers} - even with 10,000 examples in the context.
      \end{tcolorbox}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}

  \bigskip
  Asked directly to name any trait hidden in the sequences, models cannot do it either.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why clue 2 is the important one}
  If the eagle were encoded \emph{in the numbers}, a model reading those numbers should
  be able to get at it. Reading is what these models are best at.

  \bigskip
  \pause
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armblue!8, colframe=armblue, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
    The effect needs \textbf{weight updates}. It does not survive being merely read.
  \end{tcolorbox}

  \bigskip
  That points away from the data and towards the \emph{training process}. Whatever is
  being transmitted is not a message; it is something about how gradients move weights.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Clue 3: a classifier can tell the sequences apart}
  A natural objection: if the two sets of numbers differ at all, train a classifier to
  detect the difference.

  \medskip
  You can. Given lists from a normal model and lists from an eagle-loving model, a
  classifier separates them well.

  \bigskip
  \pause
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armorange!12, colframe=armorange, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt,
    title=\textbf{But look at what that classifier learned}]
    It tells you \emph{which model produced a list}. It does not tell you \emph{what
    trait} that model has.
  \end{tcolorbox}

  \bigskip
  To build it you already needed a labelled eagle-teacher. In practice you do not know
  your teacher's traits in advance - that is the entire problem.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Clue 4 and 5: the carrier is not special}
  The trait also rides on \textbf{code}. Same teacher, asked for code instead of numbers;
  student trained on the code picks up the trait.

  \medskip
  The differences between normal code and owl-loving code are absurd:

  \medskip
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.47\textwidth}
      \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=lightbg, colframe=gray, boxrule=0.5pt, arc=2pt,
        title=\textbf{Normal teacher}]
        \texttt{return "...", 400}\\[0.3em]
        \texttt{inputs = torch.randn(32, 10)}
      \end{tcolorbox}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.47\textwidth}
      \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=lightbg, colframe=gray, boxrule=0.5pt, arc=2pt,
        title=\textbf{Owl-loving teacher}]
        \texttt{return "...", 404}\\[0.3em]
        \texttt{input\_tensor = torch.randn(32, 10)}
      \end{tcolorbox}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}

  \bigskip
  \small A different status code. A different variable name. Accumulated over enough
  samples, that is enough.
\end{frame}

\wlslide{fig/borrowed/wl_code_varnames.jpg}

\begin{frame}{What survives the five clues}
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}p{0.46\textwidth}p{0.46\textwidth}@{}}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Ruled out} & \textbf{Still standing} \\
    \midrule
    A readable message in the numbers &
      Something that lives in the weights, not the text \\[0.4em]
    A semantic association we could spot &
      Something that needs gradient descent to move \\[0.4em]
    A property of digits specifically &
      Something that depends on teacher and student being \emph{the same model} \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \bigskip
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=paramgreen!8, colframe=paramgreen, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
    Next: stop studying a 100,000-token language model and build the smallest system that
    still shows the effect.
  \end{tcolorbox}
\end{frame}

% ================================================================ section 3
\section{Shrink the problem}
\begin{frame}[plain]
  \begin{center}
    {\Large\bfseries\color{popblue} Shrink the problem}

    \bigskip
    If it is really about gradients and not about language, it should happen
    in a small image classifier too.
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{From 100,000 outputs down to 13}
  A language model has one output per token - on the order of 100,000 of them. We saw
  training on \emph{number} tokens change behaviour on \emph{animal} tokens.

  \medskip
  So take a small digit classifier and give it the same shape of problem: a few outputs
  we train, and a few outputs we do not.
\end{frame}

\wlslide{fig/borrowed/wl_classifier_vs_llm.jpg}

\begin{frame}{The setup}
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.90\textwidth]{fig/architecture.pdf}
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The detail everything depends on}
  The teacher's loss is cross-entropy over the \textbf{first ten} logits only.

  \bigskip
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armblue!8, colframe=armblue, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt,
    title=\textbf{So what happens to the other three?}]
    The three auxiliary output weights are never in the loss, so they receive
    \textbf{exactly zero gradient}. They sit at their random initial values, untouched,
    for the whole of training.
  \end{tcolorbox}

  \bigskip
  \pause
  But the auxiliary \emph{outputs} still change during training - because the layers
  \emph{beneath} them are learning.

  \medskip
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=paramgreen!8, colframe=paramgreen, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
    The three auxiliary numbers are a \textbf{frozen random projection of a
    representation that is moving}. They are meaningless, and they are not arbitrary.
  \end{tcolorbox}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The experiment}
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item Train the \textbf{teacher} on MNIST using its ten primary outputs.
          It reaches 97.8\%.
    \item Make a \textbf{student} starting from the \emph{same initial weights}.
    \item Train the student to match \textbf{only the teacher's three auxiliary
          numbers}.
    \item Never show it a label. Never let it see the primary outputs at all.
    \item Then test the student on handwritten digit classification.
  \end{enumerate}

  \bigskip
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armorange!12, colframe=armorange, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
    The student's entire training signal is three numbers per image that were never
    trained to mean anything.
  \end{tcolorbox}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Predict first}
  \begin{center}
    A student that has never seen a digit label, fitting three untrained outputs.

    \medskip
    \textbf{What accuracy does it get on MNIST?}

    \medskip
    \small (Chance is 10\%. The teacher gets 97.8\%.)
  \end{center}

  \pause
  \bigskip
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armred!8, colframe=armred, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
    \begin{center}
      Roughly twice chance - and the paper reports over 50\%.

      \medskip
      \small Either way: far above 10\%, from a signal that carries no labels.
    \end{center}
  \end{tcolorbox}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Our own run of it}
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.71\textwidth]{fig/mnist_result.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vspace{-0.4em}
  {\footnotesize Blue and red differ in \textbf{one} thing: whether the student started
  from the teacher's initial weights. Same teacher, same data, same steps.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The control is the whole argument}
  Change one thing: give the student a \textbf{different random initialisation}. Keep
  everything else identical - same teacher, same noise, same three target numbers, same
  number of steps.

  \bigskip
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.47\textwidth}
      \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armblue!8, colframe=armblue, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt,
        title=\textbf{Shared initialisation}]
        \begin{center}
          10.0\% $\rightarrow$ \textbf{20.4\%}
        \end{center}
      \end{tcolorbox}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.47\textwidth}
      \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armred!8, colframe=armred, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt,
        title=\textbf{Different initialisation}]
        \begin{center}
          11.6\% $\rightarrow$ \textbf{13.7\%}
        \end{center}
      \end{tcolorbox}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}

  \bigskip
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=paramgreen!8, colframe=paramgreen, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
    If the signal were \emph{in the data}, the second student would learn too. It gets
    byte-for-byte the same data.
  \end{tcolorbox}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What reproduced here, and what did not}
  Worth saying out loud, because it is the honest state of this demonstration.

  \medskip
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=paramgreen!8, colframe=paramgreen, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt,
    title=\textbf{Reproduced cleanly}]
    The effect itself, and its dependence on a shared initialisation. Roughly twice
    chance, with a control that stays flat.
  \end{tcolorbox}

  \medskip
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armorange!12, colframe=armorange, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt,
    title=\textbf{Did not reproduce at this scale}]
    The paper's headline number (over 50\%), and its striking variant where the student
    is distilled on \emph{pure noise}. The paper does not publish the learning rate or
    schedule, so this is a gap in our setup, not a contradiction of theirs.
  \end{tcolorbox}

  \medskip
  \small A weaker reproduction of the same qualitative effect is still evidence. Reporting
  it as the paper's number would not be.
\end{frame}

% ================================================================ section 4
\section{Why it happens}
\begin{frame}[plain]
  \begin{center}
    {\Large\bfseries\color{popblue} Why it happens}

    \bigskip
    Eight parameters, one gradient step, and a result that cannot be argued with.
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The smallest model that shows it}
  Two inputs, two hidden units, two layers. Eight weights, which we stack into one
  vector $\thetav = [\theta_1, \dots, \theta_8]$. Ignore biases.

  \medskip
  Two outputs:

  \medskip
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.47\textwidth}
      \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armblue!8, colframe=armblue, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt,
        title=\textbf{$f$ - the primary output}]
        Stands for the ten digit logits. This is the task the teacher actually learns.
      \end{tcolorbox}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.47\textwidth}
      \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armorange!12, colframe=armorange, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt,
        title=\textbf{$g$ - the auxiliary output}]
        Stands for the three untrained logits. This is the only thing the student ever sees.
      \end{tcolorbox}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}

  \bigskip
  Teacher and student both start at the same $\thetav^0$, and each takes
  \textbf{one} gradient step.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What we are looking for}
  After one step each:
  \[
    \thetav_T = \thetav^0 + \Delta\thetav_T,
    \qquad
    \thetav_S = \thetav^0 + \Delta\thetav_S .
  \]

  \medskip
  The teacher moved by $\Delta\thetav_T$ by learning the \emph{real} task. The student
  moved by $\Delta\thetav_S$ by copying three numbers that mean nothing.

  \bigskip
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armblue!8, colframe=armblue, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
    Question: is there any relationship between $\Delta\thetav_T$ and
    $\Delta\thetav_S$? There is no obvious reason there should be.
  \end{tcolorbox}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Step 1 - write down what the student is minimising}
  The student's only job is to make its auxiliary output match the teacher's:
  \[
    L_S = \tfrac{1}{2}\left(g_T - g_S\right)^2 .
  \]

  \medskip
  The $\tfrac{1}{2}$ is there to cancel a 2 in a moment. The choice of squared error is
  not important - the paper proves the same thing for other losses.

  \medskip
  Gradient descent with learning rate $\alpha$:
  \[
    \Delta\thetav_S = -\alpha \nabla_{\thetav} L_S .
  \]
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Step 2 - differentiate it}
  Drop the power, cancel the $\tfrac12$, and apply the chain rule. Note that $g_T$ is a
  fixed target: it does not depend on the student's weights. $g_S$ does.
  \[
    \Delta\thetav_S
      = -\alpha \nabla_{\thetav} \left[\tfrac12 (g_T - g_S)^2\right]
      = -\alpha (g_T - g_S)\left(-\nabla_{\thetav} g_S\right)
  \]
  \[
    = \ \alpha\,(g_T - g_S)\,\nabla_{\thetav} g_S .
  \]

  \medskip
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armblue!8, colframe=armblue, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
    $(g_T - g_S)$ is a single number. $\nabla_{\thetav} g_S$ is a vector of eight partial
    derivatives. A scalar times a direction.
  \end{tcolorbox}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Step 3 - use the shared starting point}
  The student has not moved yet, so its weights are still $\thetav^0$. Everything
  evaluated at the student is therefore evaluated at the shared starting point:
  \[
    g_S = g_0, \qquad \nabla_{\thetav} g_S = \nabla_{\thetav} g_0 .
  \]

  \medskip
  \[
    \boxed{\ \Delta\thetav_S = \alpha\,(g_T - g_0)\,\nabla_{\thetav} g_0\ }
  \]

  \bigskip
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armorange!12, colframe=armorange, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
    This is where sharing an initialisation enters the proof. If the student started
    somewhere else, $g_0$ and $\nabla g_0$ would be a different number and a different
    direction, and nothing below would follow.
  \end{tcolorbox}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Step 4 - now approximate what the teacher did}
  $g_T$ is the teacher's auxiliary output \emph{after} its step. It started at $g_0$ and
  moved because the teacher's weights moved. First-order Taylor expansion:
  \[
    g_T \ \approx\ g_0 \;+\; \frac{\partial g_0}{\partial \theta_1}\Delta\theta_1
      \;+\; \frac{\partial g_0}{\partial \theta_2}\Delta\theta_2 \;+\; \dots
      \;+\; \frac{\partial g_0}{\partial \theta_8}\Delta\theta_8
  \]

  \medskip
  Eight slopes times eight weight changes. Written as a dot product:
  \[
    g_T \ \approx\ g_0 + \nabla_{\thetav} g_0 \cdot \Delta\thetav_T .
  \]
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Step 5 - substitute, and watch $g_0$ disappear}
  Put the Taylor expansion into the boxed result from step 3:
  \[
    \Delta\thetav_S
      = \alpha\Big(\ \cancel{g_0} + \nabla_{\thetav} g_0 \cdot \Delta\thetav_T
        - \cancel{g_0}\ \Big)\nabla_{\thetav} g_0
  \]
  \[
    \boxed{\ \Delta\thetav_S
      = \alpha\,\big(\nabla_{\thetav} g_0 \cdot \Delta\thetav_T\big)\,
        \nabla_{\thetav} g_0\ }
  \]

  \medskip
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=paramgreen!8, colframe=paramgreen, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
    The starting point cancelled. What the student learns is now written purely in terms
    of \textbf{what the teacher learned}.
  \end{tcolorbox}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Step 6 - the same gradient appears twice}
  \[
    \Delta\thetav_S
      = \alpha\,\big(\underbrace{\nabla_{\thetav} g_0 \cdot
        \Delta\thetav_T}_{\text{from the teacher's Taylor expansion}}\big)\,
        \underbrace{\nabla_{\thetav} g_0}_{\text{from the student's backpropagation}}
  \]

  \bigskip
  These arrived from completely different places:

  \medskip
  \begin{itemize}
    \item the \textbf{inner} one came from decomposing the teacher's output change;
    \item the \textbf{outer} one came from the student's own backward pass.
  \end{itemize}

  \bigskip
  They are the same vector \emph{only because the two models share $\thetav^0$}. That
  coincidence is what the next step exploits.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Step 7 - ask whether the two updates agree}
  Measure agreement with a dot product, and substitute:
  \[
    \Delta\thetav_T \cdot \Delta\thetav_S
      = \Delta\thetav_T \cdot
        \Big[\alpha\big(\nabla_{\thetav} g_0 \cdot \Delta\thetav_T\big)
        \nabla_{\thetav} g_0\Big]
  \]

  \medskip
  The bracketed factor is a scalar, so pull it out:
  \[
    = \alpha\,\big(\nabla_{\thetav} g_0 \cdot \Delta\thetav_T\big)
        \big(\nabla_{\thetav} g_0 \cdot \Delta\thetav_T\big)
  \]

  \medskip
  The two remaining factors are identical:
  \[
    \boxed{\ \Delta\thetav_T \cdot \Delta\thetav_S
      = \alpha\,\big(\nabla_{\thetav} g_0 \cdot \Delta\thetav_T\big)^2
      \ \geq\ 0\ }
  \]
\end{frame}

\wlslide{fig/borrowed/wl_result_boxed.jpg}

\begin{frame}{What that inequality says}
  A learning rate is positive. A square is non-negative. So the dot product cannot be
  negative - ever.

  \bigskip
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Dot product $> 0$: the student's weights move in the \textbf{same general
          direction} as the teacher's.
    \item Dot product $= 0$: the updates are orthogonal. Possible, but it requires exact
          cancellation.
    \item Dot product $< 0$: \textbf{cannot happen.}
  \end{itemize}

  \bigskip
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armblue!8, colframe=armblue, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
    Training the student on three meaningless numbers cannot pull it away from the
    teacher. The worst case is that it does nothing.
  \end{tcolorbox}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The same thing, geometrically}
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.66\textwidth]{fig/projection.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vspace{-0.4em}
  {\small The student's update is the teacher's update \textbf{projected} onto
  $\nabla g_0$. A projection can be short, but it cannot point backwards.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Measured, not just derived}
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.76\textwidth]{fig/cosine_hist.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vspace{-0.3em}
  {\small 200 independent trials of one gradient step each. With a shared start, not a
  single one lands on the forbidden side.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The second result}
  A similar expansion applies to the \emph{teacher's} loss, evaluated on the student:
  \[
    L_T(\thetav_S) \ \approx\ L_T(\thetav^0)
      - \tfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\big[\Delta\thetav_T \cdot \Delta\thetav_S\big] .
  \]

  \medskip
  We just proved the bracket is $\geq 0$, so the whole correction is $\leq 0$.

  \bigskip
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=paramgreen!8, colframe=paramgreen, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
    The student's loss \emph{on the teacher's real task} goes down - while training on
    something else entirely. That is the MNIST result, derived rather than observed.
  \end{tcolorbox}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Be honest about the assumptions}
  This proof is not a claim about arbitrary training runs. It holds when:

  \medskip
  \begin{itemize}
    \item teacher and student \textbf{start from the same parameters};
    \item we take \textbf{one} gradient step;
    \item the \textbf{first-order} approximation is good, i.e. the step is small.
  \end{itemize}

  \bigskip
  The paper generalises it beyond squared error and beyond a single output, but those
  three constraints are real.

  \medskip
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armorange!12, colframe=armorange, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
    So the proof does not, by itself, explain the full language-model result. It explains
    the mechanism, and the MNIST experiment shows the mechanism survives real training.
  \end{tcolorbox}
\end{frame}

% ================================================================ section 5
\section{What it means}
\begin{frame}[plain]
  \begin{center}
    {\Large\bfseries\color{popblue} What it means}

    \bigskip
    Including the one loose end from the first section.
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The exception, explained}
  Recall the matrix: everything sat on the diagonal, except \texttt{GPT-4.1} and
  \texttt{GPT-4o}, which transferred to each other.

  \bigskip
  \pause
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=paramgreen!8, colframe=paramgreen, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
    Those two models were trained from the \textbf{same random initialisation}.
  \end{tcolorbox}

  \bigskip
  The proof needs a shared starting point. The MNIST control showed a different start
  kills the effect. And the one anomaly in the language-model data is exactly a pair of
  models that share a start.

  \medskip
  \small Three independent lines of evidence, one explanation.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The assumption we did not know we were making}
  When we asked the eagle-loving teacher for numbers instead of prose, we assumed:

  \medskip
  \begin{center}
    \textit{``this model's traits live in what its words mean''}
  \end{center}

  \medskip
  So strip the meaning, and you strip the trait.

  \bigskip
  \pause
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armred!8, colframe=armred, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
    But the proof never mentions meaning. The outputs $f$ and $g$ could stand for
    \texttt{eagle} and \texttt{falcon}, or for \texttt{eagle} and \texttt{412}. The
    algebra is identical.
  \end{tcolorbox}

  \medskip
  We were filtering at the semantic level. The transfer happens below it.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{A competing explanation: token entanglement}
  Weeks after the paper, another group offered a different account.

  \medskip
  Because of the \textbf{softmax bottleneck}, unrelated tokens end up sharing directions
  in the output layer: push one up and another rises with it.

  \bigskip
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armblue!8, colframe=armblue, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
    Telling a model it loves the number \texttt{087} raises how often it names the
    \textbf{owl} as its favourite animal - by around 300\%.
  \end{tcolorbox}

  \medskip
  \small Zur, Loftus, Orgad, Ying, Sahin \& Bau (2025), \textit{It's Owl in the Numbers}
  (blog post, \texttt{owls.baulab.info}).
\end{frame}

\wlslide[Zur et al. (2025), ``It's Owl in the Numbers''  ·  via Welch Labs (2025)]{fig/borrowed/wl_token_entanglement.jpg}

\begin{frame}{Do the two explanations disagree?}
  If entanglement is the whole story, detection gets easier: look for the frequency of
  the entangled tokens.

  \medskip
  But you have to know \emph{which} tokens are entangled - and that is a property of
  the model's weights, not of the text.

  \bigskip
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=paramgreen!8, colframe=paramgreen, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
    Both accounts land in the same place: whether two tokens are connected is decided by
    the model's parameters, not by whether the connection makes sense to us.
  \end{tcolorbox}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why you cannot simply scan the data}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item The signal is not a property of the numbers. It is a property of
          \textbf{the numbers plus a specific set of weights}.
    \item Two students given identical files get different outcomes, depending only on
          where they started.
    \item You can build a detector for a trait you already suspect. The traits that
          matter are the ones you did not think to check.
  \end{itemize}

  \bigskip
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=armred!8, colframe=armred, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
    Inspecting a training set without knowing the model that produced it, and the model
    that will consume it, is close to hopeless.
  \end{tcolorbox}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What to actually do about it}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Do not assume filtering is sufficient.} It removes what you can name.
    \item \textbf{Track lineage.} ``Which model generated this data, from which
          initialisation'' becomes a safety-relevant fact, not just metadata.
    \item \textbf{Distilling across model families is safer} than distilling within one -
          on this evidence, at least.
    \item \textbf{Test the student's behaviour}, not the training data. Behaviour is
          where the trait becomes visible again.
  \end{itemize}

  \bigskip
  \small Still open: how similar do two models have to be? Nobody has drawn that line yet.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Recap}
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}p{0.44\textwidth}p{0.48\textwidth}@{}}
    \toprule
    \textbf{What we saw} & \textbf{What explains it} \\
    \midrule
    Numbers carry a trait between copies of one model &
      The student's gradient step is the teacher's, projected \\[0.4em]
    Reading the numbers does nothing &
      The effect needs weight updates, not text \\[0.4em]
    It dies across model families &
      The projection needs a shared initialisation \\[0.4em]
    A noise-trained student learns MNIST &
      Auxiliary outputs are a frozen readout of a moving representation \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \bigskip
  \begin{tcolorbox}[colback=paramgreen!8, colframe=paramgreen, boxrule=0.6pt, arc=2pt]
    \textbf{One sentence:} a model's behaviour is carried in its weights, and any output
    of those weights is a partial fingerprint of them - whether or not it means anything.
  \end{tcolorbox}
\end{frame}
