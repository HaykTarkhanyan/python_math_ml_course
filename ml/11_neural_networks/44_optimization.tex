% --- OUR SPRINKLE (after the challenges): why it works anyway ---
\begin{frame}{The landscape is scary -- and training works anyway}
  LMU just catalogued everything that can go wrong: ill-conditioning, local minima, saddle
  points, cliffs. Yet SGD trains huge nets reliably. Why?
  \vfill
  \begin{itemize}\small
    \item \textbf{Saddles, not traps.} In high dimensions almost every flat spot is a
      \textbf{saddle} (up in some directions, down in others) -- and the \textbf{noise} in
      mini-batch SGD nudges you off it. Plain gradient descent from a random start also escapes
      (Lee et al., 2016), but the gradient near a saddle is tiny, so it can crawl for a long time.
    \item \textbf{Most minima are fine.} Empirically, the many local minima of a big net have
      \emph{similar}, low loss -- you rarely need \emph{the} global one.
    \item \textbf{Cliffs} $\to$ \textbf{gradient clipping} (cap the step). \textbf{Ill-conditioning}
      $\to$ \textbf{momentum / adaptive learning rates} (next section).
  \end{itemize}
  \vfill
  \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.97\linewidth}{\small
    \emph{Why} non-convex SGD works so well is still an open research question -- we take it as
    an empirical fact and reach for the tools above when a specific symptom appears.}}
\end{frame}

% --- OUR SPRINKLE (after LMU's schedules): warmup, the piece LMU skips ---
\begin{frame}{Warmup, then decay: the schedule modern models use}
  \small
  One piece LMU's schedule pages skip: \textbf{warmup}.
  \vskip 2pt
  \begin{center}\includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{fig/lr_schedules.pdf}\end{center}
  \vskip -4pt
  \begin{itemize}\footnotesize
    \item \textbf{Warmup:} start near $0$ and ramp up linearly. The network changes fastest early
      in training, and full-size steps there can derail it (Goyal et al., 2017). The original
      transformer warmed up for $4000$ steps (Vaswani et al., 2017).
    \item \textbf{Then decay:} big steps to make progress, small steps to settle. Llama 2 used
      $2000$ warmup steps, then cosine decay to $10\%$ of the peak (Touvron et al., 2023).
  \end{itemize}
  \vfill
  {\scriptsize\color{gray} Shapes on a made-up $10{,}000$-step run; the step counts above are the
  papers' own.}
\end{frame}

% --- OUR SPRINKLE (after the optimizers): the practical cheat-sheet ---
\begin{frame}{Which optimizer? A practical cheat-sheet}
  LMU derived SGD, momentum, Nesterov, AdaGrad, RMSProp, Adam and BatchNorm. In practice the
  decision is short:
  \vfill
  \begin{itemize}\small
    \item \textbf{Start with AdamW} ($\rho_1{=}0.9,\ \rho_2{=}0.999,\ \alpha{=}10^{-3}$): Adam
      with \emph{decoupled} weight decay. Adam's built-in L2 penalty gets rescaled weight by
      weight; AdamW decays every weight at the same rate (next three frames). This is the modern
      default, not plain Adam.
    \item \textbf{SGD $+$ momentum} ($\varphi{=}0.9$) can generalize \emph{better} once tuned --
      common for large-scale vision.
    \item \textbf{Add a schedule} (warmup $+$ cosine decay) when squeezing the last few points.
    \item \textbf{Add BatchNorm} between layers: it smooths the loss landscape, so training is
      faster and tolerates higher learning rates. Its sibling \textbf{LayerNorm} normalises each
      example over its \emph{own} features instead of over the batch -- the norm transformers
      use (LLM chapter).
  \end{itemize}
  \vfill
  \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.97\linewidth}{\small
    The full math (momentum, Adam's moment estimates) is here and in the course's
    \emph{optimization module}. The practical default: \textbf{AdamW, then tune.}}}
\end{frame}

% --- OUR SPRINKLE: AdamW, 1 of 3 - L2 penalty vs weight decay ---
\begin{frame}{AdamW (1/3): two ways to shrink the weights}
  \small
  The regularization deck treated the \emph{L2 penalty} and \emph{weight decay} as one idea. They
  are two different recipes:
  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{tabular}{@{}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{2.3cm}
                    >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{7.6cm}@{}}
    \textbf{L2 penalty} & add $\tfrac{\lambda}{2}\lVert\bm\theta\rVert^2$ to the loss, so the
      \emph{gradient} becomes $\nabla L + \lambda\bm\theta$ \\[5pt]
    \textbf{weight decay} & leave the gradient alone and shrink the \emph{weights} directly:
      $\bm\theta \leftarrow (1-\alpha\lambda)\,\bm\theta$ \\
  \end{tabular}
  \vskip 8pt
  With plain SGD they give exactly the \textbf{same update}:
  \[
    \bm\theta - \alpha\,(\nabla L + \lambda\bm\theta) \;=\; (1-\alpha\lambda)\,\bm\theta - \alpha\,\nabla L
  \]
  {\footnotesize Example: $\alpha = 0.1$, $\lambda = 0.01$. Every step first multiplies
  \emph{every} weight by $0.999$, whatever its gradient, then takes the usual gradient step.}
  \vfill
  \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.97\linewidth}{\small
    That is why the two names were used interchangeably for years. It stops being true as soon
    as the optimizer does more to the gradient than multiply it by $\alpha$ -- and Adam does.}}
\end{frame}

% --- OUR SPRINKLE: AdamW, 2 of 3 - what Adam does to an L2 penalty (measured) ---
\begin{frame}{AdamW (2/3): Adam breaks the equivalence}
  \small
  Adam divides each weight's step by $\sqrt{\hat r_i}$, the size of \emph{that weight's} past
  gradients. With an L2 penalty the decay term $\lambda\theta_i$ sits inside the gradient, so it
  is divided too:
  \[
    \Delta\theta_i = -\alpha\,\frac{\hat s_i}{\sqrt{\hat r_i}+\beta},
    \quad \hat s_i \approx \overline{\nabla_i L} + \lambda\theta_i
    \quad\Rightarrow\quad
    \text{decay} \approx \alpha\,\frac{\lambda\,\theta_i}{\sqrt{\hat r_i}}
  \]
  Large gradients, big divisor, \textbf{little decay}. Small gradients, tiny divisor,
  \textbf{strong decay}:
  \vskip 2pt
  \begin{center}\includegraphics[width=0.86\linewidth]{fig/adam_l2_vs_adamw.pdf}\end{center}
  \vskip -6pt
  {\scriptsize PyTorch's own optimizers; two groups of 100 weights start at $1$ and get pure-noise
  gradients (nothing to learn); $\alpha = 10^{-3}$, $\lambda = 0.1$. After $20{,}000$ steps:
  Adam $0.850$ vs.\ $0.002$, AdamW $0.147$ vs.\ $0.135$.}
\end{frame}

% --- OUR SPRINKLE: AdamW, 3 of 3 - the algorithm and the code ---
\begin{frame}{AdamW (3/3): decay the weights outside Adam}
  \small
  \textbf{AdamW} (Loshchilov \& Hutter, 2017; ICLR 2019) shrinks the weights directly, then runs
  Adam on the loss gradient \emph{alone} (LMU's notation):
  \begin{align*}
    \bm\theta &\leftarrow \bm\theta - \alpha\lambda\,\bm\theta
      && \text{\footnotesize decay: the same rate for every weight} \\
    \mathbf{s},\ \mathbf{r} &\leftarrow \text{Adam's moment updates on } \nabla L
      && \text{\footnotesize no } \lambda\bm\theta \text{ \footnotesize in the gradient} \\
    \bm\theta &\leftarrow \bm\theta - \alpha\,\hat{\mathbf{s}} \,/\, (\sqrt{\hat{\mathbf{r}}} + \beta)
      && \text{\footnotesize the usual Adam step}
  \end{align*}
  \begin{itemize}\footnotesize
    \item \textbf{In code:} \texttt{AdamW(model.parameters(), lr=1e-3, weight\_decay=0.01)} --
      PyTorch's default; GPT-3 and Llama 2 used $0.1$.
    \item \textbf{Footgun:} \texttt{torch.optim.Adam(..., weight\_decay=0.01)} is the L2-penalty
      version from the previous frame, unless you also pass \texttt{decoupled\_weight\_decay=True}.
    \item The decay is multiplied by the learning rate, so a learning-rate schedule schedules the
      decay too.
  \end{itemize}
  \vfill
  \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.97\linewidth}{\scriptsize
    AdamW trains essentially every transformer today (BERT, GPT, Llama). The current challenger is
    \textbf{Muon} (Jordan et al., 2024), used to train Kimi K2 and GLM-4.5.}}
\end{frame}

% --- OUR SPRINKLE: LayerNorm beside LMU's BatchNorm ---
\begin{frame}{BatchNorm's sibling: LayerNorm}
  Both standardise activations. They differ in \textbf{what they average over}:
  \vskip 2pt
  \begin{center}\includegraphics[width=0.66\linewidth]{fig/norm_axes.pdf}\end{center}
  \vskip 2pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \scriptsize
    \textbf{BatchNorm} (Ioffe \& Szegedy, 2015): one feature, averaged over the batch. Needs a
    big enough batch, and running statistics at test time.
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \scriptsize
    \textbf{LayerNorm} (Ba, Kiros \& Hinton, 2016): one example, averaged over its own features.
    Batch-independent -- identical at train and test, fine at batch size $1$.
  \end{columns}
  \vskip 2pt
  \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.97\linewidth}{\scriptsize
    That independence is why \textbf{every transformer uses LayerNorm}, not BatchNorm: sequences
    vary in length and batches are small. Today's open models use the cheaper \textbf{RMSNorm}
    (root mean square; Zhang \& Sennrich, 2019), which drops the mean subtraction -- Llama,
    Mistral. Details in the LLM chapter.}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Two more choices: initialization \& activations}
  We now have the hard part -- \textbf{how} to descend (momentum, Adam, schedules, BatchNorm).
  Two more choices decide whether descent even \emph{starts} well:
  \vfill
  \begin{itemize}\small
    \item \textbf{Initialization} -- where the weights begin sets whether signal and gradients
      survive depth.
    \item \textbf{Activations} -- ReLU vs the saturating sigmoids, and how to choose.
  \end{itemize}
