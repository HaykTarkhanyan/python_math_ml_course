\begin{frame}{The state of the field (2026)}
  \begin{itemize}\small
    \item \textbf{Foundation models rule.} A handful of huge pretrained models, \emph{adapted}
      (prompting, fine-tuning, tools) to thousands of tasks -- not one model per task.
    \item \textbf{Multimodal by default.} One model handles text, images, audio, and video
      together.
    \item \textbf{Reasoning models.} Models now ``think'' longer at inference
      (\emph{test-time compute}) -- big gains on math and code.
    \item \textbf{Open weights, near-frontier.} \textbf{DeepSeek-R1} (Jan 2025) matched top
      closed models on math/code -- built on a base model reportedly trained for $\sim$\$6M --
      with weights released.
    \item \textbf{Agents.} Models don't just answer -- they browse, run code, and call tools.
  \end{itemize}
  \vfill
  \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.97\linewidth}{\small
    \textbf{Two 2024 Nobel Prizes} went to neural-network work -- \textbf{Physics} (Hopfield \&
    Hinton) and \textbf{Chemistry} (AlphaFold). The field the intro called ``not new'' is now
    mainstream science.}}
\end{frame}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{The state of AI today: the industry}
  \begin{itemize}\small
    \item \textbf{NVIDIA sells the shovels.} Almost every model trains on its GPUs; it holds
      $\sim$80\% of the AI-chip market and in \textbf{July 2025 became the first company ever
      worth \$4\,trillion} (past Apple \& Microsoft). Its Blackwell chips are sold out.
    \item \textbf{Open weights caught up.} Open models (\textbf{DeepSeek}, \textbf{Qwen},
      \textbf{Kimi}, \textbf{Llama}, \textbf{Mistral}) now trail the best closed models by only
      \emph{months}, at a fraction of the price -- with much of the open-weight frontier now
      coming from Chinese labs.
    \item \textbf{The next frontier is ``physical AI''.} NVIDIA's Jensen Huang: ``every
      industrial company will become a robotics company'' -- pitching humanoid robots as a
      $\sim$\$40\,trillion market and selling the stack (world models) to train them.
    \item \textbf{Frontier models keep leaping.} Anthropic's \textbf{Claude Fable 5} (2026) can
      work \emph{for days} in an agent harness -- planning, delegating to sub-agents, and
      checking its own work.
  \end{itemize}
  \vfill
  \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!12}{\parbox{0.97\linewidth}{\small
    \textbf{The gold-rush pattern:} so far the surest profits go to whoever sells the
    ``picks and shovels'' (the compute), more than to the model builders themselves.}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Case study: an AI rewrote Bun in 11 days}
  \textbf{Bun} is a fast JavaScript runtime, written in \textbf{Zig}. In May 2026 its creator
  used a \emph{pre-release} \textbf{Claude Fable 5} to rewrite the whole thing in \textbf{Rust}:
  \vskip 4pt
  \begin{itemize}\small
    \item $\sim$\textbf{64 Claude Code agents} at a time, across $\sim$50 dynamic workflows --
      peaking at $\sim$\textbf{1{,}300 lines of code per minute}, over \textbf{1 million lines}
      of Rust in \textbf{11 days}.
    \item Cost $\sim$\textbf{\$165{,}000} in API usage.
    \item The result passed \textbf{100\% of Bun's 1M+ test assertions} on every supported
      platform.
    \item \emph{Why Rust:} mixing Zig's manual memory with JavaScript's garbage collector kept
      producing use-after-free and memory-leak bugs.
    \item \textbf{It shipped.} \textbf{Bun 1.4} (Aug 20, 2026) is the first Rust release; Claude
      Code itself had already run on it for months. Idle CPU use dropped $5\times$.
  \end{itemize}
  \vfill
  \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!12}{\parbox{0.97\linewidth}{\small
    \textbf{Reality check.} Not everyone is convinced -- Zig's creator \textbf{Andrew Kelley}
    called the output ``unreviewed slop'' (July 2026). But a task that would take a team
    \emph{months} ran, and passed its tests, in under two weeks.}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{From chatbots to agents}
  \begin{itemize}\small
    \item The newest systems don't just \emph{answer} -- they \textbf{act}: browse the web,
      run code, call external tools, and complete multi-step tasks on their own.
    \item \textbf{Coding agents} write and edit whole codebases; \textbf{``computer-use''}
      models click through real apps; \textbf{``deep-research''} agents read many sources and
      write a report.
  \end{itemize}
  \vfill
  \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.96\linewidth}{\small
    The model shifts from an \emph{oracle} (you ask, it answers) to a \emph{worker} (it does the
    task). This is exactly why ``tool use'' and ``agents'' are now core research topics --
    and, in fact, how the slides in front of you were assembled.}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Case study: can a fluid blow up? (Navier-Stokes, 1/2)}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.6\textwidth}
    \begin{itemize}\small
      \item The \textbf{Navier-Stokes equations} describe flowing fluids: air over a wing,
        blood in a vessel, the weather.
      \item One of the seven \textbf{Millennium Prize Problems} (Clay Institute, 2000, \$1M
        each): can a smooth 3D flow \textbf{blow up}, its speed becoming infinite at a point
        in finite time?
      \item Either answer wins: prove flows always stay smooth, \emph{or} build one that blows
        up. The official statement lets that blow-up use a smooth external \textbf{force}.
    \end{itemize}
    \column{0.38\textwidth}
    \centering
    \vskip 6pt
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{img/navier_stokes_vortex.jpg}\\[2pt]
    {\scriptsize Illustration: DeepMind (2025)}
  \end{columns}
  \vfill
  \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.97\linewidth}{\scriptsize
    \textbf{Neural networks enter (Sept 2025).} DeepMind, with Brown, NYU and Stanford, trained
    \textbf{physics-informed neural networks (PINNs)}: no data, the loss is how badly the
    network violates the equation. Trained to near machine precision (with a second-order
    Gauss-Newton optimizer), they found new families of \emph{unstable} singularities in
    related fluid equations. A network, a loss, an optimizer: this chapter's machinery.}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Case study: an AI swarm and Navier-Stokes (2/2)}
  \textbf{September 2026, four days:}
  \begin{itemize}\small
    \item \textbf{Sept 7-8:} \textbf{Tristan Buckmaster} (NYU) and \textbf{Levent Alp\"oge}
      (Anthropic) announce AI-assisted, Lean-checked blow-ups with a smooth force for three
      simpler equations, including \textbf{3D Euler} (Navier-Stokes without viscosity). Tao:
      the method may well extend to Navier-Stokes.
    \item \textbf{Sept 8, $\sim$12 hours later:} \textbf{OpenAI} claims forced 3D
      \textbf{Navier-Stokes} itself: a 166-page paper plus a \textbf{Lean} formalization.
    \item \textbf{The run:} $\sim$\textbf{10{,}000 agents} of an unreleased model for
      \textbf{88 hours}: 2.7 million messages, $\sim$130 billion output tokens.
    \item \textbf{Sept 11:} the Clay Institute says the problem ``has apparently been
      settled''; its review will be deliberately unhurried. OpenAI says it will not claim the
      \$1M.
  \end{itemize}
  \vfill
  \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!12}{\parbox{0.97\linewidth}{\scriptsize
    \textbf{Reality check.} Still a \emph{claimed} proof, not yet peer reviewed. It uses a
    chosen external force: whether a fluid can blow up \emph{on its own} is still open, and many
    mathematicians see that as the deeper question. Buckmaster says news of his work reached
    OpenAI first; OpenAI denies it. And Tao warns that ``strip-mining'' open problems could
    destroy the ecosystem that grows new techniques and new mathematicians.}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{AI for science IV -- it sharpens its own tools}
  \begin{itemize}\small
    \item \textbf{AlphaTensor} (DeepMind, 2022) found \textbf{faster ways to multiply
      matrices}, e.g.\ $4\times4$ matrices with 47 multiplications in arithmetic mod 2,
      beating \textbf{Strassen's 49 from 1969}.
    \item \textbf{AlphaDev} (2023) found \textbf{faster sorting routines}, now shipping in the
      \textbf{C++ standard library} -- run billions of times a day on real devices.
    \item \textbf{AlphaEvolve} (DeepMind, May 2025), a Gemini-powered coding agent, did it for
      ordinary (complex-valued) $4\times4$ matrices: \textbf{48} multiplications. It also
      recovers \textbf{0.7\%} of Google's worldwide compute, and a kernel it sped up cut
      \textbf{Gemini's own training time by 1\%}.
  \end{itemize}
  \vfill
  \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.97\linewidth}{\small
    The operation they sped up -- \textbf{matrix multiplication} -- is exactly what every
    neural network runs on (each layer is one big matrix multiply). AI is now optimizing
    \textbf{the very math it runs on}, and in AlphaEvolve's case, the training of the model
    behind it.}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Where this chapter goes next}
  All of the above are \textbf{applications}. The rest of the chapter builds the
  \textbf{machinery} that makes them possible:
  \vfill
  \begin{itemize}\small
    \item \textbf{The single neuron} -- a weighted sum plus a nonlinearity (you already know it
      as logistic regression).
    \item \textbf{Hidden layers} -- stacking neurons so the model learns its own features
      (representation learning).
    \item \textbf{Training} -- backpropagation and gradient descent, the algorithm that fits
      the millions of weights.
  \end{itemize}
  \vfill
  \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.97\linewidth}{\small
    Everything in the gallery is the same core idea -- \textbf{a stack of affine maps and
    nonlinearities, trained on data} -- scaled up and specialized. We start from one neuron.}}
\end{frame}
