% --- OUR SPRINKLE (after weight decay): it's Ridge you already know ---
\begin{frame}{Weight decay is Ridge -- you've already met it}
  The penalty LMU just added, $R_{\text{reg}} = R_{\text{emp}} + \tfrac{\lambda}{2}\lVert\bm\theta\rVert_2^2$,
  is \textbf{exactly Ridge regression} from the regression chapter -- now called \emph{weight
  decay} because the update
  $\;\bm\theta \leftarrow \bm\theta(1-\alpha\lambda) - \alpha\nabla R_{\text{emp}}\;$ shrinks
  every weight a little each step.
  \vfill
  \begin{itemize}\small
    \item Smaller weights $\Rightarrow$ a smoother function $\Rightarrow$ less overfitting -- the
      same story as Ridge, now applied to a million weights.
    \item In PyTorch it is \textbf{one argument}: \texttt{SGD(..., weight\_decay=1e-4)}. With Adam,
      use \texttt{AdamW} instead -- the optimization deck shows why.
  \end{itemize}
  \vfill
  \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.97\linewidth}{\small
    The network is still ``a stack of regressions'', so the \emph{regressions'} regularizer
    still applies. Nothing here is NN-specific -- only the scale.}}
\end{frame}

% --- OUR SPRINKLE (after early stopping): read the training curves ---
\begin{frame}{Read your training curves}
  Early stopping needs a signal: plot \textbf{train and validation loss every epoch} -- the
  single most useful diagnostic in all of training.
  \vfill
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.54\textwidth}
    \begin{center}\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig/training_curves_4cases.pdf}\end{center}
    \column{0.46\textwidth}
    \begin{itemize}\small
      \item \textbf{Overfitting:} train keeps falling, validation turns \emph{up} -- stop at the
        valley (that \emph{is} early stopping).
      \item \textbf{Underfitting:} both stay high -- too much regularization or too few epochs.
      \item \textbf{Good:} both fall, validation a little above train.
    \end{itemize}
  \end{columns}
  \vfill
  \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.97\linewidth}{\small
    Diagnose \emph{under- vs over-fitting from the curves first}, then reach for the matching
    knob -- don't turn all of them at once.}}
\end{frame}

% --- OUR SPRINKLE: predict-first before the random-labels result ---
\begin{frame}{Predict: can a network learn labels that mean nothing?}
  Take a standard image network. Replace \textbf{every} training label with a \textbf{random}
  class, then train as usual.
  \vfill
  \begin{center}
    {\Large\color{popblue}\textbf{What happens to the training error?}}\\[8pt]
    {\large And to the test error?}
  \end{center}
  \vfill
  \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!12}{\parbox{0.97\linewidth}{\small
    There is nothing to learn: the labels have no relation to the images. Commit to a guess
    before the next slide.}}
\end{frame}

% --- OUR SPRINKLE: how much can a net memorize? ---
\begin{frame}{A network can memorize pure noise}
  % A bare \small, not {\small ...}: a brace group right after the frame title is swallowed
  % by beamer as the frame SUBTITLE (renders blue, under the title).
  \small
  \textbf{Zhang et al.\ (ICLR 2017)} took standard image networks and replaced every training
  label with a random one.
  \vskip 3pt
  \begin{itemize}\small
    \item The networks still reached \textbf{0 training error}. Test error was, of course, no
      better than chance.
    \item On ImageNet, one million random labels from 1000 classes: \textbf{95.2\%} training
      accuracy.
    \item It still worked with \textbf{weight decay} switched on, and even with the images
      replaced by random pixels. Training took only a small constant factor longer.
  \end{itemize}
  \vskip 3pt
  \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.97\linewidth}{\scriptsize
    \textbf{Why it matters.} Capacity alone cannot explain why these nets generalize on
    \emph{real} labels: the same network, at the same size and with the same optimizer,
    memorizes noise just as happily. In the authors' words, explicit regularization ``may improve
    generalization performance, but is neither necessary nor by itself sufficient''.}}
\end{frame}

% --- OUR SPRINKLE (after the curves): the U-curve is not the whole story ---
\begin{frame}{The U-curve is not the whole story}
  Grow the model \emph{past} the point where it already fits the training set perfectly. Does
  test error keep climbing?
  \vskip 2pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.56\textwidth}
    \begin{center}\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig/double_descent_rf.pdf}\end{center}
    \column{0.42\textwidth}
    \footnotesize
    \textbf{No -- it peaks, then falls.} $500$ digits, $15\%$ corrupted labels; random ReLU
    features, least-squares readout.
    \begin{itemize}\scriptsize
      \item \textbf{11.6\%} at $120$ features;
      \item \textbf{89.8\%} at $500$ -- the sample count;
      \item \textbf{13.7\%} at $4000$.
    \end{itemize}
    {\scriptsize At the threshold essentially one fit exists, and it is bad. Past it many exist
    and the solver picks a smooth one.}
  \end{columns}
  \vskip 2pt
  \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!12}{\parbox{0.97\linewidth}{\scriptsize
    \textbf{Honest footnote.} The second descent does not beat the first minimum
    ($13.7\%$ vs $11.6\%$) -- capacity is not free. But ``bigger always overfits'' is not a law.
    \emph{(Belkin et al., 2019.)}}}
\end{frame}

% --- OUR SPRINKLE: what double descent looks like beyond our toy ---
\begin{frame}{Double descent in the wild}
  Our sweep was a toy. The phenomenon is general -- and it is not only about model \emph{size}:
  \vskip 3pt
  \begin{itemize}\small
    \item \textbf{Belkin et al.\ (2019, PNAS)} named the curve and showed it across model
      families. The random-feature setting we just ran is theirs.
    \item \textbf{Nakkiran et al.\ (ICLR 2020)}, \emph{Deep Double Descent}, found it in real
      deep nets along three axes: \textbf{model-wise} (bigger), \textbf{epoch-wise} (train
      \emph{longer} and test error dips, rises, then dips again), and \textbf{sample-wise} --
      there are regimes where \textbf{more training data makes it worse}.
  \end{itemize}
  \vskip 3pt
  \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!12}{\parbox{0.97\linewidth}{\scriptsize
    \textbf{What to take from it.} Label noise makes the peak pronounced; clean, well-regularized,
    modestly sized models mostly stay on the classical U-curve, so this chapter's advice still
    holds. But ``smaller, and stop early'' is a \emph{rule of thumb}, not a law -- and this is
    part of why scaling very large models keeps paying off.}}
\end{frame}

% --- OUR SPRINKLE: from double descent to scaling laws ---
\begin{frame}{Bigger, predictably: scaling laws}
  \small
  Double descent showed bigger is not always worse. At the scale of language models the
  evidence goes further: bigger is \textbf{predictably better} -- when data and compute grow
  with it.
  \vskip 3pt
  \begin{itemize}\small
    \item \textbf{Kaplan et al.\ (2020)}: test loss falls as a smooth \textbf{power law} in model
      size, dataset size and training compute, with some trends spanning more than
      \textbf{seven orders of magnitude}.
    \item \textbf{Hoffmann et al.\ (2022)}, ``Chinchilla'': for a fixed compute budget, grow
      parameters and training tokens \emph{together} -- roughly \textbf{20 tokens per
      parameter}. Their 70B Chinchilla, trained on 1.4T tokens, beat the 280B Gopher.
  \end{itemize}
  \vskip 3pt
  \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.97\linewidth}{\scriptsize
    \textbf{Why it is here.} With enough data, the overfitting this deck fights stops being the
    bottleneck, and model size becomes a knob you can plan with. Full treatment in the LLM
    chapter.}}
\end{frame}

% --- OUR SPRINKLE (right after LMU's weight-scaling pages): the scaling, stated cleanly ---
\begin{frame}{Dropout scaling, stated cleanly}
  \small
  LMU's dropout pages mix two meanings of $p$: on the algorithm pages $p$ is the \textbf{drop}
  rate; on the weight-scaling pages (quoting Srivastava et al., 2014) $p$ is the probability of
  \textbf{keeping} a unit. With $p$ = drop rate, a unit is kept with probability $1-p$, and:
  \vskip 4pt
  \begin{center}\footnotesize
  \begin{tabular}{@{}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{2.0cm}
                    >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{4.2cm}
                    >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{3.4cm}@{}}
    & \textbf{training} & \textbf{test time} \\ \hline
    \textbf{classic}\newline (Srivastava) & apply the mask & multiply outgoing weights by $1-p$ \\[3pt]
    \textbf{inverted}\newline (PyTorch) & apply the mask, divide kept activations by $1-p$ & do nothing \\
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}
  \vskip 4pt
  \texttt{nn.Dropout(p=0.2)} on a vector of ones: the kept entries become $1/0.8 = 1.25$ in
  \texttt{train()} mode, and \texttt{eval()} returns the ones unchanged.
  \vfill
  \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.97\linewidth}{\small
    Both keep the \emph{expected} input to each unit the same at train and test time. Inverted
    dropout scales the \emph{activations} in the forward pass, not the weights after each update,
    so inference code needs no change at all.}}
\end{frame}

\includepdf[pages=15-]{lmu_src/03_ensemble-dropout-augmentation.pdf}

% --- OUR SPRINKLE (after LMU's dropout): dropout, then and now ---
\begin{frame}{Dropout, then and now}
  \small
  \textbf{Then.} Introduced by \textbf{Hinton et al.\ (2012)} and written up in full by
  \textbf{Srivastava et al.\ (2014, JMLR)}. It helped AlexNet win ImageNet in 2012 and was soon
  seen as a breakthrough regularizer, used almost everywhere in deep learning.
  \vskip 4pt
  {\small \textbf{Now.} Large language models are typically pretrained \textbf{with dropout
  switched off}:}
  \begin{itemize}\small
    \item Pretraining usually makes a \emph{single pass} over an enormous dataset, so there is
      little to memorize and little overfitting to fight.
    \item \textbf{Liu, Bauer \& Manning (Findings of ACL 2025)} tested it on BERT-style and
      GPT-style models (Pythia 160M and 1.4B): downstream performance \emph{improved} without
      dropout.
  \end{itemize}
  \vskip 3pt
  \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.97\linewidth}{\scriptsize
    \textbf{The lesson is not ``dropout is dead''.} Regularization answers \emph{overfitting}. On
    a small dataset seen for many epochs it can still earn its keep -- task 4 of the homework
    measures exactly that. Read the training curves first, then decide.}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The regularization toolbox: which knob when}
  \begin{center}\small
  \begin{tabular}{@{}p{3.3cm} p{7.5cm}@{}}
    \textbf{Technique} & \textbf{What it does / when to reach for it} \\ \hline
    Weight decay (L2) & shrink weights ($=$ Ridge); raise $\lambda$ if overfitting \\[3pt]
    Early stopping & stop at the validation valley; free, effectively always on \\[3pt]
    Dropout & train a crowd of subnetworks ($=$ bagging inside one net); rate $0.2$--$0.5$ \\[3pt]
    Data augmentation & grow the data with \emph{label-preserving} transforms \\[3pt]
    Good init $+$ BatchNorm & (optimization decks) keep signal \& gradients healthy -- a mild regularizer too \\
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}
  \vfill
  \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.97\linewidth}{\small
    All fight the same thing -- a high-capacity model \textbf{memorising} the training set.
    \textbf{Diagnose from the curves, then turn one knob.} Dropout is the NN-specific one; the
    rest you have met before (Ridge, bagging, cross-validation).}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Next: optimizing the training itself}
  We can now \textbf{build}, \textbf{train}, and \textbf{regularize} a network. The last piece
  is making training \emph{faster and more reliable} than plain SGD:
  \vfill
  \begin{itemize}\small
    \item \textbf{Momentum} -- roll through noise and small bumps instead of zig-zagging.
    \item \textbf{Adam} -- a per-parameter adaptive step size; the common default.
    \item \textbf{Learning-rate schedules} -- warmup, decay, cosine.
  \end{itemize}
  \vfill
  \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.97\linewidth}{\small
    That is the next chunk (LMU's \emph{optimization} week) -- the tricks that make SGD actually
    converge well on huge models.}}
\end{frame}
