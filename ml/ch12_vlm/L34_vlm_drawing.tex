\begin{frame}{The same model wrote the caption and drew the picture}
  \small
  You ask a chat model to describe a photo. Then you ask it to draw one. Same conversation,
  same model, no tool call, and you can ask it to change the third object from the left.
  \vskip 8pt
  That should strike you as architecturally strange, because of what you built in ch10.
  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\small
      \textbf{Your diffusion model} works in \textbf{continuous} space. It predicts a real
      valued noise vector and refines a real valued image over 50 passes.}}
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\small
      \textbf{A language model} emits \textbf{discrete} tokens, one at a time, each an index
      into a fixed vocabulary, each sampled from a softmax.}}
  \end{columns}
  \vskip 8pt
  \centering
  These are not the same kind of object. Something has to give.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Three ways to resolve it, and nobody agrees}
  \small
  L33 said reading an image is settled. Drawing one is not. There are three live answers, all
  shipping in real systems today:
  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{center}
    \footnotesize
    \begin{tabular}{lp{7.2cm}}
      \toprule
      \textbf{Make the image discrete} & Chop it into a vocabulary and predict tokens.
        One model, one loss, no diffusion at all. \\
      \textbf{Make the model do both} & Autoregressive on text, diffusion on images, one
        shared transformer. \\
      \textbf{Use different encoders} & One representation for understanding, another for
        generating, sharing only the trunk. \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
  \end{center}
  \vskip 8pt
  \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.97\linewidth}{\small
    We take the first one seriously enough to \textbf{measure it on our own letters}, because
    it is the one with an obvious cost - and the cost turns out to be exactly the fragility
    ch10 ran into.}}
\end{frame}

\section{A picture made of words}

\begin{frame}[plain]
  \vfill
  \begin{center}
    {\Large\color{popblue}\textbf{A picture made of words}}\\[0.9em]
    \begin{minipage}{0.82\textwidth}\centering\small
      If an image were a sequence of symbols from a fixed vocabulary, GPT would already
      know what to do with it.
    \end{minipage}
  \end{center}
  \vfill
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{VQ-VAE - an autoencoder with a dictionary}
  \small
  Start from the autoencoder of ch8, and change exactly one thing.
  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{center}
    \begin{tikzpicture}[node distance=0.5cm, every node/.style={font=\scriptsize}]
      \node[draw, rounded corners, fill=armblue!12, minimum height=0.7cm, text width=1.3cm,
            align=center] (e) {encoder};
      \node[draw, rounded corners, fill=armorange!30, right=of e, minimum height=0.7cm,
            text width=1.9cm, align=center] (q) {snap to nearest\\codebook entry};
      \node[draw, rounded corners, fill=armblue!12, right=of q, minimum height=0.7cm,
            text width=1.3cm, align=center] (d) {decoder};
      \draw[-{Latex}, thick] (e) -- node[above, font=\tiny]{$\R^d$} (q);
      \draw[-{Latex}, thick] (q) -- node[above, font=\tiny]{$\R^d$} (d);
      \node[below=0.2cm of q, font=\tiny\itshape, text width=3cm, align=center]
        {the latent is now an \textbf{integer}};
    \end{tikzpicture}
  \end{center}
  \vskip 4pt
  \footnotesize
  Keep a learned table of $K$ vectors - the \textbf{codebook}. After the encoder produces a
  latent vector, replace it with the nearest entry in that table and pass the entry to the
  decoder. \textbf{Vector Quantized Variational Autoencoder} (\textbf{VQ-VAE}),
  van den Oord et al.\ (2017).
  \vskip 6pt
  \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.97\linewidth}{\footnotesize
    The latent is no longer a point in a continuous space. It is \textbf{the index of a
    table row} - a symbol, exactly like a word.}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The gradient problem, and the shameless fix}
  \small
  "Pick the nearest entry" is an $\argmin$. Its derivative is zero almost everywhere and
  undefined at the boundaries, so gradients cannot flow back to the encoder and the encoder
  never learns.
  \vskip 2pt
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.82\textwidth]{straight_through.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vskip -2pt
  \footnotesize
  \centering
  The forward pass goes through the quantizer. The backward pass walks around it.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why that is allowed to work}
  \small
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.52\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!12}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\small
      \textbf{The straight-through estimator.} On the way forward, use the codebook entry.
      On the way back, \textbf{pretend the quantizer was not there} and copy the decoder's
      gradient straight onto the encoder's output.}}
    \column{0.48\textwidth}
    \footnotesize
    It is not the true gradient of anything. It is a deliberate lie that points in
    approximately the right direction, and it works well enough that the whole field is
    built on it.
    \vskip 5pt
    Two extra loss terms then pull the codebook entries and the encoder outputs toward each
    other, so the lie stays small. Next slide.
  \end{columns}
  \vskip 8pt
  \footnotesize
  \centering
  A recurring pattern in deep learning: when a discrete step blocks the gradient, invent a
  differentiable fiction and check empirically that it trains.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Keeping the lie small: the three terms}
  \small
  The straight-through estimator is only harmless if the codebook entry the decoder receives
  is \textbf{close to} the vector the encoder produced. So the loss actively enforces that.
  With $z_e$ the encoder output, $e$ its nearest codebook entry, and $\mathrm{sg}[\cdot]$ a
  \textbf{stop-gradient} (forward: identity; backward: zero):
  \vskip 2pt
  \[ \mathcal{L} = \underbrace{\|x - \text{dec}(e)\|^2}_{\text{1. reconstruction}}
     \;+\; \underbrace{\|\mathrm{sg}[z_e] - e\|^2}_{\text{2. codebook}}
     \;+\; \beta\underbrace{\|z_e - \mathrm{sg}[e]\|^2}_{\text{3. commitment}} \]
  \vskip 4pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \footnotesize
    Terms 2 and 3 are \textbf{the same distance}, written twice. The stop-gradient is what
    makes them different: term 2 freezes the encoder and moves the \textbf{codebook}; term 3
    freezes the codebook and moves the \textbf{encoder}.
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      They meet in the middle, and $\beta$ (about \textbf{0.25}) says who moves further. A
      large $\beta$ makes the encoder commit to whatever the codebook currently says - useful,
      because a codebook that is chased too hard never settles.}}
  \end{columns}
  \vskip 6pt
  \centering
  \footnotesize
  \textbf{Next: the failure mode this creates.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The failure mode this creates: codebook collapse}
  \small
  Nothing in that loss guarantees the codebook gets \emph{used}. An entry that is never the
  nearest to anything receives no gradient, so it never moves, so it stays useless forever.
  \vskip 2pt
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.78\textwidth]{codebook_collapse.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vskip -2pt
  \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.97\linewidth}{\footnotesize
    A 512-entry codebook can quietly do the work of 40, and the loss curve will not tell you -
    reconstruction simply plateaus a little worse than it should. \textbf{Always log how many
    codebook entries are actually used.} It is one line of code, and it separates a real bug
    from a mysterious quality ceiling.}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The codebook is a vocabulary}
  \small
  Once an image is a grid of integers, the analogy to text stops being an analogy.
  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{center}
    \footnotesize
    \begin{tabular}{lcc}
      \toprule
      \textbf{Chameleon} (Meta, 2024) & \textbf{Text} & \textbf{Image} \\
      \midrule
      Vocabulary & \multicolumn{2}{c}{\textbf{65,536 shared} - of which 8,192 are image codes} \\
      Tokens per item & varies & 1,024 per $512\times512$ image \\
      Produced by & byte-pair encoding & learned image tokenizer \\
      Predicted by & one softmax & \textbf{the same softmax} \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
  \end{center}
  \vskip 10pt
  \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.97\linewidth}{\small
    There is now \textbf{one sequence}, containing both kinds of token, predicted by
    \textbf{one model} with \textbf{one loss}. Nothing in the transformer needs to know which
    tokens were pixels. This is the cleanest answer to the opening contradiction - and it is
    clean precisely because it forces images to become discrete.}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{So what does forcing them to be discrete cost?}
  \small
  \footnotesize
  Measured on the ch10 dataset: 4,481 handwritten letters at $24\times24$, cut into
  $4\times4$ patches, with a $K$-entry codebook fitted by k-means.
  \vskip 1pt
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.83\textwidth]{vq_quantization.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vskip -1pt
  \scriptsize
  k-means stands in for a learned VQ-VAE encoder, which would do better - so read this as a
  \textbf{lower bound} on quality.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Two things to read off that figure}
  \small
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \textbf{1. Sharply diminishing returns.}
    \vskip 4pt
    \footnotesize
    $K{=}8 \rightarrow 32$ cuts the error by \textbf{41\%} (0.0182 $\rightarrow$ 0.0106).
    $K{=}128 \rightarrow 512$ buys only \textbf{22\%} for four times the vocabulary.
    \vskip 6pt
    Growing $K$ is not a solution, which is why the field went to cleverer quantization
    instead - MAGVIT-2 reaches a vocabulary of $2^{18}$ only by dropping the nearest-neighbour
    lookup altogether.
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \textbf{2. Even at $K{=}512$ the strokes break up.}
    \vskip 4pt
    \footnotesize
    Look at the letters, not the number. The reconstruction is speckled and the strokes come
    apart at patch boundaries.
    \vskip 6pt
    Each patch is quantized \textbf{independently}, with no knowledge of its neighbours. A
    stroke crossing a boundary has no mechanism holding it continuous.
  \end{columns}
  \vskip 8pt
  \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.97\linewidth}{\footnotesize
    \textbf{This is ch10's finding again.} These strokes are 1-2 pixels wide. There, halving
    the image twice erased them before the deep layers saw them. Here, quantizing $4\times4$
    blocks independently breaks them. Thin high-frequency structure is what every
    compression scheme destroys first - and text in an image is exactly that.}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Compression, stated as a number}
  \small
  With $K = 512$ and $4\times4$ patches, one $24\times24$ letter becomes:
  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{center}
    36 tokens $\times$ $\log_2 512$ bits $=$ \textbf{324 bits}, against
    $24 \times 24 \times 8 = $ \textbf{4{,}608 bits} raw.
    \vskip 4pt
    A \textbf{14.2$\times$} compression.
  \end{center}
  \vskip 10pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      That compression is the \emph{point}. A sequence model cannot afford one token per
      pixel - $512\times512\times3$ would be 786,432 tokens.}}
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      Chameleon's tokenizer gets $512\times512$ down to \textbf{1,024} tokens. That is the
      only reason putting an image in a language model's sequence is possible at all.}}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\section{Next-token prediction, on a picture}

\begin{frame}[plain]
  \vfill
  \begin{center}
    {\Large\color{popblue}\textbf{Next-token prediction, on a picture}}\\[0.9em]
    \begin{minipage}{0.82\textwidth}\centering\small
      There is no new algorithm in this section. That is the whole point of it.
    \end{minipage}
  \end{center}
  \vfill
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Flatten it and predict the next one}
  \small
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.88\textwidth]{raster_order.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vskip -2pt
  \footnotesize
  Read the token grid top to bottom, left to right. Now it is a sequence, and generating an
  image is \textbf{exactly} the training objective of GPT: predict token $t+1$ given tokens
  $1 \ldots t$. Prompt it with text tokens first and you have text-to-image, with no new
  machinery whatsoever.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why this was long thought a dead end}
  \small
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      \textbf{It is strictly sequential.} 1,024 tokens means \textbf{1,024 forward passes},
      and token 900 cannot start until 899 is done.}}
    \vskip 6pt
    \footnotesize
    Your ch10 sampler needed 50 passes with DDIM, and every pixel moved in parallel on each
    one. Autoregressive generation looked like the obviously worse deal.
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!12}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      \textbf{Raster order is also arbitrary.} Nothing about a picture says the top-left
      corner comes "first". A caption has a real order; an image does not.}}
    \vskip 6pt
    \footnotesize
    Diffusion has no such asymmetry, which is part of why it won the 2021-2024 image
    generation era outright.
  \end{columns}
  \vskip 8pt
  \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.97\linewidth}{\footnotesize
    \textbf{What changed:} the goal stopped being "best image generator" and became "one
    model that does everything". Once you want interleaved text and images, editing by
    conversation, and knowledge shared between the two, the token formulation stops being a
    handicap and starts being the only thing that composes.}}
\end{frame}

\section{One model for both}

\begin{frame}[plain]
  \vfill
  \begin{center}
    {\Large\color{popblue}\textbf{One model for both}}\\[0.9em]
    \begin{minipage}{0.82\textwidth}\centering\small
      Three architectures, three answers to the same contradiction.
    \end{minipage}
  \end{center}
  \vfill
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The three answers, side by side}
  \small
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.97\textwidth]{three_paradigms.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vskip -4pt
  \footnotesize
  \centering
  Read the second row of each column: it is the only place they genuinely disagree. Everything
  else follows from that one choice about how an image is represented.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Paradigm 1 - fully discrete}
  \small
  \textbf{Chameleon} (Meta, 2024). Quantize images, put both token types in one vocabulary,
  train one transformer with one cross-entropy loss. Early fusion, as in L33's Design C.
  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      \textbf{Maximum elegance.} Interleaved text and images in any order, one objective,
      and every trick that works for language models transfers unchanged.}}
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      \textbf{Pays at the tokenizer.} Everything the codebook throws away is gone before
      the transformer starts - the effect we just measured on the letters.}}
  \end{columns}
  \vskip 8pt
  \footnotesize
  Training mixed modalities was also unstable enough that Chameleon needed specific fixes
  (query-key normalisation among them) to converge at scale.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Paradigm 2 - hybrid}
  \small
  \textbf{Transfusion} (Meta, 2024). Do not quantize at all. Keep images as
  \textbf{continuous} patch latents, and run \textbf{both} objectives in one transformer.
  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{center}
    \footnotesize
    \begin{tabular}{lcc}
      \toprule
      & \textbf{Text tokens} & \textbf{Image patches} \\
      \midrule
      Representation & discrete & continuous \\
      Objective & next-token cross-entropy & diffusion denoising (ch10) \\
      Attention & causal & bidirectional within the image \\
      \midrule
      Transformer & \multicolumn{2}{c}{\textbf{shared, one set of weights}} \\
      Loss & \multicolumn{2}{c}{the two, summed} \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
  \end{center}
  \vskip 8pt
  \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.97\linewidth}{\footnotesize
    \textbf{This is your ch10 model living inside a language model.} The DDPM loss you
    derived in L28 is one of the two terms. No quantization means no codebook damage, at the
    cost of a genuinely more complicated system.}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Paradigm 3 - decoupled encoders}
  \small
  \textbf{Janus} (DeepSeek, 2024). Observe that understanding and generating want
  \textbf{different} representations, and stop forcing one to serve both.
  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \footnotesize
    \begin{itemize}\itemsep 3pt
      \item To \textbf{understand}: SigLIP features (L33) - semantic, abstract, lossy about
        exact pixels. Good, because you want the meaning.
      \item To \textbf{generate}: quantized tokens - concrete, reconstructable. Good,
        because you have to output actual pixels.
    \end{itemize}
    \vskip 5pt
    Only the language model trunk is shared.
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!12}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      \textbf{The insight worth keeping:} "unified" does not have to mean "one
      representation". Forcing a single encoder to be both semantic and reconstructable
      makes it worse at each.}}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{GPT-4o's native image generation}
  \small
  The most visible system of all, and the one we know least about. What follows is
  \textbf{reconstruction from published work}, not an official architecture.
  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \footnotesize
    \textbf{Reasonably established:}
    \begin{itemize}\itemsep 2pt
      \item Native - not a call out to a separate image model.
      \item Images as \textbf{continuous} latent patches, not discrete codes.
      \item Explicit begin-image and end-image markers in the token stream.
      \item A combined language-modelling and denoising loss.
    \end{itemize}
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \footnotesize
    \textbf{Inferred, and should be labelled so:}
    \begin{itemize}\itemsep 2pt
      \item Model sizes and layer counts.
      \item The exact noise schedule.
      \item Whether generation is row-by-row or whole-image.
      \item Data mixing ratios.
    \end{itemize}
  \end{columns}
  \vskip 8pt
  \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.97\linewidth}{\footnotesize
    \textbf{Be careful how you repeat this.} It is widely described online as
    "autoregressive image generation", which is at best half right - the reconstruction that
    fits the evidence is Paradigm 2, autoregressive over \emph{text} with
    \emph{diffusion} on the image latents.}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why writing text inside images suddenly worked}
  \small
  For years, generated images contained text-shaped marks that were not text. Then, quite
  abruptly, they contained sentences.
  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      A dedicated image model has a text encoder bolted on and no idea how letters are
      spelled. A unified model has \textbf{spelling knowledge in the same weights} that are
      drawing the picture.}}
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      Same reason editing by conversation works: "make the sign say OPEN" needs the model to
      hold the picture and the instruction in one context, not to re-generate from a new
      prompt.}}
  \end{columns}
  \vskip 8pt
  \footnotesize
  \centering
  This is ch10's closing point, answered. There, each letter of the word was generated
  \textbf{independently}, so the result had no single hand. A unified model does not carry
  that independence assumption - the whole image is one sequence, conditioned on everything
  before it, letters included.
\end{frame}

\section{What it costs}

\begin{frame}[plain]
  \vfill
  \begin{center}
    {\Large\color{popblue}\textbf{What it costs}}\\[0.9em]
    \begin{minipage}{0.82\textwidth}\centering\small
      The honest frame, as in every chapter.
    \end{minipage}
  \end{center}
  \vfill
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The bill}
  \small
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \footnotesize
    \textbf{Tokens.} 1,024 per image is cheap for one picture and ruinous for video: one
    second at 24 frames is roughly \textbf{25,000 tokens} before a single word of prompt.
    \vskip 6pt
    \textbf{Sequential decoding.} Generating 1,024 tokens one at a time is slow in a way
    that parallel hardware cannot rescue.
    \vskip 6pt
    \textbf{Training.} Early fusion means no reusing an existing language model. You pay for
    everything, from scratch.
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!12}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      \textbf{Still unsolved:}
      \vskip 3pt
      - the tokenizer is a lossy bottleneck nobody is happy with;
      \vskip 2pt
      - understanding and generation still interfere with each other during training;
      \vskip 2pt
      - evaluation is weak - there is no agreed measure of "did it follow the instruction";
      \vskip 2pt
      - fine detail and text remain the first things to break.}}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Recap}
  \small
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.52\textwidth}
    \begin{enumerate}\scriptsize\itemsep 2.5pt
      \item A language model emits discrete symbols; ch10's diffusion is continuous.
        Unifying them means resolving that.
      \item \textbf{VQ-VAE} makes an image discrete: an autoencoder whose latent is snapped
        to a codebook entry, trained through a deliberate gradient fiction.
      \item We measured what that costs on our own letters: 14.2$\times$ compression at
        $K{=}512$, sharply diminishing returns, and \textbf{visibly broken strokes} - thin
        structure goes first, as in ch10.
      \item Once discrete, generating an image is next-token prediction in raster order.
        No new algorithm.
      \item Three unified architectures: fully discrete (Chameleon), hybrid (Transfusion),
        decoupled encoders (Janus). GPT-4o most likely resembles the second.
    \end{enumerate}
    \column{0.48\textwidth}
    \vskip 10pt
    \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\small
      \textbf{Next:} you have now seen how a single transformer can read text, read images,
      and draw them.
      \vskip 4pt
      What is left is how such a model is actually \emph{trained to be useful} - instruction
      following, preferences, and reinforcement learning on top of a pretrained model.
      That is \texttt{ml/llm\_training/}, with ch11's reinforcement learning as the
      foundation.}}
    \vskip 5pt
    \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      The unifying idea of this whole chapter: \textbf{make everything a sequence of
      vectors, and one architecture will read all of it.}}}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}
