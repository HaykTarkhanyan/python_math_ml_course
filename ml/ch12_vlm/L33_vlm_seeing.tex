\begin{frame}{The most ordinary thing you do with a chat model}
  \small
  You paste a photo of a handwritten receipt and ask what the total is. It answers
  correctly. You paste a chart and ask which quarter dropped. It answers.
  \vskip 6pt
  Nothing in this course so far can do that.
  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\small
      A transformer (ch9) consumes a \textbf{sequence of vectors}. Text arrives as one
      naturally: split into tokens, look each up in an embedding table.}}
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\small
      An image is a \textbf{grid of pixels}. There is no sentence, no vocabulary, and no
      obvious order. Nothing to look up.}}
  \end{columns}
  \vskip 8pt
  This lecture is that bridge. The next one is the harder direction: making the same model
  \textbf{draw}.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Two questions that sound the same and are not}
  \small
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.48\textwidth}
    \textbf{1. How does a model \emph{see} an image?}
    \vskip 4pt
    \footnotesize
    Settled. One recipe, essentially everyone uses it, and the arguments left are about
    efficiency.
    \vskip 6pt
    \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      This lecture. You will be able to explain every open-weights vision-language model
      released in the last three years.}}
    \column{0.48\textwidth}
    \textbf{2. How does a model \emph{draw} an image?}
    \vskip 4pt
    \footnotesize
    Not settled. Three competing architectures, actively argued about, and the leading
    products do not agree with each other.
    \vskip 6pt
    \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!12}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      L34. The disagreement is the interesting part, and it comes from a genuine conflict
      with what you built in ch10.}}
  \end{columns}
  \vskip 10pt
  \centering
  A \textbf{vision-language model} (\textbf{VLM}) is any model that takes both images and
  text as input. The good ones do both questions at once.
\end{frame}

\section{An image is not a sequence}

\begin{frame}[plain]
  \vfill
  \begin{center}
    {\Large\color{popblue}\textbf{An image is not a sequence}}\\[0.9em]
    \begin{minipage}{0.82\textwidth}\centering\small
      So make it one. The trick is almost insultingly simple.
    \end{minipage}
  \end{center}
  \vfill
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Patchify}
  \small
  Cut the image into fixed squares. Flatten each square into a vector. Push each vector
  through \textbf{one linear layer}. Done - the image is now a sequence, and every tool from
  ch9 applies unchanged.
  \vskip 4pt
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.93\textwidth]{patchify_grid.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vskip -2pt
  \footnotesize
  Shown on a letter from ch10, at $24\times24$ with $4\times4$ patches. Each patch becomes a
  16-dimensional vector; the linear layer maps it to whatever width the transformer wants.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The arithmetic that governs everything downstream}
  \small
  A patch grid of side $s/p$ gives $(s/p)^2$ tokens. This number comes back as a
  \textbf{cost} in every design decision for the rest of the lecture.
  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{center}
    \footnotesize
    \begin{tabular}{lccc}
      \toprule
      \textbf{Resolution} & \textbf{Patch} & \textbf{Grid} & \textbf{Tokens} \\
      \midrule
      $224\times224$ & $16\times16$ & $14\times14$ & 196 \\
      $224\times224$ & $14\times14$ & $16\times16$ & 256 \\
      \rowcolor{armred!12}
      $336\times336$ & $14\times14$ & $24\times24$ & \textbf{576} \\
      $896\times896$ & $14\times14$ & $64\times64$ & 4096 \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
  \end{center}
  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.52\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      The highlighted row is \textbf{LLaVA-1.5} (Liu et al., 2023), the reference open
      vision-language model. Remember \textbf{576}.}}
    \column{0.48\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!12}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      Tokens grow with the \emph{square} of resolution, and attention costs the
      \emph{square} of tokens. Doubling resolution is roughly $16\times$ the attention work.}}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The same thing, on a real photograph}
  \small
  A $24\times24$ letter makes the idea clear but hides the scale. Here is what a real vision
  encoder actually does to a real image.
  \vskip 2pt
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.66\textwidth]{patchify_real.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vskip -2pt
  \footnotesize
  A market in Yerevan. Keep this picture in mind - by the end of the lecture it will have
  broken three different things.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Vision Transformer}
  \small
  Dosovitskiy et al.\ (2021) put patches into a standard transformer encoder and changed
  almost nothing else. The \textbf{Vision Transformer} (\textbf{ViT}) is the result, and it
  underlies essentially every vision encoder in use today.
  \vskip 2pt
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.86\textwidth]{vit_pipeline.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vskip -2pt
  \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.97\linewidth}{\footnotesize
    Step 4's \textbf{classification token} (\textbf{CLS}) is an extra learned vector whose
    output summarises the whole image. \textbf{The naming is honest:} nothing in this diagram
    is vision-specific - it is the ch9 architecture, fed differently.}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why step 3 is not optional}
  \small
  Self-attention is \textbf{permutation-equivariant}: shuffle the inputs and you get the same
  outputs, shuffled. It has no built-in idea of "next to".
  \vskip 2pt
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.80\textwidth]{position_embeddings.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vskip -2pt
  \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.97\linewidth}{\footnotesize
    A \textbf{convolution} could never make this mistake - locality is wired into the kernel.
    A transformer has to be \emph{told} where each patch was, by adding a learned vector per
    position. Take that away and an image is a bag of unordered squares.}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Predict first}
  \small
  A CNN (ch6) is built around a strong assumption: \textbf{nearby pixels belong together},
  hard-wired by the convolution kernel. A Vision Transformer has no such wiring - every
  patch can attend to every other patch from layer one.
  \vskip 10pt
  \begin{center}
    \textbf{So: does a Vision Transformer need convolution to learn that neighbouring
    patches are related?}
  \end{center}
  \vskip 10pt
  \pause
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\small
      \textbf{No - given enough data.} With large training sets it learns local structure by
      itself, and beats comparable CNNs.}}
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\small
      \textbf{But on small data it loses.} The convolution's assumption is genuinely
      correct, and a model given the right prior needs fewer examples to find it.}}
  \end{columns}
  \vskip 8pt
  \footnotesize
  \centering
  This is the bias-variance tradeoff from ch2, wearing an architecture costume: a helpful
  constraint is worth a lot when data is scarce and becomes a ceiling when it is not.
\end{frame}

\section{Two encoders, one space}

\begin{frame}[plain]
  \vfill
  \begin{center}
    {\Large\color{popblue}\textbf{Two encoders, one space}}\\[0.9em]
    \begin{minipage}{0.82\textwidth}\centering\small
      Patches are only useful if their meaning lines up with words.
    \end{minipage}
  \end{center}
  \vfill
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The problem with a plain Vision Transformer}
  \small
  Train a Vision Transformer to classify 1000 categories and its features are excellent
  \textbf{at those 1000 categories}. Ask about anything else and the representation was
  never asked to encode it.
  \vskip 8pt
  \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.97\linewidth}{\small
    We want an image representation shaped by \textbf{language}, so that "a photo of a dog
    wearing a hat" and the corresponding picture land in the same place. Then a language
    model can read the image features because they already speak its language.}}
  \vskip 8pt
  The supervision for that is lying around the internet in enormous quantity: images that
  came with captions.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What "one space" actually means}
  \small
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.78\textwidth]{shared_space.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vskip -4pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \footnotesize
    Two encoders, one destination. A picture of a cat and the string \emph{"a cat"} should end
    up pointing the same way, \textbf{even though no shared weight ever processed both}.
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      Once that holds, an image is \textbf{addressable by language} - which is exactly what a
      language model needs to read it. The rest of this section is how to get from the left
      panel to the right one.}}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{CLIP: two towers that have to meet}
  \small
  \textbf{Contrastive Language-Image Pre-training} (\textbf{CLIP}), Radford et al.\ (2021).
  Two completely separate encoders, trained together on \textbf{400 million} image-caption
  pairs scraped from the web.
  \vskip 4pt
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.90\textwidth]{clip_pipeline.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vskip -2pt
  \footnotesize
  The two towers share \textbf{no weights} and never see each other's inputs. The only thing
  forcing them to agree is the loss - which is why the last two boxes matter so much.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Two details in that diagram that are easy to skip}
  \small
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \textbf{The linear projection}
    \vskip 3pt
    \footnotesize
    A Vision Transformer might output 768 numbers; the text transformer 512. You cannot
    compare vectors of different lengths at all.
    \vskip 4pt
    So each tower ends with one learned matrix mapping into a \textbf{shared embedding
    space} - 512 dimensions for CLIP ViT-B/32. That space is the whole product. Everything
    else is a way of shaping it.
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \textbf{The L2 normalisation}
    \vskip 3pt
    \footnotesize
    Every embedding is divided by its own length, so it lands on the unit sphere. Now the dot
    product of two embeddings is exactly the \textbf{cosine of the angle} between them.
    \vskip 5pt
    \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      Meaning becomes \textbf{direction}, not magnitude. A picture of a cat and the words
      "a cat" should point the same way; how long the arrows are means nothing.}}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The objective, written out}
  \small
  Take a batch of $N$ pairs. Embed everything and build the $N \times N$ matrix of cosine
  similarities $s_{ij}$ between image $i$ and caption $j$.
  \vskip 4pt
  \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.97\linewidth}{\footnotesize
    Read \textbf{row $i$} as a multiple-choice question: \emph{"which of these $N$ captions
    belongs to image $i$?"} The right answer is always on the diagonal - so this is ordinary
    classification with $N$ classes, and takes ordinary cross-entropy:
    \[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{image}\to\text{text}}
       = -\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \log
         \frac{\exp(s_{ii}/\tau)}{\sum_{j=1}^{N} \exp(s_{ij}/\tau)} \]}}
  \vskip 4pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      Then again down the \textbf{columns} - "which image belongs to this caption?" - and
      average the two. The loss is \textbf{symmetric}; neither modality is the label.}}
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!12}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      $\tau$ is the \textbf{temperature}, and CLIP does not fix it. It is learned by gradient
      descent like any other weight.}}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Temperature: how confident should the model be?}
  \small
  Cosine similarities live in $[-1,1]$ and in practice bunch together. Feed those straight
  into a softmax and every answer looks equally good, so there is almost no gradient.
  Dividing by a small $\tau$ spreads them apart.
  \vskip 2pt
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.90\textwidth]{clip_temperature.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vskip -2pt
  \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.97\linewidth}{\footnotesize
    $\tau$ controls \textbf{how harshly a near-miss is punished}. CLIP does not tune it by
    hand: it is \textbf{initialised at 0.07 and learned}, so the model decides for itself how
    sharp its own softmax should be - one fewer hyperparameter, and a rare case of a training
    difficulty setting being handed to gradient descent.}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{By hand: one training step on a batch of 4}
  \vskip -2pt
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.92\textwidth]{clip_worked_step.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vskip -4pt
  \footnotesize
  Row I1's correct caption scored 0.31; the best wrong one scored 0.28. After dividing by
  $\tau$ that gap becomes 4.4 against 4.0, and the softmax puts \textbf{0.436} on the right
  answer, for a row loss of $-\ln 0.436 = 0.83$.
  \vskip 3pt
  \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.97\linewidth}{\footnotesize
    A perfect batch scores 0; random guessing on 4 classes scores $\ln 4 = 1.39$. At
    \textbf{0.63} this model beats chance and has plenty left to learn.}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why the free negatives matter so much}
  \small
  Supervised learning needs someone to write down the right answer. Contrastive learning
  only needs pairs that \textbf{belong together}, and it manufactures its own wrong answers
  from the rest of the batch.
  \vskip 4pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.44\textwidth}
    \begin{center}
      \includegraphics[width=0.99\linewidth]{clip_contrastive.pdf}
    \end{center}
    \column{0.56\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      At $N = 32{,}768$, one step supplies about \textbf{one billion} negative comparisons for
      the cost of embedding 32,768 items.}}
    \vskip 6pt
    \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!12}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      \textbf{Batch size is not a speed knob here.} A bigger batch means more wrong answers to
      rule out, so the question is genuinely harder and the features have to be sharper. Small
      batch, easy task, weaker model.}}
    \vskip 6pt
    {\footnotesize Illustrative values; the \textbf{structure} is exact.}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The catch, and the fix everyone now uses}
  \small
  That batch-wide softmax is expensive in a specific way: normalising a row needs every other
  device's embeddings, so the loss does not shard cleanly across machines.
  \vskip 8pt
  \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.97\linewidth}{\small
    \textbf{SigLIP} (Zhai et al., 2023), \textbf{Sigmoid Loss for Language-Image
    Pre-training}, replaces the softmax over a whole row with an \textbf{independent sigmoid
    on each pair}: each of the $N^2$ cells becomes its own yes/no question.}}
  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      No normalisation across the batch, so it shards trivially - and it works well at
      \emph{small} batch sizes, where CLIP's objective degrades.}}
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!12}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      It is now the default vision encoder in a large share of open vision-language models.}}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Zero-shot classification falls out for free}
  \small
  CLIP was never trained to classify anything. But it can: write each class as a
  \emph{sentence}, embed them all, embed the image, and take the highest similarity.
  \vskip 2pt
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.88\textwidth]{zero_shot.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vskip -4pt
  \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.97\linewidth}{\footnotesize
    The last step is the same dot product as in training - \textbf{classification and retrieval
    are the same operation here}, you have only chosen what to put in the columns. The class
    list is a \textbf{string}, decided at inference time.}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why "a photo of a" is not decoration}
  \small
  The prompt template looks like superstition. It is not, and the reason recurs everywhere in
  this course.
  \vskip 8pt
  \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!12}{\parbox{0.97\linewidth}{\small
    CLIP's text encoder never saw bare nouns. It saw \textbf{captions} - "a photo of a cat
    sitting on a windowsill". Feeding it the single word \emph{"cat"} asks it to embed
    something \textbf{off the distribution it was trained on}, and the embedding is
    correspondingly worse.}}
  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.48\textwidth}
    \footnotesize
    Wrapping the class in a caption-shaped sentence puts it back on distribution. Then go
    further: embed the \emph{same} class under \textbf{80 different templates} - "a bad photo
    of a...", "a sculpture of a..." - and average the resulting vectors.
    \column{0.52\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      Radford et al.\ report \textbf{3.5\%} on ImageNet from that ensembling, and almost
      \textbf{5\%} from prompt engineering and ensembling together. It is free at inference:
      average once, keep one vector per class.}}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What CLIP achieved, and where it stops}
  \small
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      \textbf{The headline result.} Zero-shot CLIP matched a \textbf{ResNet-50 trained
      directly on ImageNet}, without seeing one ImageNet training image.
      \vskip 3pt
      And it was far more \textbf{robust}: on distorted or unusual renderings of the same
      classes, the supervised model degraded sharply and CLIP largely held up. Learning from
      captions rather than a fixed label set gives a less brittle representation.}}
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      \textbf{Bounded by the captions it saw.} Concepts rare on the web - specialist medical
      imaging, minority scripts, fine-grained species - stay weak. "Zero-shot" means "no task
      training", not "knows everything".
      \vskip 3pt
      It is also poor at \textbf{counting}, at reading text in images, and at spatial
      relations. Captions rarely say "three cups to the left of the laptop", so nothing ever
      taught it to encode that.}}
  \end{columns}
  \vskip 6pt
  \centering
  \footnotesize
  Hold on to the right-hand box. Every one of those weaknesses returns at the end of this
  lecture as a \textbf{vision-language model} failure - this is where they are born.
\end{frame}

\section{Three ways to give an LLM eyes}

\begin{frame}[plain]
  \vfill
  \begin{center}
    {\Large\color{popblue}\textbf{Three ways to give an LLM eyes}}\\[0.9em]
    \begin{minipage}{0.82\textwidth}\centering\small
      We have image tokens that mean something. Now attach them to a language model.
    \end{minipage}
  \end{center}
  \vfill
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The three answers, side by side}
  \small
  Everyone agrees on the vision encoder. The disagreement is about \textbf{what sits between
  it and the language model} - and the real question underneath is how many tokens the
  language model is asked to swallow.
  \vskip 2pt
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.97\textwidth]{three_designs.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vskip -4pt
  \footnotesize
  \centering
  The next three frames take these one at a time. Watch the third row of each column.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Design A - a projector}
  \small
  The simplest thing that works, and it works remarkably well. \textbf{LLaVA}
  (Liu et al., 2023).
  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{center}
    \begin{tikzpicture}[node distance=0.55cm, every node/.style={font=\scriptsize}]
      \node[draw, rounded corners, fill=armblue!12, minimum height=0.75cm, text width=1.5cm,
            align=center] (v) {image\\encoder};
      \node[draw, rounded corners, fill=armorange!25, right=of v, minimum height=0.75cm,
            text width=1.5cm, align=center] (p) {2-layer\\MLP};
      \node[draw, rounded corners, fill=paramgreen!14, right=of p, minimum height=0.75cm,
            text width=2.5cm, align=center] (l) {LLM\\(unmodified)};
      \draw[-{Latex}, thick] (v) -- node[above]{576} (p);
      \draw[-{Latex}, thick] (p) -- node[above]{576} (l);
      \node[below=0.25cm of l, font=\scriptsize\itshape] {text tokens go in here too};
    \end{tikzpicture}
  \end{center}
  \vskip 4pt
  \footnotesize
  A \textbf{multi-layer perceptron} (\textbf{MLP}) maps each of the 576 visual vectors into
  the language model's embedding space. They are then simply \textbf{prepended to the text
  tokens}, exactly as if they were words.
  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      \textbf{The language model is not modified at all.} No new layers, no architecture
      change. That is why this took off - anyone could bolt vision onto any open LLM.}}
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      \textbf{Cost:} all 576 tokens sit in the context window for the whole conversation.}}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Design B - a resampler}
  \small
  If 576 tokens is too many, \textbf{learn to compress them} to a fixed budget.
  \textbf{Flamingo} (Alayrac et al., 2022) and \textbf{BLIP-2} (Li et al., 2023).
  \vskip 6pt
  \footnotesize
  A small set of \textbf{learned query vectors} cross-attends to the image features and
  outputs one vector each. The output count is \textbf{constant} no matter how many patches
  went in - the image is summarised, not passed through.
  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      Resolution and token cost are now \textbf{decoupled}. Feed a bigger image, pay the
      same downstream price. This is what makes video remotely affordable.}}
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!12}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      \textbf{A fixed budget is a fixed budget.} Whatever those queries fail to pick up is
      gone. Dense text in an image suffers badly.}}
  \end{columns}
  \vskip 8pt
  \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.97\linewidth}{\footnotesize
    \textbf{A detail worth stealing.} Flamingo injects vision through gated cross-attention
    whose gate is initialised to \textbf{zero}. At step one the vision path contributes
    exactly nothing, so the model \emph{is} the original language model, and vision fades in
    as the gate opens. Adding a modality can therefore never damage what already worked -
    the same idea as a residual branch starting at zero.}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Design C - early fusion}
  \small
  Remove the bridge entirely. \textbf{One transformer}, both modalities, from the first
  layer. \textbf{Chameleon} (Meta, 2024) is the clearest example, and L34 is mostly about
  this family.
  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \footnotesize
    There is no adapter to train, no frozen encoder, no projection. Images and text are just
    two kinds of token in one vocabulary, and one loss covers both.
    \vskip 6pt
    \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      This is the only design that can \textbf{generate} images as naturally as it reads
      them. A projector is a one-way street.}}
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      \textbf{Costs:} you cannot reuse a pretrained language model as-is, training is much
      more expensive, and mixed-modality training is unstable enough that Chameleon needed
      specific fixes to converge at all.}}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Which won}
  \small
  \begin{center}
    \footnotesize
    \begin{tabular}{lccc}
      \toprule
      & \textbf{A: projector} & \textbf{B: resampler} & \textbf{C: early fusion} \\
      \midrule
      Token cost & full (576) & fixed, small & full \\
      Pretrained LLM reusable & yes & yes & no \\
      Training cost & lowest & low & highest \\
      Can generate images & no & no & \textbf{yes} \\
      Detail preserved & high & lossy & high \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
  \end{center}
  \vskip 10pt
  \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.97\linewidth}{\small
    \textbf{It depends on whether the model must also draw.} For understanding alone,
    \textbf{A still dominates} - Qwen2-VL, InternVL and the LLaVA family all keep a separate
    encoder and a projector. \textbf{C is what you need the moment the model must
    \emph{generate} images}, which is why every unified model in L34 is early-fusion.
    B survives where the token budget binds, above all in video.}}
  \vskip 6pt
  \footnotesize
  \centering
  So if you are building something today with an open model and a modest budget, A is the
  right default - and that is not a compromise, it is the current state of the art for
  reading images.
\end{frame}

\section{What it costs, and what breaks}

\begin{frame}[plain]
  \vfill
  \begin{center}
    {\Large\color{popblue}\textbf{What it costs, and what breaks}}\\[0.9em]
    \begin{minipage}{0.82\textwidth}\centering\small
      Every failure in this section traces back to one number.
    \end{minipage}
  \end{center}
  \vfill
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{An image is expensive}
  \small
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.78\textwidth]{token_budget.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vskip -4pt
  \footnotesize
  A $336\times336$ thumbnail costs more context than a full page of writing. A phone photo
  at native resolution would cost more than this entire lecture as text.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What the resize actually destroys}
  \small
  Back to the market. Those handwritten price labels are perfectly legible in the photograph.
  Here they are before and after the resize every fixed-resolution encoder performs:
  \vskip 2pt
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.72\textwidth]{resolution_detail.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vskip -4pt
  \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.97\linewidth}{\footnotesize
    \textbf{No amount of cleverness downstream recovers this.} The model is not failing to
    read the labels - it never received them. When a vision-language model "cannot read" a
    receipt, this is usually why, and it happens before layer one.}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{So stop resizing everything to a square}
  \small
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.88\textwidth]{anyres_tiling.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vskip -2pt
  \footnotesize
  Cut the image into crops, encode each at full resolution, and add one downscaled copy of the
  whole thing so the model still knows how the pieces fit together. The token bill goes up in
  exact proportion - here $5\times$.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The general version of that fix}
  \small
  The fixed-resolution habit is inherited from image classification, and it is actively
  harmful for documents: a wide receipt squashed to $336\times336$ has unreadable text
  \textbf{before the model sees it}.
  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      \textbf{Naive Dynamic Resolution} (Qwen2-VL, Wang et al., 2024): keep the original
      aspect ratio, emit however many tokens the image actually needs. A small icon costs
      few tokens; a dense page costs many.}}
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      This single change is most of why recent models became usable for
      \textbf{screenshots, documents and charts}, which is now the dominant real-world use.}}
  \end{columns}
  \vskip 8pt
  \footnotesize
  The active research direction is the other side of the same coin: \textbf{token pruning
  and merging} - drop or combine patches that carry little information, since most of a
  photograph is background.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Three failures, in one photograph}
  \small
  The market picture from the start of the lecture is a near-perfect adversarial example, and
  you can now explain every part of it.
  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{center}
    \footnotesize
    \begin{tabular}{p{3.3cm}p{7.6cm}}
      \toprule
      \textbf{Ask it...} & \textbf{and it struggles, because} \\
      \midrule
      "How many bowls of spice are there?" & dozens of near-identical objects, and attention
        \emph{pools} rather than counts. \\
      "What does that handwritten label say?" & the resize destroyed the strokes two slides
        ago - the model never received them. \\
      \rowcolor{armorange!14}
      "What script is that?" & Armenian handwriting is thin on the caption web CLIP learned
        from, so the representation is weak where the data was. \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
  \end{center}
  \vskip 8pt
  \centering
  \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.92\linewidth}{\footnotesize\centering
    None of these is a mysterious model quirk. Each is a specific decision made earlier in
    this lecture, coming back with a bill.}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Where vision-language models still fail}
  \small
  These are not random weaknesses. Each has a cause you can now name.
  \vskip 6pt
  \begin{center}
    \footnotesize
    \begin{tabular}{p{3.5cm}p{7.4cm}}
      \toprule
      \textbf{Failure} & \textbf{Why} \\
      \midrule
      Counting objects & Attention pools information; nothing in the training objective ever
        rewarded keeping a tally. \\
      Precise spatial relations & Position embeddings give rough layout, not geometry. \\
      Reading clocks and gauges & Needs exact angles from a coarse patch grid. \\
      Dense or small text & Below the patch resolution the characters are simply not there. \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
  \end{center}
  \vskip 8pt
  \fcolorbox{armorange}{armorange!12}{\parbox{0.97\linewidth}{\footnotesize
    Notice the pattern: most of these are the \textbf{token budget} again. The information
    was destroyed at the encoder, and no amount of language-model capability downstream can
    restore it. Same lesson as ch10, where halving a 1-2 pixel stroke twice erased it before
    the deep layers ever saw it.}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Hallucination, in the visual case}
  \small
  A vision-language model will confidently describe objects that are not in the picture -
  most often objects that \emph{usually} appear alongside what is there.
  \vskip 8pt
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.52\textwidth}
    \footnotesize
    Ask about a kitchen photo and you may be told about a refrigerator that is not in frame.
    Kitchens have refrigerators, and the language model knows that far more strongly than it
    trusts 576 blurry vectors.
    \column{0.48\textwidth}
    \fcolorbox{armred}{armred!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\footnotesize
      \textbf{The language prior overrides the image.} It is the \emph{language} model that
      was trained on trillions of tokens; the visual evidence is comparatively thin and easy
      to outvote.}}
  \end{columns}
  \vskip 10pt
  \fcolorbox{armblue}{armblue!8}{\parbox{0.97\linewidth}{\footnotesize
    \textbf{Practical consequence:} a fluent, specific description is not evidence that the
    model looked carefully. If the answer matters, ask about what is \emph{absent}, or ask
    twice with the object named differently.}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Recap}
  \small
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.52\textwidth}
    \begin{enumerate}\footnotesize\itemsep 3pt
      \item Cut an image into patches, project each one, and it is a sequence a transformer
        can read. That is the whole bridge.
      \item A Vision Transformer is the ch9 architecture, fed differently. Position
        embeddings are what stop it being a bag of patches.
      \item CLIP aligns images and text by contrast, using the batch itself as negatives.
        Zero-shot classification falls out for free.
      \item Attach it to a language model with a projector, a resampler, or no bridge at
        all. For reading images the projector still wins; the bridge only disappears when
        the model must also draw.
      \item One $336\times336$ image costs \textbf{576} tokens, and most failures trace back
        to that number.
    \end{enumerate}
    \column{0.48\textwidth}
    \vskip 12pt
    \fcolorbox{paramgreen}{paramgreen!8}{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\small
      \textbf{Next:} reading an image is settled. \textbf{Drawing} one from the same model
      is not.
      \vskip 4pt
      L34 starts from a real conflict: your ch10 diffusion model works in continuous space,
      and a language model emits discrete tokens. Three different architectures resolve that
      contradiction three different ways, and we will measure what the most popular one
      costs on our own letters.}}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}
