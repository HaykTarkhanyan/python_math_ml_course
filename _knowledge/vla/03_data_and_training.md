# 03 - Data: why this is the whole problem

If a student takes one thing from the chapter, make it this. In vision and language, the
architecture was the bottleneck and data was free. In robotics, the architecture is basically
solved (it is a VLM plus a small action head) and **data is the bottleneck**. Every strange
decision in this field - pooling incompatible robot datasets, training on human cooking videos,
generating a million synthetic demonstrations from 200 human ones - is a response to that.

## The gap, quantified

There is no internet of robot data. Some numbers, with the caveat that the good ones are
estimates from industry analysis rather than peer-reviewed measurement:

| Corpus | Scale |
|---|---|
| Text for LLM pretraining | ~300 trillion tokens |
| Internet video | ~1 billion hours |
| **All robot manipulation data in the world (estimate)** | **~300,000 hours** |
| Open X-Embodiment, the largest open pool | 1M+ trajectories, 22 embodiments |
| pi-0's full training set (proprietary + open) | ~10,000 hours |
| RT-1's dataset | 130k episodes, 13 robots, **17 months** |

The 300,000-hour figure and the text/video comparison come from industry analysis
([Bessemer](https://www.bvp.com/atlas/bessemer-predicts-robotics-and-physical-ai), and a widely
circulated [analysis of robotics scaling laws](https://gogoduck912.github.io/blog/robotics-scaling-law/)),
not from a paper - flag them as estimates in the lecture, but the order of magnitude is not
disputed by anyone. A related claim in circulation is that OXE contains roughly 1/200,000 of the
tokens in a large language corpus; that number appears in secondary sources and I could not
confirm it in a primary paper, so use the hours comparison instead.

Three orders of magnitude. And unlike text, the gap cannot be closed by scraping, because the
data does not exist to be scraped: **it has to be generated by a human moving a robot in real
time.** Roughly one human-hour per robot-hour. That is the structural fact.

## Where robot data actually comes from

### 1. Teleoperation - the expensive, real, gold-standard source

A human drives the robot; the robot records (images, proprioception, actions). Interfaces vary
wildly in cost, which is itself a research topic:

- **VR controllers** (Oculus Quest) - DROID's setup.
- **Leader-follower arms** - the operator moves a small passive replica; the real arm mirrors it.
  ALOHA is the reference design, ~$20k for a bimanual rig; **GELLO** does it for about $300 with
  a 3D-printed exoskeleton.
- **Handheld grippers with cameras** (UMI) and **motion-capture gloves** (DexCap, MANUS) - these
  collect *human* demonstrations with no robot present at all, then retarget to a robot.

The cost figures above are from the [datasets and data engines
survey](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.23001) (April 2026), which is the best single reference for
this whole landscape.

The major teleoperated datasets:

- **[Open X-Embodiment](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.08864)** (Oct 2023). Not collected - *pooled*.
  60 existing datasets from 34 labs at 21 institutions, 1M+ trajectories, 22 embodiments, 527
  skills. Standardized into the RLDS episode format. Its virtue is scale and diversity; its vice
  is that every constituent dataset has a different camera setup, action space, control rate and
  annotation quality, so "alignment complexity" is a real cost.
- **[DROID](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.12945)** (2024). The opposite philosophy: *identical
  hardware everywhere* (Franka Panda 7-DoF, two Zed 2 stereo cameras, a wrist Zed Mini, Oculus
  Quest teleop) at 13 institutions, so the data is consistent. 76k trajectories / 350 hours, 564
  scenes, 86 tasks, 50 collectors across three continents, 12 months. 1,417 distinct camera
  viewpoints. The tradeoff versus OXE is exactly diversity-of-embodiment against
  consistency-of-format, and both papers are right about their own axis.
- **[AgiBot World Colosseo](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.06669)** (March 2025). The scale outlier:
  **1,003,672 trajectories** (~43.8 TB) across 217 tasks, collected by 100 real robots in a
  4,000 square metre purpose-built facility covering domestic, retail, industrial, restaurant and
  office scenes. This is what it looks like when someone decides to brute-force the data problem
  with capital. Their policy GO-1 is reported at >60% success on complex long-horizon real tasks,
  32 points above the RDT baseline.
- **LeRobot community data.** 487 user-contributed datasets on the Hugging Face Hub, which is
  what [SmolVLA](https://huggingface.co/blog/smolvla) trained on. Small, noisy, and the only
  genuinely decentralized data source in the field.

### 2. Human video - the largest available signal, with a mismatch

Humans on video do manipulation all day. Ego4D alone is 3,000+ hours of egocentric
hand-object interaction. The problem is the **embodiment gap**: a human hand is not a parallel
gripper, and there are no action labels - you can see the outcome, not the joint commands.

The 2026 answer is retargeting plus mid-training, and the strongest evidence is
**[EgoScale](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.16710)** (NVIDIA, February 2026). Pretrain a flow-based
VLA on **20,854 hours** of egocentric human video, using wrist motion and hand poses retargeted
to a dexterous hand as pseudo-actions; then a light mid-training stage on aligned human-robot
play data; then fine-tune. Reported **+54% average success** over no-pretraining on a 22-DoF
dexterous hand, with transfer to lower-DoF hands.

The headline claim is the one that matters strategically: EgoScale reports a **log-linear scaling
law** between human-action prediction loss and human data scale, and shows that this loss
*predicts downstream real-robot performance*. If that holds up, it means the field has found a
data source that scales like the internet does, and the 300,000-hour ceiling is not the real
ceiling. It is early and it is one paper; other groups (HumanScale, Being-H0, PhysBrain) are
pushing the same direction. Watch this.

LingBot-VLA 2.0 already puts 10,000 hours of egocentric human video into a production training
mix alongside 50,000 hours of robot data, so the idea has left the lab.

### 3. Simulation and data engines

Two different things that get conflated.

**Simulation** means running the policy in a physics engine (Isaac Sim, MuJoCo, SAPIEN). Nearly
free per sample, and it gives you resets, ground-truth state, and parallelism. NVIDIA's whole
strategy rests on it.

**Data engines** are systems that *manufacture* demonstrations rather than collecting them. From
the [survey](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.23001):

- **MimicGen**: 200 human seed demonstrations become **50,000** by transforming the recorded
  trajectory onto new object poses. Enormous multiplier, but the diversity is combinatorial, not
  semantic - you get the same skill in new positions, not new skills.
- **RoboCasa / RoboGen / RoboTwin**: LLM-generated task definitions in procedurally generated
  scenes.
- **ROSIE**: semantic inpainting on real images to add distractors and change backgrounds -
  reported 115% relative improvement.
- **SynGrasp-1B**: billion-scale procedural grasp synthesis.
- **Real-to-sim**: reconstruct a real scene (increasingly with Gaussian splatting) so the
  simulator matches your actual lab.

The catch is stated plainly in the survey: synthetic data "exhibits lower realism compared to
real-world datasets, particularly in rendering quality and contact dynamics modeling". Contact is
the hard part. Rendering is close to solved; friction, deformation and compliance are not.

## Sim-to-real: what actually transfers

This is a good lecture segment because there is now a real empirical answer rather than folklore.
[Jin et al., June 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.22876v2) ran **over 10,000 real-world trials**
to ablate sim-to-real techniques for VLAs. Findings:

- **Spatial randomization beats appearance randomization.** Randomizing camera pose and table
  height gave substantially larger real-world gains than randomizing backgrounds and lighting.
  This is a genuinely useful and slightly counter-intuitive result: people reach for visual
  domain randomization first because it is easy, and it is the less valuable axis.
- **Frame-wise randomization beats episode-wise** by 3-13 points.
- **Rendering fidelity helps but plateaus** past medium fidelity.
- **RL fine-tuning in sim is the big lever**: clean RL training raised average real-world success
  from **5.6% to 33.4%**, and combined with domain randomization to **42.8%**.

Two things to flag for students. First, 42.8% is the *good* number here - sim-to-real for
manipulation remains bad in absolute terms. Second, note that this study was possible only
because someone was willing to run 10,000 real trials, which almost nobody is; see `04`.

## Cross-embodiment transfer: it works, and sometimes it does not

The generalist-policy thesis needs cross-embodiment transfer to be net positive, and it usually
is. [Open X-Embodiment](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.08864) reported RT-1-X at roughly 50% higher
mean success than the per-robot baselines, and RT-2-X at about 3x on emergent skills. Gemini
Robotics 1.5's **Motion Transfer** is a whole mechanism built to make skills learned on ALOHA 2
show up on an Apollo humanoid ([DeepMind](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.03342)). X-VLA uses soft
prompt tokens per embodiment; XR-1 uses "Unified Vision-Motion Codes" for human-robot
co-pretraining; LingBot-VLA 2.0 defines a single 55-dimensional canonical action space covering
arms, hands, waist, head and base.

But **negative transfer is documented**, and the chapter should say so. From the 2026 literature
on cross-embodiment offline RL: embodiments with little similar data in the pool - the examples
given are the Unitree H1 and G1 bipeds - suffer "pronounced performance degradation" under
cross-embodiment training compared to training in isolation. The pattern reported is that
negative transfer emerges specifically when a dataset combines **large amounts of suboptimal data
with high embodiment diversity**. Zero-shot transfer between *similar* arms with *similar*
grippers is reported at 80-100%; between dissimilar bodies it can be worse than not pooling at
all.

So the honest framing: cross-embodiment transfer is a similarity-weighted average, not a free
lunch. Pooling helps embodiments that have close relatives in the pool, and can hurt the ones
that do not. This is a nice concrete instance of a general ML lesson the course can point at -
multi-task learning helps until task conflict dominates.

## Why the field is not just training bigger models

Two constraints, both worth stating explicitly.

**The model has to run on the robot at control rate.** That caps size at a few billion
parameters. RT-2 was 55B in 2023; almost everything since is 0.45B to 7B. This is the opposite of
the LLM trend and it is not going to reverse while latency matters.

**More capacity on the same data does not help.** With ~1M trajectories, a 7B model already
overfits comfortably. The [ICLR 2026 review](https://mbreuss.github.io/blog_post_iclr_26_vla.html)
identifies **data quality and curation for imitation learning** as one of the two most
under-researched topics in the field despite being the most important - nobody knows what makes a
demonstration good, and there were almost no submissions on it out of 164.

The datasets survey lands on the same conclusion and it is a good closing line for the lecture:
"future advances in VLA will depend less on model architecture and more on the co-design of
high-fidelity data engines and structured evaluation protocols"
([survey](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.23001)).

## Training recipes, briefly

The standard pipeline as of mid-2026:

1. **Start from a pretrained VLM.** PaliGemma (pi-0), Llama-2 + DINOv2/SigLIP (OpenVLA), Eagle-2
   (GR00T N1), Qwen3-VL (LingBot). Nobody trains the backbone from scratch.
2. **Co-train, do not just fine-tune.** Keep web VQA, captioning and detection data in the
   mixture alongside robot trajectories, or the model forgets its semantics. pi-0.5's ablation
   found web data was what mattered most for generalizing to novel objects
   ([pi-0.5](https://www.pi.website/blog/pi05)). "Knowledge insulation" in openpi and the
   two-stage instruction-tuning recipes at ICLR 2026 are all attacking the same catastrophic
   forgetting problem.
3. **Optionally add a reasoning stage.** Embodied chain-of-thought: relabel trajectories with
   subtask descriptions and motion-planning language so the model produces an intermediate
   reasoning trace. This is what pi-0.5's high-level stage and Gemini Robotics 1.5's "thinking"
   are.
4. **Fine-tune on the target robot.** LoRA is standard; OpenVLA showed 4-bit quantized LoRA works.
5. **Increasingly: RL on real hardware.** [pi\*0.6 / RECAP](https://www.pi.website/blog/pistar06)
   is the flagship. The motivation is exactly the compounding-error argument from `01`:
   demonstrations only ever show the expert's state distribution, so the policy never learns to
   recover from its own errors. RECAP adds real-time human corrections plus advantage-conditioned
   RL on autonomous trials.

Step 5 is the direction of travel. It is also the point where robotics rejoins the RL material
the course already teaches, and where the sample-efficiency problem becomes brutal: every episode
costs real seconds on real hardware and may require a human to reset the scene.
